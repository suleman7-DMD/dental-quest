Bone Homeostasis — The Balance That Governs Everything

Alveolar bone is living tissue in constant remodeling. When the balance tips toward resorption, disease begins. Understanding the THREE causes of that tipping determines your entire treatment approach. → Resorption Patterns → Treatment Pipeline

ALVEOLAR BONE HOMEOSTASIS
Definition: Bone Homeostasis
The dynamic equilibrium between osteoblast (bone-forming) and osteoclast (bone-resorbing) activity. In health, formation ≥ resorption → net bone mass is maintained. Bone turns over continuously throughout life — old bone is replaced by new bone without net loss.
Constant balance: Osteoblasts (build) vs Osteoclasts (destroy) Osteoblasts — derived from mesenchymal stem cells. Produce osteoid matrix (collagen type I + ground substance), which mineralizes into mature bone. Also produce alkaline phosphatase. When entombed in matrix, become osteocytes (mechanosensory cells that detect loading).

Osteoclasts — derived from monocyte/macrophage lineage (hematopoietic). Multinucleated giant cells. Create resorption lacunae (Howship's lacunae) via acid + proteolytic enzymes. Activated by RANKL signaling.
Think of bone like a bank account — deposits (osteoblasts) and withdrawals (osteoclasts). Health = balanced budget. Disease = spending more than you earn. The question is always: what caused the overspending?
RANKL / OPG Signaling Axis RANKL (Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor Kappa-B Ligand) — produced by osteoblasts/stromal cells. Binds to RANK receptor on osteoclast precursors → activates osteoclast differentiation and resorption activity.
OPG (Osteoprotegerin) — decoy receptor produced by osteoblasts. Binds RANKL before it can reach RANK → inhibits osteoclast activation.

In health: OPG and RANKL are balanced → controlled remodeling.
In disease: Inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, PGE2) upregulate RANKL production → RANKL overwhelms OPG → unopposed osteoclast activation → net bone resorption.
OPG is the body's built-in osteoclast brake. Inflammation releases the brake (downregulates OPG) while simultaneously hitting the accelerator (upregulates RANKL).
What tips the balance toward resorption?
RESORPTION > FORMATION = BONE BREAKDOWN EXAM Three distinct causes, each with different mechanisms and different treatment implications. All three can coexist in the same patient:
  1. Plaque-induced inflammation (primary etiology)
  2. Systemic/environmental risk modifiers (accelerators)
  3. Traumatic occlusal forces (co-destructive with active infection)
→ Plaque-Induced Pathway → Systemic Factors → Traumatic Occlusal Forces (Essay 4)
Critical Concept: Host Response Does the Damage
KEY The bacteria do NOT directly destroy bone. They trigger the host inflammatory response, and it is the host's own inflammatory mediators (IL-1β, TNF-α, PGE2, MMPs) that cause connective tissue destruction and bone resorption. Two patients with identical bacterial loads can have vastly different disease because their host responses differ.
Bacteria are the match. Your immune system is the gasoline. Some people have a little gasoline (mild response, minimal destruction). Some people are walking around with a full tank (aggressive response, rapid destruction). Same match, very different outcomes.

Bone Resorption Patterns — Thickness Determines Everything

The morphology of surrounding bone determines whether disease creates horizontal or vertical loss. This is the bridge between pathology and surgical treatment planning. → Defect Classification → Bone Architecture

BONE THICKNESS DETERMINES LESION MORPHOLOGY
Three Factors That Determine Resorption Pattern KEY From lecture slides: "The pattern of bone resorption depends on: (1) thickness and quality of bone, (2) pathway of inflammation, (3) vascular network from gingival inflammatory lesion into the underlying alveolar process." The morphology of the investing root and its relationship to the volume, mineralization density, and shape of surrounding bone are major factors in determining osseous lesion formation.
THIN BONE (buccal/lingual plates) Entire thin plate dissolves uniformly → HORIZONTAL LOSS. Even reduction like a wall crumbling down evenly. No enough bone volume for a defect to form WITHIN it — so inflammation just dissolves it flat.

Clinical consequence: Tissue follows bone and RECEDES → shallow PD despite real attachment loss. Patient can have severe bone loss buccally but only probe 3mm because tissue receded WITH the bone.

Typical location: Buccal plates (especially thin over prominent roots like canines, premolars).
A thin wall just CRUMBLES — there's nothing left standing to form a crater. Like a paper fence dissolving in rain vs. a thick stone wall getting a cave carved into it.
→ Suprabony Pocket
THICK BONE (interproximal septum) Enough volume for inflammation to carve a DEFECT while walls remain standing → VERTICAL / INFRABONY LOSS. Irregular craters and defects form. Papilla does NOT recede → DEEP POCKETING (tissue stays high while bone goes low).

Clinical consequence: Deep interproximal pockets with significant probing depths. "Most pockets are found interproximally, where craters and all variations of resorptive lesions occur" (lecture slides).

Typical location: Interproximal septum between teeth (thicker cancellous bone with marrow spaces).
A thick cliff develops a CAVE — enough material for inflammation to carve inward while leaving outer walls standing.
→ Infrabony Pocket → Wall Defect Classification
Root Proximity and Marrow Space — The Second Factor
Roots diverge (space between)
Cancellous bone with marrow, blood supply, inflammatory pathway access → infrabony defects. Marrow provides route for inflammation to penetrate deep.
Roots close together
Thin dense cortical bone, no marrow → horizontal loss or complete dissolution. Inflammation can't penetrate easily through dense cortical plate.
Vascular Network — The Third Factor
Interproximal (near septal vessels)
Rich vascular network → complex defects (craters, infrabony). Blood supply enables inflammatory cell delivery deep into bone.
Mid-buccal over thin cortical bone
Sparse vascular network → simple horizontal loss. Limited pathway for inflammatory cell infiltration.
Clinical Pearl: Disease Begins Interproximally
The interdental area (septal vessels) is where initial osseous lesions form. This is why most pockets are interproximal — thick bone with rich blood supply gets carved into craters and complex defects rather than dissolving uniformly. The buccal bone (thin, sparse vasculature) shows horizontal loss.

Plaque-Induced Pathway — From Biofilm to Bone Loss

Plaque is the PRIMARY etiology of periodontal attachment loss. The pathway from biofilm accumulation through gingivitis and potentially to periodontitis is the central concept of the entire course. → Bone Homeostasis

CASCADE OF PERIODONTAL DESTRUCTION
The Chain Reaction — Step by Step
Step 1
Plaque bacteria accumulate on tooth surfaces (biofilm)
Step 2
Bacteria interact with host cells (PMNs, macrophages)
Step 3
Host releases inflammatory mediators (IL-1β, TNF-α, PGE2, MMPs)
Step 4
CT destruction + bone resorption (RANKL upregulation)
Step 5
Pocket formation (epithelium migrates apically)
Step 6
Clinical attachment loss (CAL)
Step 7
Tooth mobility (as attachment apparatus lost)
Step 8
Tooth loss (if untreated)
GINGIVITIS
Inflammation confined to gingiva KEY >10% BOP, PD ≤3mm, NO clinical attachment loss. Bone levels normal on radiograph (1-2mm apical to CEJ). Junctional epithelium intact at original position.

100% REVERSIBLE with plaque removal.

Treatment: OHI, D1110 prophy. No re-evaluation needed for gingivitis alone (assess at next routine recall).

Exception: Atypical gingivitis — profuse bleeding with minimal local factors → suspect systemic cause (leukemia, hormonal, drug-induced gingival overgrowth) → physician referral.
PERIODONTITIS
Inflammation extends to bone/PDL KEY Interdental CAL ≥2 non-adjacent teeth, OR buccal/lingual CAL >3mm + pocketing >3mm at ≥2 teeth. IRREVERSIBLE bone loss.

Rule out 5 NON-PERIO causes of CAL first: (1) trauma, (2) caries extending to CEJ, (3) endodontic lesion, (4) root fracture, (5) biologic width violation.

Stage severity (I-IV) + Grade progression rate (A-C).

Treatment: Phase I: SRP (D4341/D4342) → re-eval → surgery if non-responsive. → Treatment Pipeline → SRP
Critical: Gingivitis ≠ Periodontitis
Gingivitis and periodontitis are related but progression from one to the other is NOT guaranteed. Most gingivitis does NOT progress to periodontitis. They are different diseases with different implications. Löe proved plaque → gingivitis. He did NOT prove plaque → periodontitis. The plaque-periodontitis link is more complex: requires susceptible host response, risk factors, and Socransky's burst theory explains the episodic nature.
KEY LITERATURE
LIT Löe et al. 1965 — Experimental Gingivitis in Man
Design: 12 participants with healthy gingiva stopped all oral hygiene.
Part 1: 10-20 days without OH → 100% developed clinical gingivitis. Proved: plaque accumulation causes gingivitis (causal, not just associative).
Part 2: Oral hygiene resumed → 4-10 days for gingivitis to resolve completely. Proved: gingivitis is 100% reversible when etiologic factor removed.
What this study PROVED: Plaque → gingivitis (causal). Gingivitis is reversible.
What this study DID NOT prove: That gingivitis leads to periodontitis. That plaque causes bone loss. Anything about attachment loss.
EXAM Fleisher tests this distinction. Many students wrongly state Löe proved plaque causes perio. He proved plaque causes gingivitis only.
LIT Socransky 1984 — Burst Theory of Periodontal Destruction
Periodontal destruction is EPISODIC — asynchronous bursts of active destruction followed by periods of complete quiescence (remission). A site can be quiet for years, then suddenly lose attachment, then go quiet again. Only 12% of sites lost >2mm over 6 years.

Critical implication: When you probe and find attachment loss, you're measuring a historical record of past destruction. You have NO idea if the site is actively breaking down RIGHT NOW. There are currently no reliable tests to determine if a site is in the active process of losing attachment.

Nuance for exam: Periodontitis is classified as "chronic" — answer YES on exam. But Socransky would argue this doesn't fit perfectly because disease goes into full remission at sites, sometimes permanently. Know this nuance if Fleisher asks about Socransky's perspective.

Systemic Risk Factors — Modifiers, Not Causes

These factors MODIFY the host response but do NOT cause periodontal disease independently. They accelerate breakdown that plaque initiated. → Bone Homeostasis

RISK MODIFIERS — NOT INDEPENDENT CAUSES
Critical Distinction: Cause vs Risk Factor
Cause: Bacteria / microbial biofilm = the precipitating etiologic agent (for exam purposes).
Risk Factors: Smoking, diabetes, genetics — increase the LIKELIHOOD of disease. They predispose you. But they are NOT causal. Plenty of smokers never get periodontitis. Plenty of diabetics don't either.
EXAM "What CAUSES periodontitis?" → Bacteria / microbial biofilm. Smoking MODIFIES host response, it doesn't CAUSE the disease.
SMOKING
Single greatest modifiable risk factor KEY Mechanisms:
• Vasoconstriction → less bleeding on probing (masks disease severity)
• Impaired neutrophil function → reduced host defense
• Delayed wound healing post-surgery
• Fibrotic tissue → smoker can have severe bone loss but tissue appears clinically "healthy" because tissue is thick, fibrotic, and doesn't bleed
• Poorer response to both surgical and non-surgical therapy
• Impaired regeneration (reduced angiogenesis)
A smoker's mouth is like a crime scene with all the evidence hidden. Severe disease behind a fibrotic, non-bleeding curtain. This is why you can't rely on BOP alone in smokers — absence of bleeding does NOT mean health.
Dose-Response Relationship (Tomar & Asma 2000) EXAM Risk of periodontitis increases with cigarettes/day:
• ≤9 cigarettes/day = 3× greater risk
• ≥31 cigarettes/day = 6× greater risk

Former smoker risk reduction:
• 0-2 years quit = 3× greater risk (still high)
• ≥11 years quit = same risk as non-smoker
LIT Tomar & Asma, J Perio May 2000: 53% of US periodontitis is attributable to smoking (42% current smokers + 11% former smokers). The single major preventable risk factor for periodontitis in the US. Current smokers = 4× greater risk. Former smokers = 2× greater risk.
DIABETES
Bidirectional relationship with periodontitis KEY HbA1c ≥7.0% = Grade C modifier in 2017 classification.

Mechanisms:
• Elevated glucose → Advanced Glycation End-products (AGEs) accumulate in tissues
• AGEs bind RAGE receptors on macrophages → hyperinflammatory state
• Upregulated IL-1β, TNF-α → exaggerated bone resorption
• Impaired wound healing (microangiopathy, reduced collagen synthesis)
• Impaired neutrophil function (chemotaxis, phagocytosis)

Bidirectional: Uncontrolled diabetes worsens perio, AND active periodontitis worsens glycemic control. Treating perio can improve HbA1c.
Diabetes turns the immune thermostat up too high. Every bacterial insult gets an oversized inflammatory response, creating more collateral tissue damage.
IMMUNOSUPPRESSION HIV/AIDS, organ transplant immunosuppressive medications, chemotherapy — all reduce host defense capacity. Neutropenia (low neutrophil count) particularly devastating for periodontal tissues since neutrophils are the first-line defense in the sulcus.

Drug-induced gingival overgrowth — cyclosporine (transplant), phenytoin (seizures), calcium channel blockers (hypertension) → pseudopockets from tissue hyperplasia, NOT true attachment loss.
GENETICS Hyper-responsive macrophage phenotype (elevated PGE2, IL-1β production). Can't modify — just accounts for why some patients break down faster with similar bacterial loads.

IL-1 polymorphism — patients with this genetic variant produce 2-4× more IL-1β in response to bacterial challenge → accelerated bone loss → Grade C modifier.

Family history of early tooth loss from perio is a red flag for genetic susceptibility.
You can't change your genes. But knowing a patient has a genetic predisposition means more aggressive monitoring (SPT q3mo is even more critical).

Master Defect Classification — Suprabony vs Infrabony

WHERE and HOW bone was destroyed determines what you can do about it. This is the fork in the road between resection and regeneration. → Osseous Surgery → GTR/GBR

BONE DESTRUCTION CREATES DEFECTS
SUPRABONY DEFECT = HORIZONTAL BONE LOSS KEY Bone crest is at the same level across multiple teeth. Base of pocket sits ABOVE (coronal to) bone crest. Bone destroyed evenly — no walls to classify.

Treatment options:
• SRP (if mild-moderate)
• Gingivectomy (if adequate KT + no bone work needed)
• Simple flap (open flap debridement)
• No regeneration possible — nothing to contain a graft → Suprabony Pocket → Osseous Resection
INFRABONY DEFECT = VERTICAL BONE LOSS KEY Pocket extends BELOW the bone crest INTO a defect carved by disease. Classified by number of remaining bony walls. The walls determine containment → containment determines regenerative potential.

MUST raise a flap to access. Cannot treat blind. Need direct visualization to assess defect morphology and clean thoroughly.

Treatment depends on wall count: more walls = more contained = better regeneration prognosis. → Infrabony Pocket → 3-Wall → 2-Wall → 1-Wall
What is a "Wall"?
The original tooth socket has bony walls surrounding the root: buccal, lingual, mesial, distal. The root surface itself acts as an additional wall. When periodontitis destroys bone, it eats away some walls but may leave others standing. The NUMBER of remaining bony walls tells you how CONTAINED the defect is — and containment is EVERYTHING for regeneration.

3-Wall Defect — Most Contained, Best Regeneration

Three bony walls remain intact with the root surface acting as the 4th wall. This creates a trough/trench shape that is ideal for graft containment. → GTR

3-WALL (TROUGH / TRENCH) DEFECT
Anatomy: 3 bony walls + root = self-contained defect KEY Mesial, distal, and lingual (or buccal) bony walls remain intact. The root surface acts as the 4th wall. Creates a narrow trough or trench shape.

MOST CONTAINED defect type — graft material naturally stays in place without additional mechanical retention.
Imagine a narrow trench dug between three standing walls — pour concrete in and it stays put. That's why graft material is most predictable here.
Why 3-Wall Has Best Prognosis • Blood supply from 3 directions → best healing environment
• Walls provide structural containment for graft material
• Membrane stabilization is easiest (walls prevent collapse)
• Multiple surfaces for cell migration into defect
• Most predictable regeneration with GTR
Treatment: REGENERATION ESSAY 3 GTR membrane + bone graft + biologic (GEM 21 if available). This is where regeneration is most predictable. Membrane sits in the defect stabilized by the 3 walls. Blood clot fills the contained space. Progenitor cells have optimal environment for new bone, new cementum, and new PDL formation. → GTR Full Details

2-Wall Defect — Interdental Crater (Most Common)

The most common periodontal defect. Both interproximal bony walls lost, buccal + lingual walls intact. Creates a crater between two teeth. → Defect Classification

2-WALL = INTERDENTAL CRATER
Anatomy: Buccal + lingual walls intact, interproximal walls lost KEY MOST COMMON periodontal defect. Disease ate through the interdental septum between teeth but left the cheek-side and tongue-side bone standing. Creates a crater or trough between two adjacent teeth.

Patient literally CANNOT clean this — floss stops at level of buccal/lingual plates and never reaches crater depth. Toothbrush can't reach. Proxy brush can't reach. Even hygienist barely reaches during recalls. Over time → progressively deeper and worse.
Disease ate through the septum between teeth but left the cheek-side and tongue-side bone standing. Like a bridge with the middle collapsed but the sides still up.
Regeneration: MODERATE prognosis Less contained than 3-wall → graft can escape laterally through missing walls. Possible with biologics (GEM 21, EMD/Emdogain) and membrane + graft combination. Requires more careful containment strategy than a 3-wall defect. → GTR + Biologics
Resection option: Shallow craters For shallow 2-wall craters, resection may be more predictable: flatten bone to depth of crater using osteoplasty (non-supporting bone) and minimal ostectomy (supporting bone) to establish positive architecture. Then APF.

Result: Minimal probing depths everywhere, patient CAN clean it. Trade-off: big open embrasure space interproximally → food traps → patient needs proxy brush.

Resect vs regenerate decision: cost-risk-benefit analysis. With 2 walls → regeneration possible. Consider patient factors (cost, compliance, post-op burden of membrane management). At the time many of Fleisher's cases were done, GTR barely existed as an option. → Osseous Surgery
CONNECTIONS: → Defect Classification | → Osseous Surgery (Resection) | → GTR (Regeneration) | → Combination Defect

1-Wall (Hemiseptal) Defect — Least Contained, Resect Don't Regenerate

Only 1 bony wall remains. Minimal containment, minimal blood supply, worst regenerative prognosis. Best treated with resection. → Defect Classification

1-WALL (HEMISEPTAL) DEFECT
Anatomy: Only 1 bony wall remains KEY LEAST CONTAINED infrabony defect. Minimal blood supply from only 1 direction. Graft material won't stay — nothing to hold it in place on 3 sides.

Hemiseptal — bone loss on one side of the interdental septum but not the other. One tooth's bone level is lower than the adjacent tooth's level.
Like trying to build a sandcastle with only one wall — sand just falls out the other three sides. You can't contain graft material with only one wall of support.
Treatment: RESECTION, not regeneration EXAM Worst prognosis for regeneration → best treated with resective osseous surgery. Remove bone to establish even, positive architecture. Osteoplasty + ostectomy to eliminate the defect and create a contour that tissue can follow predictably.

Fleisher's clinical guideline: with 1 wall → he probably wouldn't attempt regeneration. The containment isn't there for predictable results. → Ostectomy/Osteoplasty
CONNECTIONS: → Defect Classification | → Osseous Surgery (Resection) | → Combination Defect (1-wall top)

Combination Defect — Character Changes, Dual Approach

Most common real clinical defect. Wall count changes from base to top. Requires both resection AND regeneration in the same surgery. → Defect Classification

COMBINATION DEFECT — MOST COMMON IN REAL PRACTICE
Anatomy: Character CHANGES from base to top KEY Most common defect in real clinical practice. The defect is not uniform — it transitions from more contained at the base to less contained coronally:

• At the BASE (deepest): 3-wall (most contained) — trough shape
• MIDDLE: transitions to 2-wall — crater widens
• At the TOP (most coronal): 1-wall (least contained) — defect opens up

"Since most periodontal defects offer a morphologic combination in appearance: more contained at the base, less so at the coronal part" (Fleisher lecture slides)
Treatment = DUAL APPROACH (same surgery) ESSAY 3 EXAM
RESECT the TOP
Uncontained coronal portion (1-wall) — resect with osteoplasty/ostectomy when resection won't jeopardize adjacent teeth. Remove the portion that can't be predictably regenerated.
REGENERATE the BOTTOM
Contained apical portion (3-wall base) — predictable regeneration with GTR membrane + bone graft + biologic. The containment at the base makes this portion ideal.
BOTH subtraction and addition in the same defect. This is the most common real-world scenario and the reason you need to understand both osseous resection and regeneration principles. → Osseous Surgery → GTR
LIT Fleisher lecture slides: "Since most periodontal defects offer a morphologic combination in appearance: more contained at the base, less so at the coronal part" → resect top + regenerate bottom. This is what Fleisher's clinical cases demonstrate — resecting uncontained portions and regenerating contained portions in the same procedure.

Bone Architecture — Positive vs Reverse vs Flattened

Tissue follows bone. ALWAYS. Understanding architecture types is essential for surgical planning. Positive architecture is the goal; reverse architecture is pathologic and must be corrected surgically. → Osseous Surgery

TISSUE FOLLOWS BONE — THE CARDINAL RULE
The Entire Reason Osseous Surgery Exists
"Soft tissue does not conform to abrupt architectural changes in bone produced by disease or surgery." Gingival tissue generally follows the underlying bone contour. Fix the bone → fix the tissue contour → fix the pocket. You CANNOT determine bone architecture by looking at tissue alone. Must PROBE or RAISE A FLAP to assess.
POSITIVE Architecture = HEALTHY = THE GOAL KEY Interproximal bone is MORE CORONAL than radicular (buccal/lingual) bone. This is the natural scallop: bone is higher between teeth than on the cheek/tongue sides.

Creates the normal gingival scallop with physiologic sulcus depths (≤3mm).

This is what osseous surgery aims to restore. The 4 goals of resective surgery all lead here: create contours that tissue will parallel, permit effective plaque control, allow wound closure.
REVERSE Architecture = PATHOLOGIC EXAM Interproximal bone is LOWER than radicular bone. Disease ate away the interproximal bone (craters), flipping the natural contour.

Creates pocketing — tissue at radicular bone level is more coronal than the crater floor → deep interproximal pocket that patient can't clean.

Must correct surgically — either resect radicular bone down to crater level (establish positive architecture) or regenerate bone up to fill the crater. → Osseous Recontouring
FLATTENED Architecture = Intermediate Bone at same level everywhere — no scallop. This can be an intermediate step during osseous surgery: flatten craters first, then recontour to establish positive architecture.

Sometimes acceptable endpoint if creating positive architecture would sacrifice too much supporting bone.
BONE SOUNDING — Mapping the 3D Defect Before Surgery EXAM Horizontal plane probing: From facial and lingual, locates the crest of proximal bony walls (how HIGH the walls go).
Vertical plane probing: Locates the base of the crater (how DEEP the defect goes).

This is how you MAP the 3D bone architecture before raising a flap. Combined with radiographs (which only show interproximal bone), bone sounding gives the clinical picture of buccal/lingual architecture that X-rays can't show.
ROOT TRUNK — Critical for Multi-Rooted Teeth KEY The depth of a periodontal defect in mm is NOT as significant for multi-rooted teeth as the relationship of the defect to the root trunk. Root trunk dimension is often NARROWEST for the first molar.

• Shallow defect (1-3mm) within root trunk dimensions = manageable
• Deep defect (>3mm) extending apical to root trunk → furcation involvement → dramatically worse prognosis

A 3mm defect above the furcation is TOTALLY different from a 3mm defect reaching into the furcation. The furcation changes everything.

Pocket Types — Pseudo, Suprabony, Infrabony

How to determine what kind of pocket you're dealing with, and why it matters for treatment. This is foundational — Fleisher: "How can you tell if there is a pocket or a pseudo-pocket?" → Defect Classification

INCREASED PD ON PROBING — What Kind of Pocket?
FALSE POCKET (Pseudopocket)
Excess soft tissue creates artificially deep reading Probe does NOT extend past CEJ. NO attachment loss. Bone level is NORMAL on radiograph (1-2mm apical to CEJ). Gingiva is just swollen, hyperplastic, or overgrown.

Causes: Inflammatory swelling, drug-induced gingival overgrowth (cyclosporine, phenytoin, nifedipine), altered/delayed passive eruption (Coslet classification).
How to CONFIRM pseudopocket: EXAM Need BOTH: (1) gingival margin is coronal to CEJ + (2) radiograph shows normal bone level. Probing depth alone is NOT enough — you MUST assess gingival margin position relative to CEJ.

Treatment: OHI may resolve if inflammation-caused. Gingivectomy if tissue is fibrotic/overgrown (Coslet Type 1A). Altered passive eruption may need osseous surgery (Coslet Type 1B).
EXAM Fleisher: "Lots of people got the question wrong about how to confirm pseudopocket." Need BOTH probing + radiograph. Neither alone is sufficient. Probing alone lies (pseudopockets inflate PD). Radiographs alone lie (only show interproximal bone well).
SUPRABONY POCKET
TRUE pocket — CAL present Probe extends past CEJ (attachment loss confirmed). BUT pocket bottom sits ABOVE (coronal to) bone crest. Soft tissue problem is worse than bone problem. No infrabony component.

Associated with HORIZONTAL bone loss — even bone destruction across the board. → Suprabony Defect
Treatment options: • Gingivectomy (if adequate KT + no bone work needed)
• SRP
• Simple flap (open flap debridement)
• Regeneration NOT indicated (no contained defect)

EXAM Gingival curettage and gingivectomy = SUPRABONY defects ONLY. "You do not gain access to the bone defect with either procedure." If infrabony component exists, you MUST raise a flap.
INFRABONY POCKET
TRUE pocket — extends BELOW bone crest Probe extends past CEJ AND into a bony defect. Bone has been eaten away in irregular pattern creating a crater or defect below the crest.
Classified by walls remaining 1-wall, 2-wall, 3-wall, or combination defect. Number of walls = regenerative potential. → Defect Classification
MUST RAISE A FLAP to access and treat. Cannot debride infrabony defect blind. Need direct visualization to assess defect morphology, clean root surfaces, and decide between resection vs regeneration.

Treatment depends on defect morphology:
• Contained (3-wall) → regenerate with GTR
• Uncontained (1-wall) → resect with osseous surgery
• Combination → resect top + regenerate bottom → Osseous → GTR
CLINICAL: How Do You Actually CONFIRM Pocket Type?
EXAM ESSAY 3
Step 1: Probe the site → get PD reading.
Step 2: Locate CEJ → determine gingival margin position relative to CEJ.
 • Margin APICAL to CEJ = recession = ADD recession to PD for total CAL.
 • Margin CORONAL to CEJ = tissue covering CEJ = SUBTRACT that distance from PD.
Step 3: Take radiograph → assess bone levels relative to CEJ.
 • Bone normal (1-2mm apical to CEJ) + deep PD = pseudopocket.
 • Bone loss visible + deep PD = true pocket (supra or infrabony).
Step 4: Bone loss even across teeth = suprabony. Bone loss irregular/cratered = infrabony.

Remember: Probing ALONE can lie (pseudopockets inflate PD; recession hides severity). Radiographs ALONE can lie (only show interproximal bone well, can't see buccal/lingual). TOGETHER they give you the real picture.
EXAM Fleisher's charting email: "They must identify the location of the gingival margin relative to the CEJ. This is the ONLY way."
Definition: Clinical Attachment Level (CAL)
CAL = distance from CEJ to the depth of the clinical pocket. CAL = recession + probing depth (if recession present). CAL = probing depth - distance from gingival margin to CEJ (if margin is coronal to CEJ).

CAL vs histologic attachment: Does the tip of the probe always end up at the base of the histologic sulcus? No. In inflamed tissue, the probe penetrates past the junctional epithelium into connective tissue. In healthy tissue, the probe stops at the JE. This means probing overestimates pocket depth in inflamed tissue.
Definition: Attached Gingiva vs Keratinized Tissue
Keratinized tissue (masticatory mucosa) = from free gingival margin to mucogingival junction (MGJ).
Attached gingiva = keratinized tissue MINUS the probing depth. The tissue firmly bound to underlying bone/cementum that doesn't move.

Clinical attachment level ≠ attached gingiva. They are separate measurements with separate clinical significance.

SRP — Scaling & Root Planing: The Foundation

Non-surgical periodontal therapy is the definitive treatment for mild-moderate periodontitis and the mandatory first step before any surgery. Understand its power AND its ceiling. → Treatment Pipeline

SCALING vs ROOT PLANING — They're Different Procedures
Definition: Scaling
Removal of soft and hard deposits (plaque, calculus) adhering to the tooth surface — both supra and subgingival. Mechanical disruption of biofilm. You're cleaning the OUTSIDE of the tooth.
Definition: Root Planing
Instrumentation of the ROOT SURFACE specifically — removing diseased/contaminated cementum and creating a smooth, hard, clean surface that is biologically compatible with reattachment. You're preparing the root for healing.
Scaling = cleaning the house. Root planing = sanding and refinishing the floors so new carpet can stick.
Scientific Rationale for SRP KEY Clinical benefits are derived from the removal of subgingival plaque and calculus and the disruption of the subgingival microbial flora, thereby delaying repopulation of pathogenic microbes. Goals:
• Disrupt and reduce subgingival microflora
• Remove calculus and endotoxin-contaminated cementum
• Create a biologically acceptable root surface
• Reduce inflammation → pocket reduction through tissue shrinkage (NOT bone regrowth)
• Allow tissue to reattach or form a long junctional epithelium
Measurable Endpoints of SRP Success EXAM • Loss or gain in clinical attachment levels
• Probing depths (reduced = success, but operator/force variability exists)
• Gingival inflammation / BOP (reduction expected; but BOP is NOT reliable predictor of future breakdown — Lang 1986)
• Changes in subgingival microflora (reduced motile rods/spirochetes, temporarily)

BOP Caveat: Presence of BOP is NOT a reliable indicator of future breakdown (Lang et al., J Clin Perio 1986). But absence of BOP is a somewhat reliable indicator of health. Positive predictive value is low; negative predictive value is high.
THE CATCH — SRP HAS A CEILING
Caffesse 65% Failure Rate at Deep Pockets EXAM ESSAY 3
In initial pocket depths ≥5mm, clinicians have been shown to inadequately debride roots 65% of the time.

Deeper the pocket → worse the access → more residual calculus left behind. This is WHY surgery exists — not because we want to cut, but because we physically cannot clean deep enough blind. "Closed" periodontal procedures have inherent limitations in effectively treating established periodontitis, as evidenced by findings upon flap reflection.
You wouldn't try to clean the back of a 10-foot closet through a 2-inch crack in the door. That's what SRP at 7-8mm pockets is like.
LIT Caffesse RG, Sweeney PL, Smith BA. J Clin Perio 1986: In initial pocket depths 5mm or greater, clinicians have been shown to inadequately debride roots 65% of the time.
IMPORTANT NUANCE — Shallow Pockets Can LOSE Attachment After SRP EXAM
In shallow pockets (≤3mm), instrumentation trauma can cause MORE harm than the disease. Mechanical trauma from aggressive scaling at shallow sites → tissue damage → net CAL loss. Deep pockets show gains. Shallow pockets can show losses. This is why you don't SRP a 2mm pocket.

Clinical attachment gains after SRP may not be "real":
• Decreased inflammation → decreased probing depths (Listgarten et al., J Perio 1976)
• Recession (Ramfjord & Kiester, J Perio 1954) — gums shrink, pocket looks shallower
• Reduced probing force penetration (Van Der Velden et al., J Clin Perio 1980) — healthier tissue resists probe more

True regeneration of connective tissue fibers into previously altered cementum? Not reliably demonstrated.
LIT Lindhe: Sites with initial PD <4.2mm did better with scaling alone than with flap surgery. Over-treatment is real.
SRP Remains Definitive Despite Limitations
LIT Greenstein G, J Perio 1992: Given the limitations of accomplishing complete calculus removal and the difficulty in identifying a reliable measuring index, scaling and root planing remains a definitive therapeutic approach to the management of patients with mild to moderate periodontitis and also as an initial phase of treatment for patients expected to require surgical intervention.
INSTRUMENTATION DETAILS
Effective Instrumentation Principles • Adaptation: Place leading third of cutting edge in contact with tooth surface
• Angulation: Face-to-tooth angle between 60°-85° (Gracey curette)
• Motion activation: Short, deliberate strokes (not vigorous sweeping)
• Handle roll: Rotate handle to keep blade adapted to root contour

Sharpen your curettes — before you start AND during the procedure. Nobody in sterilization sharpens instruments. You get dull curettes from the last person. Sharp = efficient = better results = less time.

Hand instruments supplemented with ultrasonics (not the other way around). At least hand instruments, with ultrasonics supplemental as indicated. Do NOT instrument past the attachment — instrument TO the base of the pocket, not past it (iatrogenic damage).
Local Anesthesia for SRP Step 1: Topical anesthetic application
Step 2: Initial injection in mucosal tissue using mepivicaine 3% (no epi)
Step 3: Subsequent injections in keratinized tissue using lidocaine 2% with 1:100,000 epi (watch for blanching)
Step 4: Overlap injection points through interproximal areas

Alternative: Oraqix (topical subgingival anesthetic) — for patients averse to injections, or for moderate pockets where lighter anesthesia suffices.

SPT — Supportive Periodontal Therapy (D4910 q3mo)

SPT is the single most important thing for your patient's long-term prognosis. Without it, even perfect surgery fails. This is not a "cleaning" — it's systematic re-assessment + instrumentation + reinforcement. → Treatment Pipeline

SPT = LIFELONG q3mo RECALL FOR PERIO HISTORY PATIENTS
What SPT IS — Not a "Cleaning" KEY It's re-assessment (new probing, BOP, radiographs as needed) + instrumentation at sites showing re-inflammation + reinforcement of OHI. Starts when Phase I (or Phase II) is complete.

This is the single most important thing for your patient's long-term prognosis.
SPT Protocol — The 4 Steps at Every Visit EXAM
Step 1
Examination, re-evaluation, diagnosis. Re-diagnosing at every recall. New probing (EVERY site, not just sites that were deep last time), check for changes, radiographs as needed.
Step 2
Motivation, re-instruction, instrumentation. Remotivate on OHI (patients WILL slack off). Clean supra + sub. Sharpen curettes before and during.
Step 3
Treatment of reinfected sites. Furcations, hard-access areas may show acute activity (suppuration). Thorough instrumentation under anesthesia, possible LAA, possible surgical intervention. Not every recall is just a cleaning.
Step 4
Polish entire dentition, apply fluoride, determine future SPT interval.
Mnemonic: "Every Monkey Tries Picking Fleas" — Examination, Motivation, Treatment, Polishing + Fluoride.
WHY q3mo SPECIFICALLY? — THE EVIDENCE
6-Month Recall: NO Scientific Evidence EXAM There is NO scientific evidence for 6-month recall in healthy patients. Origin: an 1890 women's magazine recommended seeing your dentist twice a year. Colgate picked it up in the early 1900s as a marketing slogan. That's it. No science. It's convention.

Fleisher isn't saying don't do it — it's logical, a year is too long, and it's easy to remember. But if an exam question asks "what is the evidence basis for 6-month recall in healthy patients?" the answer is there isn't one.
3-Month Recall: Overwhelming Evidence Four major long-term studies (U Michigan, U Gothenburg, U Minnesota, Loma Linda) all showed the same thing: patients receiving routine maintenance care maintained attachment levels better than those who didn't, even when home care was mediocre.

Mnemonic: "Six is a MYTH, Three is a MUST." No evidence for 6 months (healthy). Overwhelming evidence for 3 months (perio history). Axelsson & Lindhe 1981 is the study to cite.
KEY LITERATURE FOR SPT
LIT Axelsson & Lindhe 1981 — The Landmark Study
J Clin Perio: "Effect of Controlled Oral Hygiene Procedures on Caries and Periodontal Disease in Adults"
Design: 77 patients post-perio treatment. SPT group (n=52): q2mo for 2 years → q3mo for 4 more years. Non-recall group (n=25): returned to GP, sporadic visits.
Results at 6 years:
• SPT group: 99% of tooth surfaces had no change or max 1mm attachment loss
• Non-recall group: only 45% of tooth surfaces had no change or up to 1mm attachment loss

Fleisher's clarification: The point is NOT "send everyone to the periodontist." A closely monitored, effective recall program is what matters. It can be done in a GP office. Where it's done doesn't matter. THAT it's done properly is everything.
"If a patient isn't willing to commit to 3-month recalls after active therapy, they shouldn't bother doing the active therapy in the first place."
LIT Nyman, Lindhe & Rosling 1977: Without recall, surgery fails regardless of technique — pocket depths rebound to pre-surgical levels in 2 years. The surgery was not the problem; the lack of maintenance was.
LIT Rosling, Nyman & Lindhe 1976: With recall q2 weeks, patients maintained surgical reductions AND gained attachment. Demonstrated that frequent professional maintenance can optimize healing beyond what surgery alone achieves.
LIT Harper & Robinson 1987, J Clin Perio: SRP reduces levels of motile microbes and spirochetes, but for a limited period of time only. Recolonization occurs within approximately 3 months. This is the biologic basis for q3mo recall — you're disrupting bacterial repopulation before pathogens re-establish.
SPT CLINICAL PEARLS
Fleisher's Trap Questions — Students Get These Wrong EXAM
"Do you probe only sites that probed >3mm last time?"
NO. Probe EVERY SINGLE SITE, EVERY SINGLE TIME. Socransky: disease is episodic/asynchronous. A site that was 2mm last visit could be 6mm now. Record depths >3mm, but PROBE everything.
"Do you only clean supragingivally?"
NO. Clean BOTH supra and sub. Subgingival is MORE important — bacteria at the most apical extent of the pocket in anaerobic environment causes the most damage.
"Do you sharpen your curettes?"
YES. Before AND during procedure. Nobody in sterilization sharpens. You get dull curettes from the last person.
"Do you instrument past the attachment?"
NO. Instrument TO the base of the pocket. Going past = traumatizing healthy CT fibers = iatrogenic damage.

LOCALLY ADMINISTERED AGENTS (LAAs)
When to Use LAAs — NOT First-Line KEY Used at NON-RESPONSIVE sites after SRP re-eval, not as first-line treatment. You SRP first, re-evaluate at 4-6 weeks, THEN deploy these at persistent ≥5mm + BOP sites. They are an intermediate step before considering surgical intervention.

MOUTHRINSES — CHX vs LISTERINE
CHLORHEXIDINE (Peridex, PerioGard) EXAM Gold standard antimicrobial rinse. ADA-accepted. Cationic molecule — binds to negatively charged oral surfaces (substantivity = long-lasting effect).

Indications: Post-surgical (GTR membrane management), acute NUG/NUP, patients unable to perform mechanical plaque control temporarily

Side effects: Brown staining of teeth/tongue/restorations, altered taste, increased supragingival calculus formation

CRITICAL Drug Interaction: CHX is cationic, toothpastes contain anionic detergents (sodium lauryl sulfate). Even a 30-minute interval between toothpaste and CHX results in significantly reduced antiplaque effect. Recommendation: rinse at least 30 min after brushing, or use non-SLS toothpaste.
LISTERINE (Essential Oils) ADA-accepted. Contains thymol, eucalyptol, menthol, methyl salicylate.

Advantages over CHX: Available OTC, less staining, no prescription needed
Disadvantages: Less potent than CHX, alcohol content (dry mouth, burning sensation — alcohol-free versions available)

Both are ADA-accepted chemical inhibitors of plaque and calculus. CHX is stronger but has more side effects. Listerine is a reasonable long-term maintenance option.
PERIOSTAT — Sub-Antimicrobial Dose Doxycycline (SDD)
Periostat = 20mg Doxycycline 2x/day EXAM This is NOT an antibiotic dose. At 20mg, doxycycline acts as a host-modulating agent, not an antimicrobial. It inhibits matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that break down connective tissue.

Mechanism: Blocks collagenase activity → reduces CT destruction → slows attachment loss
Results: In severe disease sites (≥7mm), attachment gains were 33-52% greater with adjunctive SDD than with placebo
Who is at risk / who benefits: Patients with aggressive disease, rapid progressors (Grade C), smokers with poor response to SRP

Key distinction: Periostat is a HOST MODULATOR, not an antibiotic. It doesn't kill bacteria — it modifies the host's destructive response.
SYSTEMIC ANTIBIOTICS — When Indicated
Aggressive / Stage III-IV Grade C Periodontitis KEY Systemic antibiotics reserved for aggressive forms where local measures aren't enough because bacterial load or host response is out of control.

Common choices:
Amoxicillin + Metronidazole
Tandem or serial use. Broad coverage against perio pathogens. Most common combination for aggressive perio.
Azithromycin
Effective against anaerobes + gram-negative bacilli. Concentrates in periodontal tissues at levels significantly higher than normal gingiva. Effective adjunctive therapy for aggressive periodontitis.

Incision Decision Tree

The direction of the blade tells you the PURPOSE of the cut. Two orientations, two bevel directions, three internal bevel subtypes. → KT Decision → Flaps

INCISIONS — Two fundamental orientations
HORIZONTAL (Mesiodistal) Runs along the arch. This is where you choose BEVEL DIRECTION — and bevel direction determines the entire procedure.
Horizontal = running with the arch like a train on tracks. The bevel is which way the blade is angled as it cuts.
VERTICAL (Releasing) Incisions Perpendicular to arch. Extends from gingival margin into vestibule PAST the MGJ. Purpose = FLAP MOBILITY. Allows flap to be reflected and repositioned. Base ALWAYS wider than coronal — trapezoid shape preserves blood supply from the base.
Vertical releases are the "hinges" that let your flap door swing open. Narrow base = choke blood supply = flap necrosis.
HORIZONTAL INCISION → Two types based on BEVEL DIRECTION
EXTERNAL BEVEL
Direction: APICAL → CORONAL Blade starts OUTSIDE the gingiva on the apical/outer surface, angles inward toward base of pocket. Target = just below base of pocket.
Going coronal = going outward = EXTERNAL. The blade faces outward/externally.
Procedure = GINGIVECTOMY Goal = POCKET ELIMINATION. You're excising the ENTIRE pocket wall — cutting it off. The diseased tissue is REMOVED, not reflected. → External Bevel Detail
Healing = SECONDARY INTENTION Raw open wound left behind. No flap to cover it. Tissue heals by granulation from the wound base up.
Requirements (ALL must be met): 1. Sufficient KT to sacrifice (you're removing tissue permanently). 2. NO bone work needed (you don't get access to bone with this). 3. SUPRABONY defects ONLY (no infrabony component). If ANY requirement fails → WRONG procedure. → KT Decision: Plenty KT + No bone
INTERNAL BEVEL
Direction: CORONAL → APICAL Blade starts at or near gingival margin, angles inward toward crest of bone. Target = crest of bone.
Going apical = going inward = INTERNAL. The blade faces inward/internally toward bone.
Procedure = ALL FLAP SURGERIES Goal = POCKET REDUCTION. Tissue is reflected as a flap, not excised. Pocket elimination achieved by flap REPOSITIONING (APF). Internal bevel is the incision for osseous surgery, GTR, crown lengthening — everything. → Internal Bevel Detail → Flap Classification → Osseous Surgery
Healing = PRIMARY INTENTION Wound is COVERED/CLOSED by the flap. Tissue is sutured back.
3 subtypes — determined by starting position relative to gingival margin:
SUBMARGINAL 1-2mm FROM margin. Plenty KT — can sacrifice collar. → Detail
CREVICULAR IN the sulcus. Limited KT — preserve every mm. → Detail
PARAMARGINAL PALATE ONLY. Multiple parallel incisions to thin palatal tissue. Palate = all KT, always safe to excise.
What determines which internal bevel subtype?
Factor 1: KT width. Plenty → submarginal (can sacrifice). Limited → crevicular (preserve). Palate → paramarginal (all KT, always safe).
Factor 2: Interdental probing depth. Disease begins interproximally. Maximum PD should be in interdental regions. Deeper PD = need more tissue reduction with the internal bevel incision → influences how far from margin you start.
EXAM Both gingival curettage and gingivectomy are utilized with suprabony defects only. You do NOT gain access to bone defects with either procedure. WHY? Because flap procedures were designed to gain access to bone defects. This is a fundamental principle of perio surgery.

External Bevel Gingivectomy

The simplest surgical procedure. Excise the pocket wall from the outside. ESSAY 1 Coslet 1A is the ONLY crown lengthening scenario where gingivectomy alone works.

EXTERNAL BEVEL — COMPLETE DECISION
Blade Direction: APICAL → CORONAL (outside → in) The blade is placed on the OUTER/APICAL surface of the gingiva and angled inward toward the base of the pocket. The blade cuts through the pocket wall from the outside, excising it entirely.
Picture holding the scalpel on the outside of the gum and angling upward into the pocket. You're scooping the tissue OFF from below. "External" = blade faces externally.
The Procedure: Gingivectomy Goal: Complete pocket ELIMINATION by tissue EXCISION. The entire pocket wall is removed — nothing is reflected, nothing is repositioned. The tissue is gone. Wound type: SECONDARY INTENTION — raw, open wound. No flap to close over it. Heals by granulation from the wound base up. This means longer healing, more patient discomfort, and raw surface exposed to oral environment. Tissue sacrificed: Yes — the excised gingiva is permanently removed. This is why adequate KT is a strict requirement.
THREE STRICT REQUIREMENTS — ALL must be met
1. Sufficient KT to sacrifice You are permanently removing keratinized tissue. If you don't have enough to spare, you'll leave the patient with inadequate KT → mucogingival defect.
LIT Lang & Loe 1972: Minimum 2mm KT (1mm attached + 1mm free) needed for gingival health.
2. NO bone work needed External gingivectomy provides ZERO access to bone. You cannot see bone, modify bone, or place membranes. If you need ANY bone work → must raise a flap → internal bevel.
You cut off the roof but never opened the door to the basement. Bone stays hidden underneath.
3. SUPRABONY defects ONLY The pocket base must be ABOVE (coronal to) the alveolar crest. If there's an infrabony component, the defect extends below the bone and you can't reach it without a flap. If ANY of these 3 requirements fails → WRONG procedure. Switch to internal bevel flap.
Clinical Applications: 1. Drug-induced gingival overgrowth (phenytoin, cyclosporine, nifedipine) — pseudopocket from excess tissue, bone is normal. 2. Altered passive eruption — Coslet Type 1A — lots of KT + bone at normal position (not at CEJ). Just excise the excess tissue covering the clinical crown. → Coslet 1A 3. Suprabony pocket from tissue hyperplasia — chronic inflammation caused tissue thickening without bone involvement. 4. Epulis/fibroma excision — benign tissue masses with adequate surrounding KT. EXAM If Coslet 1B (bone AT CEJ) → gingivectomy FAILS because you'd violate biologic width. Must do flap + osseous.
Instrumentation: Kirkland knife — kidney-shaped blade for initial incision marking pocket depths (bleeding points). Orban knife — interproximal tissue removal. Marking: Use pocket marker (Goldman-Fox or similar) to puncture bleeding points at pocket depth. Connect dots with external bevel incision at 45-degree angle. Excise tissue coronal to the incision. Post-op: Pack with periodontal dressing (Coe-Pak) to protect raw wound. Remove 7-10 days.

Internal Bevel — The Universal Flap Incision

Coronal → apical. Used for ALL flap surgeries. Three subtypes based on starting position. ESSAY 1+3

INTERNAL BEVEL — Direction: CORONAL → APICAL
The blade starts at or near the gingival margin and angles inward/apically toward the crest of bone. Target = crest of bone. The blade dissects THROUGH the connective tissue, creating a flap of tissue that can be reflected off bone. The tissue is NOT excised — it is elevated as a flap and repositioned. Healing = PRIMARY INTENTION — wound is covered/closed by the flap and sutured. Heals under protection. Superior healing compared to secondary intention (external bevel).
Going apical = going inward = INTERNAL. You're making a "door" in the tissue that you'll open (reflect), do the work underneath, then close (suture) the door.
Used for ALL flap surgeries: Osseous resective surgery, OFD, Modified Widman, crown lengthening, GTR/GBR, APF, mucogingival surgeries. Internal bevel = the gateway to bone access. External bevel = no bone access. This is the fundamental distinction. → Osseous Surgery → APF
3 SUBTYPES — Starting position relative to gingival margin
SUBMARGINAL
Starts 1-2mm FROM the gingival margin NOT in the sulcus — on the outer gingival surface, 1-2mm apical to the margin. Creates a "collar" of tissue between the incision and the sulcus that is sacrificed. When: PLENTY of KT — you can afford to lose the collar. Why: The collar contains diseased sulcular epithelium, granulation tissue, and pocket lining. Removing it is therapeutic — provides a fresh wound edge for primary closure. → Submarginal Detail
CREVICULAR
Starts IN the sulcus (at gingival margin) Blade placed directly in the sulcus, hugging the tooth surface. Follows the sulcus down to the crest of bone. NO tissue is sacrificed — preserves every millimeter of KT. When: LIMITED KT — cannot afford to lose any tissue. Why: Preservation is paramount when KT is scarce. Even 1mm of sacrificed collar could create a mucogingival defect. → Crevicular Detail
PARAMARGINAL
PALATE ONLY — Multiple parallel incisions Used exclusively on the palatal surface. Multiple incisions parallel to the arch to thin thick palatal tissue. The palate is ALL keratinized tissue (no MGJ, no alveolar mucosa) → always safe to excise palatally. Why palate is special: No MGJ on palate = no elastic alveolar mucosa = APF is IMPOSSIBLE on palate. Can't move palatal tissue apically because it has nowhere to go. So you reduce bulk via paramarginal excision instead.
The palate is a brick floor — you can't slide it. Buccal tissue is a rug on hardwood — slides freely.

Submarginal Internal Bevel

Internal bevel subtype for cases with PLENTY of keratinized tissue. Sacrifices a collar of diseased tissue.

SUBMARGINAL — Starts 1-2mm FROM gingival margin
Starting Position: 1-2mm apical to the free gingival margin, on the OUTER surface of the gingiva. Not in the sulcus. Blade Path: From that starting point, the blade angles apically/internally toward the crest of bone. It creates a beveled cut through the connective tissue. What it creates: A "collar" of tissue between the incision line and the sulcus/tooth surface. This collar includes the sulcular epithelium, junctional epithelium, and inner pocket lining. It will be removed (curette away) after flap elevation.
WHY sacrifice a collar? The collar contains: - Diseased sulcular epithelium - Granulation tissue - Chronic inflammatory infiltrate - Pocket lining Removing it = therapeutic debridement + creates fresh wound edges for better primary closure. The wound margins are clean connective tissue, which heals better than inflamed tissue.
REQUIREMENT: Plenty of KT You are permanently removing 1-2mm of keratinized tissue width. Post-surgery, the KT band will be 1-2mm narrower. Adequate KT is defined as: Enough remaining after collar removal to maintain gingival health.
LIT Lang & Loe 1972: Minimum 2mm KT needed. Maynard & Wilson 1979: At least 1mm of attached gingiva required.
Clinical scenario: PLENTY KT + NEED BONE ACCESS This is the classic combination for submarginal incision: 1. Submarginal internal bevel incision (1-2mm from margin) 2. Raise full-thickness flap to bone → see/modify bone 3. Perform osseous surgery (ostectomy/osteoplasty) 4. APF to reposition flap apically 5. Vertical mattress sutures to secure flap in new position → KT Tree: Plenty KT + Need Bone → Full Thickness Flap
Interdental Probing Depth Factor The deeper the interdental probing depth, the more tissue you need to reduce with the internal bevel incision. Starting position may need to be slightly further from margin (closer to 2mm) for deeper pockets. The incision should thin the flap margin to a parabolic, knife-edge shape for intimate adaptation to bone post-APF.

→ Internal Bevel Overview → Crevicular (limited KT alternative) → KT Decision Tree → Full Thickness Flap → Osseous Surgery → APF

Crevicular (Sulcular) Internal Bevel

Internal bevel subtype for cases with LIMITED keratinized tissue. Preserves every millimeter.

CREVICULAR — Starts IN the sulcus
Starting Position: Blade placed directly IN the gingival sulcus, hugging the tooth surface. The blade rides along the root surface, following the sulcus down toward the crest of bone. What it preserves: EVERYTHING. No collar is sacrificed. No KT is lost. Every millimeter of keratinized tissue is preserved in the flap. The entire gingival margin tissue is reflected as part of the flap. What it does NOT remove: Sulcular epithelium and pocket lining stay attached to the inner surface of the flap — must be curetted away separately after reflection.
WHEN: Limited KT (<2-3mm) Every mm is precious. Cannot afford to sacrifice a collar. If you used submarginal here, you'd remove 1-2mm of already scarce KT → create a mucogingival defect → need a second surgery (FGG) to fix what you broke.
LIT Serino 1994: Sites with 3mm recession worsened 67% of the time. Sites with 4mm recession worsened 98%. Preserving KT prevents progression.
Advantage: Maximum preservation All KT is retained in the flap. After split-thickness elevation, this KT-containing flap can be repositioned apically (APF) or coronally (CAF). The full width of KT is carried to the new position. Key insight: When you APF a crevicular flap, you GAIN attached gingiva — the KT moves apically but the MGJ doesn't → net increase in attached gingiva width.
Clinical scenario: LIMITED KT + NEED BONE ACCESS 1. Crevicular internal bevel incision (in sulcus, preserve all KT) 2. Split-thickness reflection → periosteum stays on bone as "carpet" 3. Transition to full-thickness at bone crest (need to see bone for osseous work) 4. Split-thickness apically past MGJ (gain mobility) 5. → This is the COMBINED (Split-Full-Split) flap 6. APF repositions entire KT-bearing flap apically over periosteum 7. Vertical mattress sutures anchor flap to periosteum in new position → KT Tree: Limited KT + Need Bone → Combined Flap → Vertical Mattress Suture
Also used for: - Modified Widman Flap (MWF): Access only, flap returns to original position. Crevicular incision preserves tissue so you can put it back where it was. - OFD (Open Flap Debridement): Same as MWF — access, debride, replace. No osseous recontouring. - GTR/GBR procedures: Preserve maximum tissue for tension-free closure over membrane. → Flap Classification

The KT Decision Tree — Fleisher's 2 Questions

These two questions determine your incision, your flap type, and your procedure. Master this and the surgical decision almost makes itself. EXAM ESSAY 1+3

Q1: HOW MUCH KERATINIZED TISSUE DO I HAVE?
PLENTY (>3-4mm) Tissue to spare. Can sacrifice some and still have adequate KT for long-term health. Post-surgery must still have minimum 2mm KT remaining.
LIT Lang & Loe 1972: Minimum 2mm KT (1mm attached + 1mm free gingiva) needed for gingival health. This is the absolute floor.
LIT Maynard & Wilson 1979: At least 1mm of attached gingiva required (distinct from free gingiva). Attached = base of sulcus to MGJ.
LIMITED (<2-3mm) Every mm is precious. Cannot sacrifice any. Must preserve or augment.
LIT Serino 1994: 3mm recession worsened 67% of the time. 4mm recession worsened 98%. Thin/absent KT = progressive recession.

YES — Need bone access Osseous surgery (ostectomy/osteoplasty), crown lengthening (bone removal for BW), GTR/GBR (membrane placement), defect debridement, implant placement. → Osseous → Crown Lengthening
NO — Soft tissue only Pocket from excess tissue, pseudopocket, tissue overgrowth, drug-induced hyperplasia, altered passive eruption (Coslet 1A).

Flap Classification — What Are You Reflecting Off Bone?

Once you've made your incision and start reflecting — the TYPE of flap determines what you can DO. ESSAY 1+3

FLAP ELEVATED — WHAT TYPE?
3 categories based on WHAT LAYERS come off the bone
FULL THICKNESS (FTF) = Mucoperiosteal. EVERYTHING off bone. Bare bone exposed. → FTF Detail
SPLIT THICKNESS (PTF) = Mucosal/Partial. Periosteum STAYS. Bone covered by periosteum. → PTF Detail
COMBINED = Split-Full-Split. Character CHANGES by zone. Best of both worlds. → Combined Detail
KEY CONCEPT: WHY SPLIT THICKNESS ALLOWS REPOSITIONING
Full thickness = flap includes periosteum → nothing left on bone to slide over. Like pulling up the carpet AND the padding — bare concrete, nothing slides.
Split thickness = flap freed from bone BUT periosteum stays as a "CARPET." Flap slides over periosteum in any direction — like sliding furniture on a smooth floor. Release PAST the MGJ into alveolar mucosa (elastic tissue) for maximum mobility. This is why vertical mattress sutures grab periosteum — it's the anchor left behind on bone that holds the repositioned flap in its new position. → Vertical Mattress Suture
FLAP PROCEDURES FROM FLEISHER'S LECTURES Definitive Periodontal Therapeutic Techniques involving flaps: 1. Apically Position Flap (APF) — split thickness, repositioned apically. → APF 2. Contour Irregular Bone — osteoplasty for non-supporting bone. 3. Ostectomy for Bony Defects — supporting bone removal. 4. Regenerative Procedures — GTR/GBR with flap for membrane placement. 5. Debride Osseous Defects for Repair — access, clean, allow repair. Also: Modified Widman Flap (access only, no repositioning, no bone removal), OFD (access only, flap returns to original position). → Osseous Surgery

Full Thickness Flap (Mucoperiosteal)

Take EVERYTHING off bone. Direct access. The workhorse for resective and regenerative bone procedures.

FULL THICKNESS FLAP — What comes off bone
Layers reflected: Epithelium + Connective Tissue + PERIOSTEUM All three layers are elevated together as a single unit off the bone surface. Result = BARE BONE EXPOSED. Nothing remains on the bone surface. Elevation technique: BLUNT dissection using a periosteal elevator (Woodson, Prichard, Molt #9). The elevator slips under the periosteum and peels it off bone. Less technique-sensitive than sharp dissection.
You're peeling the entire covering off the bone like removing wallpaper, backing and all. One clean motion takes everything.
Advantages: 1. Direct bone access — can see and modify bone directly. Essential for ostectomy, osteoplasty, crater debridement. 2. Less technique sensitive — blunt elevation is simpler and faster than sharp dissection. 3. Faster — one elevation motion vs careful scalpel work. 4. Better visualization — bare bone lets you assess defect morphology precisely.
Disadvantages: 1. Hard to REPOSITION — no periosteum left on bone as a "bed" to slide over. Flap tends to want to return to original position. No smooth surface for flap to glide on. 2. Bone exposure risk — bare bone can undergo slight resorption from drying, trauma, or prolonged exposure. Periosteum normally protects bone. 3. Cannot anchor vertical mattress sutures — no periosteum to bite into with the deep pass of the suture (unless combined flap design used).
USE WHEN: Need to SEE or MODIFY bone - Osseous surgery (ostectomy/osteoplasty) — must see bone architecture to recontour. → Osseous - GTR membrane placement — must see defect walls to place membrane accurately. - Crown lengthening — must see bone crest to determine how much to remove for BW. - Crater/defect debridement — must see base and walls of defect. - Implant placement — must see ridge dimensions. - Any resective procedure — if you're cutting bone, you need to see it.
FULL THICKNESS FLAP — Clinical Details
Incision: Internal bevel (submarginal if plenty KT, crevicular if limited KT).
Vertical releases: Often needed for access. Base wider than coronal. Extend past MGJ.
Elevation: Begin at gingival margin. Insert periosteal elevator between flap and bone. Sweep apically with firm pressure. Periosteum peels cleanly off cortical bone.
Critical probing depth: Sites with initial PD above 4.2mm show more gain of clinical attachment following Widman flap surgery than following SRP alone.
LIT Critical probing depth = 4.2mm (from Fleisher lectures). Above this threshold, surgical access produces better outcomes than closed debridement.

Split Thickness Flap (Mucosal/Partial)

Periosteum stays on bone. Allows repositioning. The key to APF and mucogingival surgeries.

SPLIT THICKNESS — What comes off bone
Layers reflected: Epithelium + Some Connective Tissue ONLY PERIOSTEUM STAYS ON BONE. The flap consists of the outer layers only — a thinner flap than full thickness. Bone remains covered by its periosteal layer. Elevation technique: SHARP dissection using a #15 scalpel blade. The scalpel is held parallel to the bone surface and carefully separates the outer tissue layers from the periosteum. Requires skill and control.
You're peeling the wallpaper off but leaving the backing (periosteum) stuck to the wall (bone). Much more delicate work.
Advantages: 1. Allows REPOSITIONING — periosteum = "carpet" to slide flap over. Flap can move apically (APF), coronally (CAF), or laterally. 2. Maintains bone blood supply — periosteum is the primary source of blood supply to cortical bone. Leaving it intact prevents resorption. 3. Periosteum = ANCHOR — vertical mattress sutures bite into the periosteum on the bone side, holding the repositioned flap in place. 4. Protects bone — if fenestration or dehiscence present, periosteum coverage prevents further bone loss.
Disadvantages: 1. Technique sensitive — risk of perforation (thin tissue can tear during sharp dissection). Perforating the periosteum converts it to an unintentional full thickness flap. 2. Slower, more difficult — requires precise scalpel control. 3. Thinner flap — more fragile, harder to handle without damage. Contraindications: Thin gingiva <1mm — too thin to split safely. Scalpel will perforate. Shallow vestibule — no room to reposition flap apically.
USE WHEN: Need to REPOSITION flap - Apically Positioned Flap (APF) — the PTF slides apically over periosteum. → APF - Coronally Advanced Flap (CAF) — for root coverage (Bernimoulin 1981). - Mucogingival surgeries — lateral pedicle, tunnel technique. - Pocket elimination — flap repositioned to eliminate pocket without resecting bone. - When crestal bone margin is THIN → protect it (periosteum prevents resorption). - When fenestration or dehiscence is present — maintain periosteal coverage. → Vertical Mattress Suture (anchors PTF) → MGD Procedures
Apical positioning preserves KT — the math: KT width = gingival margin to MGJ. When you APF, you move the gingival margin apically BUT the MGJ doesn't move (it's a fixed anatomic landmark). So the distance from new margin to MGJ = MORE KT. Net result: KT width INCREASES post-APF. This is one of the key advantages of APF over other pocket reduction strategies.

→ Flap Classification Overview → Full Thickness (contrast) → Combined Flap → APF → Vertical Mattress Suture → MGD Procedures

Combined Flap — Split-Full-Split

Best of both worlds. The flap character CHANGES by zone. Crown lengthening, CAF, complex mucogingival. Bernimoulin 1981.

SPLIT → FULL → SPLIT — 3 Zones, 3 Purposes
The combined flap changes character at three zones:
ZONE 1: SPLIT Coronally At the papilla/coronal area. Sharp dissection. Periosteum preserved for suturing anchor and graft bed. Purpose: Preserve periosteum where you need to anchor sutures (vertical mattress). Periosteum at the papilla = suture anchor point.
ZONE 2: FULL at Bone Level Need to SEE where bone crest is. Blunt elevation with periosteal elevator. Bare bone exposed at the working zone. Purpose: Direct visualization of bone for osseous surgery, defect assessment, crown lengthening measurements. This is where the actual bone work happens.
ZONE 3: SPLIT Apically Past the bone work zone, go split again. Sharp dissection releases flap past MGJ into elastic alveolar mucosa. Maximum mobility. Purpose: Gain flap mobility for advancement or apical positioning. Alveolar mucosa is elastic → stretches → allows flap to move freely.
USE FOR: 1. Crown lengthening — need to see bone AND reposition flap. Most crown lengthening procedures use combined flap design. → Crown Lengthening 2. Coronally Advanced Flap (CAF) for root coverage — Bernimoulin 1981. Split at papilla (preserve periosteum for sutures), full at defect (see recession), split apically (gain coronal advancement). 3. Osseous surgery with APF — when you need bone access AND need to reposition. 4. Any procedure needing bone access + flap mobility.
Start careful (split to preserve suture anchor), go aggressive at the work zone (full to see bone), then go mobile (split to free the flap for movement).
WHY COMBINED IS SUPERIOR FOR MANY PROCEDURES
FTF alone: good bone access but poor repositioning. PTF alone: good repositioning but no bone access. Combined: bone access WHERE you need it (zone 2) + repositioning WHERE you need it (zone 3) + suture anchor WHERE you need it (zone 1). The zones are designed around the surgical objectives at each level.
LIT Bernimoulin 1981: Described the coronally advanced flap using split-full-split design for root coverage. Became the foundation for modern CAF technique.

→ Flap Classification Overview → Full Thickness Flap → Split Thickness Flap → Crown Lengthening → APF → Vertical Mattress Suture

Tissue Definitions — The Layers You Must Know

Periosteum, KT, alveolar mucosa, MGJ, attached gingiva. Every surgical decision depends on understanding these structures.

ANATOMIC LAYERS — From bone outward
PERIOSTEUM Thin, fibrous membrane directly on bone surface. Contains osteoprogenitor cells and is the primary source of blood supply to cortical bone. In split-thickness dissection, periosteum STAYS on bone. In full-thickness, it peels off with the flap. Surgical significance: - Stays behind in PTF → becomes the "carpet" for flap repositioning - Anchor for vertical mattress sutures (deep pass bites periosteum) - Protects bone from resorption when left intact - Contains osteoprogenitor cells that contribute to bone healing
Periosteum = cling wrap directly on bone. Thin but critically important for bone health and surgical technique.
KERATINIZED TISSUE (KT) Tough, pink, stippled tissue extending from the gingival margin to the MGJ. Firm, protective, attached to underlying bone/periosteum. Resists friction and trauma. Contains a keratinized epithelial layer on its surface. Total KT width = free gingival margin to MGJ Includes: Free gingiva (sulcular wall) + attached gingiva
LIT Lang & Loe 1972: Min 2mm KT needed for gingival health.
Like skin on the sole of your foot — built to take abuse.
ALVEOLAR MUCOSA Red, shiny, elastic tissue BELOW the MGJ in the vestibule. Movable. Non-keratinized. Loosely attached to underlying periosteum via elastic fibers. Surgical significance: - Where vertical releasing incisions extend into - Where you gain flap mobility (elastic tissue stretches) - Split-thickness dissection past MGJ into alveolar mucosa = maximum flap mobility for APF/CAF
Like the inside of your cheek — red, stretchy, mobile.
MGJ (Mucogingival Junction) The LINE between keratinized tissue and alveolar mucosa. Visible clinically as a color/texture change (pink stippled → red shiny). FIXED landmark — does NOT move with disease. Why this matters: When recession occurs, the gingival margin moves apically toward the MGJ, but the MGJ stays put → KT width decreases → eventually mucogingival defect. When you do APF, the margin moves apically toward MGJ but MGJ stays → KT width increases. Identification: Roll technique, visual color change, pull lower lip and observe transition zone.
ATTACHED GINGIVA The portion of KT that is bound to underlying bone/periosteum. Measured from base of sulcus to MGJ. NOT the same as total KT width: KT width = margin to MGJ (includes free gingiva) Attached gingiva = base of sulcus to MGJ (excludes free gingiva) Formula: Attached gingiva = KT width - probing depth
LIT Maynard & Wilson 1979: At least 1mm of attached gingiva required for periodontal health and stability.
ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS — Measurements from Fleisher
MAXILLA Greater Palatine Foramen & Artery: Located approximately 2mm from the posterior border of the hard palate. Traverses in a neurovascular bundle in a groove formed at the juncture of the palatine and alveolar processes. CRITICAL — must avoid during palatal surgery. Tissue Harvesting Depths: - Shallow palate: avoid thinning/harvesting at or beyond 7mm from alveolar crest - Average palate: 12mm from alveolar crest - High palate: 17mm from alveolar crest Incisive Foramen: Located posterior to the central incisors, deep to the incisive papilla. Contains the nasopalatine neurovascular bundle, which supplies the anterior palate from canine to canine.
The 7/12/17mm rule is critical for CTG harvesting. Go past these limits and you hit the greater palatine artery → hemorrhage.
MANDIBLE Mental Foramen: Located near the apices of premolars (#20, #29 area). Contains mental nerve (branch of IAN) — provides sensation to chin and lower lip. Must avoid during periapical surgery in premolar region. Inferior Alveolar Canal: Runs through body of mandible. Contains IAN + inferior alveolar artery/vein. Safe zone for implants/surgery depends on CBCT assessment. Lingual Nerve: Runs along lingual surface of mandible, close to 3rd molar region. Can be injured during distal wedge procedures or lingual flap reflection in posterior mandible. Mandibular Foramen: Entry point of IAN into the mandible on the medial ramus surface.

Osseous Surgery — Overview

Reshaping bone to create physiologic architecture. The goal: positive architecture, minimal PD, maintainable contours. ESSAY 1+3

ESSAY 1 + 3 Osseous surgery concepts tested in Crown Lengthening (Essay 1: "when gingivectomy alone vs flap + removing bone") and NSPT/Surgical Therapy (Essay 3: "rationale for surgical perio therapy").
OSSEOUS SURGERY — DEFINITION
The term osseous surgery encompasses varied forms of therapeutic procedures; each to manage the variety of bony lesions resulting from periodontal diseases, and with different goals of therapy in mind. OSSEOUS SURGERY is: - Fundamental to elimination of bony defects - Creation of a physiologic form - Controls and maintains a contiguous relationship with the overlying gingival tissues - Resulting in minimal probing depths KEY Tissue follows bone — ALWAYS. If you create positive bone architecture, tissue will drape over it in a physiologic contour with shallow sulci.
OBJECTIVES OF OSSEOUS RESECTIVE THERAPY
From Fleisher slides — know these cold: 1. Create contours that permit patients to accomplish effective plaque control 2. Create bone contours which gingival tissue will parallel (tissue follows bone) 3. Permit primary wound closure 4. Expose additional clinical crown for restorative treatment Objectives of Pocket Elimination: - Minimal probing depths → complete debridement with hand instrumentation during maintenance - Effective daily home care procedures - Predictable use of toothbrushing, flossing, and other adjunctive methods "The establishment of a shallow gingival sulcus by pocket elimination is a prerequisite for adequate plaque control and preventive maintenance."
INDICATIONS 1. Adequate remaining attachment once physiologic morphology is established 2. No esthetic limitations 3. No anatomic limitations (mental foramen, IAN, sinus, furcation) 4. No root hypersensitivity limitations
CONTRAINDICATIONS 1. Inadequate remaining attachment (would leave tooth unsupported) 2. Esthetic limitations (anterior zone) 3. Anatomic limitations (mental foramen, IAN, sinus proximity) 4. Root hypersensitivity limitations 5. Alternative, more effective therapy available (e.g., GTR for contained defect) Fleisher: NO resective surgery in anterior UNLESS full prosthetic reconstruction planned.
DISADVANTAGES of Resective Therapy: 1. Attachment loss caused by removal of supporting bone — the fundamental cost 2. Un-esthetic root exposure 3. Hypersensitive roots These are real trade-offs — you're sacrificing bone to create architecture that's maintainable long-term. The clinical benefit must outweigh these costs.
THE TWO TYPES OF BONE REMOVAL — Know cold
OSTECTOMY Removal of SUPPORTING (radicular + interradicular) bone to eliminate osseous defects. CAUSES attachment loss — you're removing bone that was holding the tooth in place. → Ostectomy Detail
OSTEOPLASTY Removal of NON-SUPPORTING bone for reshaping purposes. NO attachment loss. Reshaping ledges, tori, exostoses, widow's peaks. → Osteoplasty Detail
LIT Selipsky 1976 (Dent Clin North Am 20:76): Evidence suggests very little supporting bone is removed when resective techniques are properly applied. The need for splinting due to diminished attachment levels should be determined PRIOR to the procedure.
LIT Nyman et al 1977 (J Clin Perio): Without recall, surgery fails regardless of technique. Different surgical techniques + single session OHI + no recall = rebound of PD to pre-surgical levels over 2 years + further attachment loss.
LIT Rosling et al 1976 (J Clin Perio): Different techniques + repeated OHI + recall q2wks for 2yrs = maintained surgically reduced PD + clinical attachment gains in most sites.
LIT Lindhe et al 1984 (J Clin Perio): 5yr longitudinal study. High standard of home care maintained attachment levels and PD reductions more consistently than poor plaque control patients.
LIT Ramfjord et al 1982 / Isidor & Karring 1986: Professional tooth cleaning including subgingival scaling EVERY 3 MONTHS at least partly compensated for negative effects of variable self-performed plaque control.

Ostectomy — Supporting Bone Removal

The "costly" move. Removes bone that IS supporting the tooth. CAUSES attachment loss. ESSAY 1+3

OSTECTOMY — SUPPORTING BONE
Definition: The removal of radicular and interradicular SUPPORTING bone to eliminate osseous defects. Key distinction: This is bone that is directly supporting the tooth — the alveolar bone proper (lamina dura), the bone around the roots, bone between roots of multi-rooted teeth. Removing it means removing attachment apparatus = ATTACHMENT LOSS.
Ostectomy = cutting into the foundation of the building. The building (tooth) becomes less stable. You do it because the alternative (leaving pathologic architecture) is worse long-term.
WHEN IS OSTECTOMY NEEDED? 1. Eliminate infrabony defects — crater walls, hemiseptal defects, where walls must be removed to create positive architecture. 2. Establish biologic width for crown lengthening — must create 4mm distance from restorative margin to bone crest. If bone is too coronal → remove it. 3. Create positive architecture — radicular bone must be APICAL to interdental septum at completion. Sometimes radicular bone on facial/lingual must be removed to achieve this. 4. Expose adequate clinical crown — for restorative purposes when bone is covering tooth structure needed for ferrule/margin. → Crown Lengthening
Indications (same as osseous resective) - Adequate remaining attachment AFTER removal - No esthetic limitations - No anatomic limitations - No root hypersensitivity limitations - Benefits outweigh cost of attachment loss
Contraindications - Inadequate remaining attachment — tooth would lose support - Esthetic zone (anterior) unless prosthetic reconstruction planned - Adjacent teeth would be damaged (bone removal affects neighbors) - Deep furcation involvement on multi-rooted teeth - Anatomic limits (mental foramen, sinus, IAN canal)
Instrumentation: - High-speed handpiece with carbide or diamond burs + generous water irrigation - Hand instruments: Ochsenbein chisel for fine recontouring, Schluger file, bone rongeurs - Bone sounding BEFORE surgery: Probe horizontally from facial/lingual to locate crest of proximal bony walls. Probe vertically to locate base of crater. This maps the defect before you ever reflect a flap.

→ Osseous Surgery Overview → Osteoplasty (contrast — no attachment loss) → Osseous Techniques → Crown Lengthening → Bone Defects

Osteoplasty — Non-Supporting Bone Reshaping

The "free" move. Reshapes bone that is NOT supporting the tooth. NO attachment loss. ESSAY 1+3

OSTEOPLASTY — NON-SUPPORTING BONE
Definition: The surgical removal of NON-SUPPORTING bone for the purpose of reshaping it to support a physiologic gingival and osseous contour. Key distinction: This is bone that is NOT directly supporting the tooth — buccal/lingual plates that are too thick, tori, exostoses, widow's peaks, ledges. Removing it does NOT cause attachment loss because it wasn't attached to the tooth.
Osteoplasty = cosmetic recontouring of the frame. The foundation stays intact. You're smoothing the surface, not cutting into the structure. This is the "affordable" bone surgery.
INDICATIONS FOR OSTEOPLASTY
Primary Indications: 1. Pocket elimination — thick ledges create deep pockets even without attachment loss 2. Tori reduction — palatal torus, mandibular tori interfering with prosthetics or hygiene 3. Reduction of thick heavy bony ledges and/or exostoses — common on buccal of maxillary and mandibular posterior 4. Enhanced flap placement with improved alveolar contours — better flap adaptation post-APF
Additional Indications: 5. Shallow osseous craters (1-3mm deep) — flatten the crater by recontouring surrounding bone 6. Blunted interdental craters — reshape to allow tissue to follow physiologic contour 7. Small intrabony defects on buccal or lingual — can often be eliminated with osteoplasty alone 8. Widow's peak removal — bony spines at transitional line angles → Osseous Techniques (widow's peak, etc.)
Clinical Pearl: Most osseous surgery is OSTEOPLASTY When Fleisher says "very little supporting bone is removed when resective techniques are properly applied," he means most of the bone removal is osteoplasty (reshaping non-supporting ledges, peaks, and contours) rather than ostectomy (removing supporting bone). Proper technique prioritizes osteoplasty to achieve architecture with minimal attachment cost.
LIT Selipsky 1976: Very little supporting bone is actually removed with proper technique. Most of the recontouring is non-supporting bone.
Instrumentation: - High-speed carbide or diamond burs with generous water supply — primary tools for bulk removal of ledges, tori, exostoses - Ochsenbein chisels — for fine recontouring of widow's peaks and line angles - Schluger files — for refining interproximal bone contours - Rongeurs — for removing large pieces of tori or exostoses KEY Always copious irrigation. Bone is heat-sensitive — dry cutting causes necrosis.

Osseous Recontouring Techniques

Widow's peak, double parabolization, shallow craters, radicular recontouring. Step-by-step approaches from Fleisher lectures. ESSAY 1+3

SPECIFIC OSSEOUS RECONTOURING CONCEPTS
WIDOW'S PEAK What: Tiny bony spike at the transitional line angle (where facial/lingual bone meets interproximal bone). A sharp, pointed projection of bone. Problem: If left, tissue follows bone → tissue bounces UP coronally at peak → creates isolated deep pocket at the line angle that's impossible to maintain. Treatment: Remove with Ochsenbein chisel (hand instrument). Quick, precise. Goal: Establish flat or horizontal mesiodistal crest at the transitional line angles. No spikes, no points, no coronal bumps. Type: OSTEOPLASTY (non-supporting bone → no attachment loss)
A widow's peak is like a speed bump in the gutter — water (plaque/bacteria) pools behind it. Flatten the bump and everything flows smoothly.
DOUBLE PARABOLIZATION What: Treat a molar as "two premolars." Carve separate parabolic curves over mesial root and distal root individually. Bone comes UP at the furcation (stays intact). Each root gets its own scallop. Definition: The development of a parabolic curve over each root toward the furcation, with a vertical groove in the interdental bone extending apically from the furca. Purpose: Prevents furcation exposure. If you carved one big parabolic curve over the entire molar, you'd expose the furcation. By treating each root separately, the bone between roots (furcation ridge) is preserved. When: Multi-rooted teeth (molars) during osseous resective surgery.
Instead of one big arch over the whole molar, you make two mini-arches — one per root — keeping the furcation ridge intact between them.
SHALLOW CRATERS (1-3mm deep) Definition: A shallow defect is 1-3mm deep and within the dimensions of the root trunk when associated with a multi-rooted tooth. Treatment: High-speed carbide or diamond burs + generous water supply. Remove bone so that the base of the crater becomes the most coronal position in the interdental septum. Essentially flatten the crater by removing the walls down to the crater floor. Adjacent teeth: Bone of adjacent teeth may need to be removed during surgery to avoid a precipitous change in osseous morphology between teeth. You can't leave a cliff — tissue won't adapt to abrupt steps. Contrast — Deep defect: Greater than 3mm deep, and when adjacent to a multi-rooted tooth, extends apical to the root trunk. Deep defects may require regenerative approaches. → Bone Defect Classification
RADICULAR BONE RECONTOURING What: Radicular bone on the facial and lingual surfaces is contoured by ostectomy/osteoplasty to produce parabolic architecture similar to what existed prior to disease. Thickness factor: Radicular bone thickness depends primarily on the tooth-to-bone relationship existing in the area. Thicker bone = more recontouring needed. Thin bone = less to work with. Posterior principle: As the mesiodistal dimension increases (posteriorly), the bone and gingiva accept a FLATTER morphology. Anteriors have dramatic scalloping. Posteriors are relatively flat. Goal at completion: Radicular bone should be APICAL to the interdental septum = POSITIVE ARCHITECTURE.
BONE ARCHITECTURE GOALS
At completion of resective osseous surgery: 1. Positive architecture — interproximal bone MORE coronal than radicular bone. This is the healthy state. 2. Parabolic contours — scalloped bone follows tooth root anatomy. Degree of scalloping varies anterior→posterior. 3. No abrupt transitions — smooth gradual changes between teeth. No cliffs. Tissue can't follow abrupt bone steps → would leave pockets. 4. No widow's peaks — flat mesiodistal crest at line angles. 5. Furcation protection — double parabolization on molars to prevent furcation exposure.
REVERSE ARCHITECTURE = PATHOLOGIC
Reverse architecture is a morphologic relationship in which radicular bone on facial/lingual is MORE CORONAL than adjacent interdental bone. This is BOTH an etiologic factor in disease progression AND a result of the disease process. Must be corrected to positive architecture during osseous surgery.
BONE SOUNDING — Pre-surgical assessment Horizontal probing: From facial and lingual, probe horizontally to locate the crest of the proximal bony walls. Tells you the height and width of remaining bone walls. Vertical probing: Probe vertically to locate the base of the crater. Tells you the depth of the defect. Why: Maps the 3D defect geometry before reflecting a flap. Helps plan how much osteoplasty vs ostectomy will be needed. Combined with radiographs for comprehensive assessment. EXAM Radiographs alone are INSUFFICIENT for osseous defect evaluation. Facial/lingual defects are masked by overlying cortical plates. Must probe (bone sound) AND reflect flap for definitive assessment.
ROOT TRUNK — Critical for multi-rooted teeth Definition: The distance from CEJ to furcation entrance. Varies by tooth. Key fact: Root trunk dimension is often narrowest for the first molar. This means first molars are most vulnerable to furcation involvement from osseous disease. Clinical significance: The depth of periodontal defects in millimeters is NOT as significant for multi-rooted teeth as is the relationship of the defect to the root trunk. A 3mm defect that extends past the root trunk into the furcation is FAR worse than a 5mm defect that stays within the root trunk. → Bone Defect Classification

Apically Positioned Flap (APF) + Osseous Resection

The complete package: predictability, minimal PD, KT gain. The workhorse of resective perio surgery. ESSAY 1+3

APF + OSSEOUS RESECTION — The Complete Package
Apically positioned flaps in conjunction with osseous resection provide: 1. Predictability — most predictable way to achieve pocket elimination long-term 2. Achieve minimal probing depths — tissue drapes over recontoured bone in shallow sulci 3. Potential to INCREASE the zone of attached gingiva — as flap moves apical, MGJ doesn't → net KT gain 4. Stable base for restorative dentistry — predictable tissue levels for margin placement
LIT Rosling et al 1976: With proper recall, surgically reduced PD maintained over 2 years + attachment gains noted. APF + osseous is THE predictable combination.
APF STEP-BY-STEP PROCEDURE
Surgical Sequence: 1. Anesthesia — profound local anesthesia of the surgical site. Infiltration + block as needed. 2. Internal bevel incision — submarginal (plenty KT) or crevicular (limited KT). Margins thinned and parabolic. +/- vertical releasing incisions for access/mobility. 3. Crevicular incision (if submarginal used as primary) — second incision in sulcus to free the collar of tissue. Collar curetted away. 4. Flap elevation — split-thickness (or combined split-full-split). Periosteum stays on bone to serve as the carpet for repositioning. 5. Debridement — thorough root planing under direct vision. Remove all calculus, biofilm, granulation tissue from defects. 6. Osseous assessment — evaluate bone architecture. Identify defects, craters, ledges, widow's peaks, reverse architecture. 7. Osseous surgery — osteoplasty first (reshape non-supporting bone: ledges, peaks, exostoses), then ostectomy if needed (remove supporting bone for physiologic architecture). Goal = positive architecture. 8. Reposition flap apically — slide the split-thickness flap apically over the periosteum carpet. Position the flap margin at or just coronal to the new bone crest. Obliterate dead space between flap and bone. 9. Suture — vertical mattress sutures to secure flap in new apical position. Deep pass anchors to periosteum on bone. → Vertical Mattress 10. Pack — periodontal dressing (optional) to protect surgical site.
WHY APF GAINS KT Before surgery: KT width = margin to MGJ (say 3mm). After APF: Margin moves apically (say 2mm). MGJ stays put (fixed landmark). New KT width = 3mm + 2mm = 5mm. Also: Attached gingiva = base of sulcus to MGJ. After APF, the new shallow sulcus sits at the repositioned margin, and MGJ is still in original position → attached gingiva width increases too.
You're sliding the ruler's starting mark downhill but the endpoint stays put. The distance gets longer.
APF IS IMPOSSIBLE ON PALATE No MGJ on palate = no elastic alveolar mucosa = no mobility = no repositioning. All palatal tissue is dense mucoperiosteum firmly attached to palatal bone. Cannot move apically. Palatal management: Internal bevel (paramarginal) to thin tissue. Suture back at same position or slightly coronal. Reduce tissue bulk, don't try to reposition.
Principles of Periodontal Surgery (Fleisher slides): 1. Medical History — clear patient for surgery 2. Develop a full treatment plan BEFORE surgery 3. Understand the anatomy of the surgical area 4. Provide profound anesthesia 5. Follow aseptic surgical technique (pre-surgical rinse shown to reduce transient bacteria) 6. Atraumatic tissue management 7. Attain hemostasis 8. Atraumatic suturing 9. Obliterate dead space between flap and bone — critical for healing 10. Promote stable wound healing

Subtractive vs Additive Procedures

Every periodontal surgical procedure falls into one of these two camps. Pocket reduction can occur by either approach. ESSAY 3

SUBTRACTIVE PROCEDURES — Remove tissue/bone to reduce pockets
Complete List from Fleisher Lectures: 1. SRP (Scaling & Root Planing) — subgingival root curettage. Least invasive surgical approach. Can be definitive for many sites. Non-surgical but listed as subtractive. 2. Gingival Curettage — surgically removes the inner surface of the gingival pocket. No longer used as a standalone procedure. Obsolete. 3. External Bevel Gingivectomy — suprabony defects ONLY. Adequate KT required + no bone work. Secondary intention healing. → External Bevel 4. Internal Bevel Gingivectomy — same tissue reduction but performed as part of a flap procedure (tissue collar removed with internal bevel). 5. ENAP (Excisional New Attachment Procedure) — excise pocket lining to promote new attachment to root surface. 6. Modified Widman Flap — access to roots without intentionally repositioning tissue or removing bone. Clean roots under direct vision, replace flap to original position. More conservative than osseous resective. 7. OFD (Open Flap Debridement) — access only, flap goes back to original position. No osseous recontouring. 8. Complete List from Fleisher Lectures: 1. Osseous Graft — scaffold to replace bone. Materials: autograft (gold standard — own bone), allograft (human cadaver: FDBA, DFDBA), xenograft (bovine/porcine: Bio-Oss), alloplast (synthetic: HA, TCP, bioactive glass). 2. Soft Tissue Grafts — FGG (free gingival graft), CTG (connective tissue graft), Alloderm (acellular dermal matrix). Add KT or root coverage.

Suture Selection & Technique

SUTURE TYPES — Three main categories
SIMPLE INTERRUPTED Most basic. Single pass through both flap edges, knot tied to approximate tissue. Use: Closing vertical releasing incisions. Simple wound closure where repositioning is NOT needed. Advantage: Simple, fast, can remove one suture without disturbing others. Limitation: Cannot reposition tissue — only approximates. Not suitable for APF.
CONTINUOUS SLING Thread wraps around teeth in a continuous loop. FLEISHER: NEVER USE THIS. Why? If one segment fails, the entire suture line unravels. No individual suture control. Higher risk of wound dehiscence. Not taught at BU for periodontal surgery. Contrast: Interrupted sutures = each is independent. One fails, others hold.
VERTICAL MATTRESS THE suture for APF. Primary suture for repositioning flaps apically. Passes through flap AND anchors to periosteum on bone → holds flap in new apical position. This is the suture you MUST know cold. → Vertical Mattress Detail
SUTURE MATERIALS
NON-RESORBABLE Must be removed manually (7-14 days post-op). - Silk — braided, multifilament. Easy to handle, good knot security. BUT: wicks bacteria (braided structure harbors plaque). Most commonly used non-resorbable in perio. - PTFE (Gore-Tex suture) — monofilament. Non-wicking, non-porous. Excellent for membrane procedures (GTR/GBR) where bacterial wicking is critical concern. More expensive.
RESORBABLE Dissolve over time, no removal needed. - Gut — plain gut, fast absorption (~5-7 days). Unpredictable resorption. - Chromic Gut — chromium-treated, slower absorption (~10-14 days). More predictable. - Vicryl (Polyglactin 910) — braided multifilament. Predictable resorption (~60-90 days). Good knot security. Most common resorbable in perio. - Monocryl (Poliglecaprone 25) — monofilament. Smooth passage, less tissue reaction. - Polysorb — braided, coated. - PDS (Polydioxanone) — monofilament. Long-lasting (~180 days). - Caprosyn (Polyglytone 621) — monofilament. Rapid resorption.
NEEDLE TYPES
Needle Shapes: 1. 1/2 circle — most common in perio. Good for most intraoral suturing. 2. Straight needle — rarely used intraorally. Mostly for external skin closure. 3. 5/8 circle — for deeper, confined areas. More needle arc for tight spaces. 4. 3/8 circle — less common in perio.
Cutting Edge Types: 1. Taper (round body) — pushes tissue apart without cutting. For delicate tissue. Less tissue damage but harder to pass through dense tissue. 2. Conventional cutting — cutting edge on INNER curve. Risk of cutting through tissue when pulling. 3. REVERSE CUTTING — cutting edge on OUTER curve. Standard for periodontal surgery. The cutting edge faces AWAY from the wound edge → less risk of suture pulling through tissue. Strongest configuration for tissue that might tear. EXAM Reverse cutting = cutting edge on the outer curve. This is the perio standard.

Vertical Mattress Suture — APF Technique

Step-by-step APF suturing technique. Needle/material selection. Reverse cutting. THE suture for repositioning flaps. ESSAY 1

VERTICAL MATTRESS — The APF Suture
Why vertical mattress for APF? The vertical mattress suture does something no other suture can: it passes through the flap AND anchors to the periosteum that remains on bone. This holds the flap in its new APICAL position against the bone surface. Without this anchoring, the flap would spring back to its original position (elastic memory of tissue).
A simple interrupted suture just pulls two flap edges together — it can't pin a flap DOWN onto bone. The vertical mattress has a "deep pass" that grabs the periosteum on the bone side, like a tack pinning a poster to a wall.
STEP-BY-STEP TECHNIQUE
Step 1: Enter the flap (APICAL entry) Needle penetrates through the flap from the OUTER (epithelial) surface. Entry point is at the APICAL edge of the repositioned flap — this is where you want the knot to sit. Needle direction: From outside → through full thickness of flap → exits on inner (raw) surface of flap.
Step 2: Engage periosteum (the anchor) After exiting the inner surface of the flap, the needle enters the periosteum that is still attached to bone. This is the key — the periosteum left behind during split-thickness elevation serves as the anchor point. The needle passes THROUGH the periosteum and then back up through the flap from inside → out. Critical: The bite of periosteum must be secure — adequate width and depth. If the periosteum bite is too shallow, the suture will pull through and fail.
Step 3: Tie the knot APICALLY The knot is tied on the OUTER/APICAL surface of the flap — where the needle first penetrated. As you tighten, the suture pulls the flap down against the periosteum/bone, securing it in its new apical position. Result: Flap is pinned apically. The deep pass through periosteum holds it against bone. The superficial pass through the flap edge prevents the tissue from rolling. Dead space between flap and bone is obliterated.
Clinical pearls: 1. Knot position: Always APICAL (where you want the flap positioned). The knot pulls toward itself. 2. Periosteum anchor: This is why split-thickness elevation is essential for APF — the periosteum MUST be present on bone. Full-thickness elevation removes periosteum → no anchor → vertical mattress cannot function. 3. Tension: Sufficient to hold flap against bone but not so tight that tissue strangulates. Strangulation → necrosis → suture failure. 4. Spacing: Multiple vertical mattress sutures along the flap margin, typically one per tooth. 5. Interproximal tissue: The interdental papilla may be secured with simple interrupted sutures while buccal flap is held with vertical mattress.
NEEDLE SELECTION Shape: 1/2 circle — standard for most perio suturing. Adequate arc for intraoral access. Cutting edge: REVERSE CUTTING — cutting edge on the outer curve. Standard for periodontal surgery. Prevents suture from cutting through tissue when under tension. Size: Small enough for precise placement but strong enough to pass through periosteum without bending.
Reverse cutting = the sharp edge faces AWAY from the wound. Like holding a knife blade-out so you push through tissue cleanly without lacerating the wound edge.
MATERIAL SELECTION For APF with vertical mattress: - Silk (4-0 or 5-0) — good handling, good knot security. Most common for routine perio. Remove at 7-10 days. - PTFE — if bacterial wicking is a concern (membrane procedures). - Vicryl (4-0) — if resorbable preferred (patient compliance concern for suture removal). Size: 4-0 most common in perio surgery. 5-0 for finer tissue (anterior esthetics). 3-0 for thick posterior tissue.
WHY VERTICAL MATTRESS REQUIRES SPLIT THICKNESS
The entire vertical mattress technique depends on periosteum being PRESENT on the bone surface. If you used full-thickness elevation everywhere, there would be no periosteum to anchor to → vertical mattress is impossible → flap cannot be repositioned → APF fails.

This is the critical connection: KT decision → incision type → flap type → suture type. Each decision cascades into the next. Limited KT → crevicular incision → split-thickness flap (preserve periosteum) → vertical mattress suture (anchor to periosteum) → APF achieved. → Split Thickness Flap → Combined Flap

CROWN LENGTHENING — OVERVIEW
Crown Lengthening = a periodontal procedure that is restoratively driven

Unlike osseous surgery (disease-driven) or mucogingival surgery (tissue-driven), crown lengthening exists to serve restorative dentistry. The periodontist creates the conditions the restorative dentist needs to place a proper restoration.

Core principle: The restorative margin MUST NOT violate the biologic width. When decay, fracture, or insufficient tooth structure places the margin too close to (or below) the alveolar crest, we surgically move the bone and/or tissue apically to re-establish the required clearance.

Key distance: ± 4mm from restorative margin to crest of bone — allows 2mm biologic width + 1mm sulcus + ~1mm clearance for ferrule/impression.

CROWN LENGTHENING — DEFINITION
A periodontal surgical procedure that exposes more clinical crown by apically repositioning the gingival margin and/or alveolar bone crest. Indicated when insufficient sound tooth structure exists supragingivally for a biologically and restoratively sound restoration.
Crown lengthening is the periodontist answering the restorative dentist's request: "I can't see enough tooth to work with — give me more." The periodontist moves tissue and bone out of the way so the margin sits in a zone the body won't fight against.
LIT Gargiulo, Wentz & Orban 1961 (J Periodont 42:8): Established dimensions of the dentogingival junction — the foundational measurements for biologic width.

BIOLOGIC WIDTH (Supracrestal Attachment)
Biologic Width = the non-negotiable zone of tissue attachment above the bone crest
BIOLOGIC WIDTH (SUPRACRESTAL ATTACHMENT)
The combined width of the junctional epithelium and the connective tissue attachment that occupies the space between the base of the gingival sulcus and the alveolar bone crest. This is the body's "do not disturb" zone — it will ALWAYS re-establish itself.

From bone crest coronally:

~1mm connective tissue attachment (CT fibers inserting into cementum) + ~1mm junctional epithelium (JE — hemidesmosomes attaching to tooth) = ~2mm biologic width.

Then the sulcus (~1mm) sits on top of that.

So from bone crest to gingival margin ≈ 3mm total.

The Critical Measurement:

Distance from restorative margin to crest of bone = ± 4mm

Breakdown: 2mm BW + 1mm sulcus + ~1mm clearance for ferrule/impression

If margin is within 2mm of bone → BW violation → chronic inflammation, bone resorption, pocket formation

What happens when BW is violated:

1. Chronic inflammatory response at margin-tissue interface

2. Body resorbs bone to re-establish its required 2mm attachment zone

3. Now the margin is exposed AND you've lost bone → worse than where you started

4. Pocket formation, persistent bleeding, restoration failure

Think of BW as the body's "do not disturb" zone. It will ALWAYS try to re-establish itself. If you put a crown margin in this zone, the body will eat bone away until it gets its 2mm back — but now your margin is exposed and you're worse off than you started.

Diagnosing BW violation:

• Do NOT rely on radiographs alone to diagnose BW invasion — they show bone but can't precisely locate the margin relative to BW

• Probe carefully around the restoration

• Take a PA radiograph — measure distance from restorative margin to bone crest

• If margin is within 2mm of bone → BW violation confirmed

CROWN LENGTHENING — INDICATIONS & CONTRAINDICATIONS
ESSAY 1 QN KEY Essay Q2: "You remove a temp, see bleeding and ulceration. How do you determine if CL is necessary or another problem exists?"

Other problems that cause bleeding/ulceration around a temp (rule these out FIRST):

1. Plaque-induced gingivitis — inadequate OH around temporary restoration

2. Open margins on temp — overhanging or short margins trapping bacteria

3. Excess cement or temp material subgingivally → foreign body reaction

4. Allergic reaction to temporary material

5. Occlusal trauma on temp → TFO (trauma from occlusion)

How to distinguish — the decision algorithm:

1. Probe carefully around the restoration

2. Take a PA radiograph

3. Measure distance from restorative margin to bone crest

4. If margin is within 2mm of bone → BW violation → CL needed

5. If margin is 3-4mm from bone and you still have inflammation → it's one of the other causes above

6. Fix the OTHER cause first. Only do CL if you've ruled everything else out AND confirmed BW invasion

Don't jump to CL. The exam wants to see that you THINK before you cut. Check the simplest explanations first. A dentist who reaches for the scalpel before checking for excess cement is a dangerous dentist.
Indications for Crown Lengthening:

1. Tooth decay at or below the gingival margin

2. Tooth fracture below the gingival margin — with adequate remaining periodontal support and attachment

3. Teeth with excessive wear — insufficient clinical crown for retention

4. Teeth with insufficient interocclusal space — need more crown height for restoration

5. Delayed (altered) passive eruption — gummy smile, pseudo-short clinical crowns

Contraindications:

1. Inadequate remaining tooth structure/support post-surgery — would leave tooth hopeless

2. Esthetic concerns that can't be managed — especially anteriors

3. Anatomic limitations — furcation proximity (removing bone exposes furcation), adjacent teeth would be compromised

4. Root hypersensitivity concerns in the patient

5. When alternative therapy is superior — orthodontic forced eruption, deep margin elevation

6. Inadequate root length — would create unfavorable crown-to-root ratio

COSLET CLASSIFICATION (1977)
A classification of altered passive eruption based on two variables: (1) amount of keratinized tissue and (2) position of alveolar bone crest relative to the CEJ. The 4 resulting types determine the surgical approach — gingivectomy alone vs flap with osseous surgery.

Two variables, four combinations:

Variable 1: Keratinized tissue — Lots (Type 1) vs Minimal (Type 2)

Variable 2: Bone position — Normal/apical to CEJ (Subtype A) vs At the CEJ (Subtype B)

TYPE 1A — Lots KT + Bone NORMAL

→ GINGIVECTOMY ALONE

Simplest scenario. Excess tissue but bone is in normal position (2-3mm apical to CEJ). Just remove the excess gingiva. No bone needs touching.

Technique: External bevel gingivectomy.

→ Full detail → External Bevel Technique
TYPE 1B — Lots KT + Bone AT CEJ

→ GINGIVECTOMY FAILS — Need FLAP + OSSEOUS

This is the trap. Looks like 1A clinically (lots of tissue) but bone is TOO HIGH. If you just remove tissue, you violate BW because bone is right at the CEJ. Must do flap surgery + remove bone to create 4mm distance.

→ Full detail → Ostectomy needed
TYPE 2A — Minimal KT + Bone NORMAL

→ CAUTION — KT augmentation may be needed

Bone position is fine but you have no tissue to spare. Gingivectomy would remove keratinized tissue you can't afford to lose → creates MGD. May need to augment KT (FGG) before or during CL.

→ Full detail → FGG for KT augmentation
TYPE 2B — Minimal KT + Bone AT CEJ

→ MOST COMPLEX — needs bone + tissue management

Double problem: bone too high AND insufficient KT. Need osseous surgery to move bone AND tissue management to preserve/augment whatever KT exists. Combined flap approach. Highest complication risk of all four types.

→ Full detail → Combined Flap

COSLET TYPE 1A — Gingivectomy Alone
ESSAY 1 QN Lots of KT + Bone at NORMAL position (not at CEJ)

The simplest scenario. Excess gingival tissue covers the clinical crown but the bone is in its normal anatomic position (2-3mm apical to the CEJ). The excess tissue is the ONLY problem.

Why it works: Since bone is already in normal position, removing tissue won't violate biologic width. After healing, the new gingival margin will sit at the CEJ with adequate BW maintained between the sulcus base and bone crest.

Post-op: Secondary intention healing. Surgical site covered with periodontal dressing. Heals by granulation from the connective tissue base. Keratinization re-establishes from adjacent KT.

This is the "easy win." Patient has a gummy smile, you probe and find the bone is normal distance from CEJ, tons of keratinized tissue — just trim it. Like mowing an overgrown lawn. The foundation (bone) is fine; the grass (tissue) just needs cutting.

COSLET TYPE 1B — Flap + Osseous Required
ESSAY 1 QN EXAM TRAP Lots of KT + Bone AT THE CEJ

GINGIVECTOMY FAILS HERE. This is the critical distinction from 1A.

Why gingivectomy fails: If you just remove the excess tissue, you expose the bone crest — which is sitting RIGHT AT the CEJ. The biologic width would be violated because there's no room for the required 2mm of attachment (CT + JE) between the intended margin and the bone. The body will respond with chronic inflammation and bone resorption until it re-establishes BW — defeating the purpose of your surgery.

Required treatment:

1. Raise a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap

2. Perform ostectomy (remove supporting bone) to move the bone crest apically

3. Create the required 4mm distance from intended margin to new bone crest

4. Reposition flap — may need apical positioning

The trap: Clinically, 1A and 1B can look IDENTICAL — both have lots of KT and excess tissue. You CANNOT distinguish them without a radiograph or bone sounding. If you do a gingivectomy on a 1B thinking it's a 1A, the result will fail.

This is the exam favorite. They'll describe a patient with excess tissue, lots of KT — looks like a simple gingivectomy case. But the bone is at the CEJ. If you answer "gingivectomy alone," you've fallen into the trap. ALWAYS check bone level before deciding.

COSLET TYPE 2A — Caution: KT Deficiency

Treatment approach:

2. Then perform the crown lengthening procedure

3. Or combine KT augmentation with the crown lengthening in a single staged approach

It's like needing to trim a hedge but only having a thin strip of hedge. You can't just cut it — you'd have nothing left. You need to grow more hedge first (FGG), THEN shape it.

HEALING TIME — How Long Before Final Restoration?
ESSAY 1 QN KEY How long for healing? 6-8-12 weeks or more
BW RE-ESTABLISHMENT RATIONALE
The relationship of the alveolar crest to the base of the gingival sulcus, with the intervening CT and epithelial attachment, is stable and predictable in the presence of optimal plaque control and effective recall therapy. After surgical alteration, time is required for these dimensions to re-establish and stabilize before committing to a final margin position.

Why you must wait:

1. BW needs time to re-establish its ~2mm dimension above the new bone crest

2. Gingival margin position is UNSTABLE during healing — will migrate

3. If you prep and impress too early → margin position hasn't settled → gingival margin will shift → exposed margins, re-inflammation

Posterior vs Anterior:

• Posterior: More forgiving. 6-8 weeks often sufficient. Less esthetic concern.

• Anterior: Takes LONGER. More esthetic concern → need tissue to fully mature and stabilize before committing to a final margin position. 12 weeks or more. Consider waiting even longer if tissue still looks immature.

Consequences of insufficient healing time:

1. BW re-establishment continues AFTER restoration is placed

2. Bone resorption proceeds under the new crown

3. Margin becomes exposed or submerged unpredictably

4. Pocket formation around restoration

5. Treatment failure — you'll need to redo the crown AND possibly redo the CL

Creeping Attachment: Post-surgical coronal migration of gingival margin over time. Slow process — 0.5mm-1.2mm over 12 months. Must account for this in treatment planning. The margin will creep coronally, potentially covering tooth structure you exposed surgically.

Gingival Rebound: Regeneration of interdental soft tissues following surgical denudation. The papillae will partially reform even after complete removal. Factor this into margin placement planning.

CROWN LENGTHENING — 3 Complications with Remedies
ESSAY 1 QN KEY The essay asks for 3 complications and their remedies:
1. Violation of Adjacent Teeth

Bone removal to create 4mm distance on the target tooth inevitably affects neighboring teeth too. You must blend the osseous contours, which means removing bone from teeth that were HEALTHY. Creates iatrogenic defects on adjacent teeth.

Remedy:

• Pre-surgical planning with radiographs to assess how much bone removal is needed and which adjacent teeth will be affected

• Consider orthodontic forced eruption as alternative if adjacent teeth would be significantly compromised

• Inform patient of risks to adjacent teeth BEFORE surgery

2. Root Sensitivity

Exposed root surfaces post-surgery → dentin hypersensitivity. Cementum removed during root planing + newly exposed root surface = open dentinal tubules → sensitivity to thermal, osmotic, and tactile stimuli.

Remedy:

• Desensitizing agents (fluoride varnish, potassium nitrate toothpaste)

• Time — usually improves as cementum re-mineralizes and dentinal tubules occlude naturally

• Resin bonding agents if persistent

3. Esthetic Compromise

Especially concerning in the anterior region. Results in longer clinical crowns, visible root surface, tissue asymmetry, potential "black triangles" from papilla loss. The smile line may now show roots, color mismatch between enamel and exposed cementum/dentin.

Remedy:

• Careful surgical planning — anticipate esthetic outcome

• Consider orthodontic alternative for anteriors

• Gingival prosthetics (pink porcelain) in severe cases

• This is why Fleisher says NO resective surgery anterior unless full prosthetic reconstruction planned

Alternatives to Crown Lengthening:

1. Orthodontic Forced Eruption:

Move the tooth coronally with orthodontic forces, bringing bone and tissue with it (slow eruption) or without it (rapid eruption). Now you have more tooth structure above bone level WITHOUT removing bone from adjacent teeth. Especially useful when CL would damage neighbors.

Key: A stable reduced periodontium responds normally to orthodontic forces.

2. Deep Margin Elevation (DME):

Conservative technique for restoring teeth with subgingival margins. Composite is placed to elevate the margin to a supragingival position, avoiding CL entirely. Gaining popularity. Not appropriate for all situations but avoids surgery altogether when feasible.

Deep Margin Elevation is the "don't even go to surgery" option. If you can build the margin up with composite so it's supragingival, the restorative dentist can work above the tissue line and nobody needs a scalpel. Ask yourself: does this case REALLY need surgery, or can I solve it conservatively?

ALTERED PASSIVE ERUPTION
ESSAY 1 QN
ALTERED (DELAYED) PASSIVE ERUPTION
A condition in which the gingival margin fails to migrate apically to its expected position at or near the CEJ during tooth eruption. The clinical crown appears SHORT even though the anatomic crown is of normal length. The tissue stays coronally — covering the tooth.

Normal eruption has two phases:

1. Active eruption — tooth moves occlusally through bone and tissue

2. Passive eruption — after active eruption stops, the gingival margin migrates apically from the enamel surface toward the CEJ, exposing the anatomic crown

In altered passive eruption: Passive eruption doesn't complete → gingival margin stays on the enamel → patient presents with:

• "Short" clinical crowns (but normal anatomic crowns under the tissue)

• Gummy smile — excessive gingival display

• Pseudopockets — deep sulcus probing depths but NO attachment loss (GM is coronal to CEJ)

How to differentiate from true pocketing:

1. Find the CEJ (probe to it)

2. Measure gingival margin position relative to CEJ

3. If GM is coronal to CEJ → pseudopocket → altered passive eruption

4. If GM is at or apical to CEJ with probing beyond CEJ → true pocket with attachment loss

5. CAL = PD - (distance GM is coronal to CEJ). In pseudopockets, CAL may be 0 despite deep PD.

Treatment: Determined by Coslet classification (what type of altered passive eruption is it).

The patient says "my teeth are too small." In reality, the teeth are normal — they're just hiding under excess tissue. It's like a turtle pulled into its shell. Your job is to decide HOW to uncover the teeth based on bone position and KT availability (Coslet types).

THE ULTIMATE GOAL IN PERIODONTICS
ESSAY 2 QN KEY To reestablish a periodontium to its original state of health.

Standard perio treatment (SRP, flaps, osseous surgery) almost always produces repair — long junctional epithelium, reduced PD, but NO connective tissue reinsertion into previously diseased cementum.

The progression:

Health (Uncompromised) → Disease → Health (Compromised) = what we usually achieve

Health (Uncompromised) → Disease → Health (Uncompromised) = the DREAM = requires new attachment

In order to obtain uncompromised health in a periodontally diseased dentition, new attachment must take place.

Three eras CLAIMED new attachment but couldn't prove it:

Era 1 (1890s-1950s): SRP — Younger 1899, McCall 1926, Orban 1948, Waerhaug 1952 → tissue tightening, not true new attachment

Era 2 (1957-1978): Flap procedures — Prichard 1957, Wade 1962, Rosling 1976, Polson & Heijl 1978

Era 3 (1965-1981): Osseous grafts — Nabers & O'Leary 1965, Schallhorn 1977, Dragoo 1981

Pattern follows Thomas Kuhn's "Structure of Scientific Revolutions" — shared beliefs → experiments → challenge → paradigm shift.

4 Ways to Assess New Attachment:

1. Periodontal probe — unreliable, tissue tightening mimics gain

2. Radiographs — shows bone, not CT attachment

3. Re-entry procedure — see bone directly, still can't confirm CT

4. Histologic evaluation = ONLY definitive method

Every generation of periodontists thought they'd found new attachment. Probes said yes. Radiographs said maybe. But histology — the only honest judge — kept saying no. Until GTR came along.

4 KEY TERMS — Know Cold for the Essay
ESSAY 2 QN KEY

These 4 terms define the spectrum from simple healing to complete regeneration. The essay WILL test these.

REATTACHMENT
Reconnecting surgically separated HEALTHY tissue. The tissue was never diseased — just cut during surgery and sutured back. Routine after any flap procedure at the edges beyond the defect. Not exciting — doesn't involve previously diseased root.
Like gluing a clean break in a plate — both sides are intact, you're just reconnecting them.
REPAIR
A junctional epithelial cell adhesion with a root surface that has been deprived of its periodontal ligament; preventing a connective tissue attachment. Long Junctional Epithelium (LJE) = the body's DEFAULT healing. Epithelium races down root and sticks with hemidesmosomes. No CT fibers inserting into cementum. Protective but NOT ideal — LJE is fragile and doesn't restore full function.
Like covering a wound with saran wrap — protects it, but it's not real skin. The body's "good enough" solution.
NEW ATTACHMENT
CT fibers inserting into PREVIOUSLY DISEASED root surface (cementum). THIS IS THE GOAL of GTR. Real functional connection between connective tissue and a root that used to be diseased. Distinguished from reattachment because the root surface was PREVIOUSLY exposed to disease — not surgically separated healthy tissue.
Like growing real new wood grain across a crack in a plank — structural, functional, real.
REGENERATION (Melcher 1969)
The biologic process by which the architecture and function of lost tissue is COMPLETELY returned. New cementum + new PDL fibers + new alveolar bone = the complete package. New attachment is PART of regeneration. Regeneration is the whole dream — everything lost is rebuilt, good as new.
New attachment is one piece of the puzzle. Regeneration is the completed puzzle — bone, PDL, cementum, all brand new. The periodontist's masterpiece.
LIT Melcher 1969: First defined "regeneration" in the periodontal context — the biologic process by which the architecture and function of lost tissue is completely returned.
Mnemonic: RE-RE-NE-RE

REattach (healthy reconnect) → REpair (LJE default) → NEw Attachment (CT into diseased cementum) → REgeneration (full restoration)

"Repair is the body's default. Regeneration is the dream. GTR overrides the default to achieve the dream."

THE 4 CELL TYPES — "THE RACE"
Whichever cell type arrives at the root surface FIRST determines the healing outcome
ESSAY 2 QN EXAM KEY

After periodontal surgery, 4 cell types compete to populate the root surface. The winner determines healing.

1. EPITHELIUM

FASTEST

Migrates down root surface rapidly via cell division at the leading edge.

Outcome: → Long Junctional Epithelium (LJE)

= REPAIR (body's default healing)

Actually protective — prevents gingival CT from causing worse damage (resorption). LJE adhesion seals the root from the oral environment. But it's NOT new attachment — no CT fiber insertion.

The sprinter who wins the race but gives you the consolation prize. Fastest, but only provides "good enough" healing.
2. GINGIVAL CT

FAST (second after epithelium)

Outcome: → ROOT RESORPTION

WORST OUTCOME. Gingival CT cells treat the root surface as a foreign body and resorb it. Catastrophic — irreversible loss of root structure.

Key insight: Gingival CT and PDL cells migrate at roughly the same rate but are embryologically DIFFERENT — produce completely different outcomes. Gingival CT = ectomesenchyme. PDL = dental follicle origin.

LIT Nyman 1980 (JCP 7:39): Roots implanted into gingival CT → root resorption. Proved gingival CT must be EXCLUDED from root surface.
3. PDL CELLS

MODERATE speed

Outcome: → NEW ATTACHMENT

THE GOAL. PDL cells are the ONLY cells capable of forming new cementum with inserting CT fibers on previously diseased root surface. They must migrate CORONALLY along the root from their position at the apical extent of the defect.

PDL cells are unique — derived from the dental follicle, they retain the ability to differentiate into cementoblasts, osteoblasts, and fibroblasts. They are the periodontium's stem cell reservoir.

LIT Nyman 1982 (JCP 9:257): Millipore filter placed → new cementum with inserting collagen fibers on curetted root surface. Proved PDL cells possess the ability to reestablish CT attachment. THE BREAKTHROUGH.
4. BONE CELLS

SLOWEST

Outcome: → ANKYLOSIS

Root fuses directly to bone with NO PDL space. Tooth becomes non-vital, can't respond to orthodontic forces, prone to replacement resorption. The tooth is essentially absorbed into the bone over time.

LIT Karring 1980 (JCP 7:96-105): Roots implanted into bone cavities → no new CT attachment, characterized by root resorption and ankylosis. Bone cells alone CANNOT produce new attachment.

Key insight: Epithelium winning by default is actually PROTECTIVE because it prevents the worse outcome of gingival CT causing root resorption. LJE seals the root — it's the body's "better safe than sorry" response. GTR overrides this default by physically blocking both epithelium AND gingival CT → gives PDL cells time to win instead.

"Every Girl Plays Ball" — Epithelium (fastest, repair) → Gingival CT (fast, resorption) → PDL (moderate, new attachment) → Bone (slowest, ankylosis). "The fastest runner gives the worst prize."

LANDMARK STUDIES — Karring, Nyman & Lindhe
ESSAY 2 QN LIT KEY

Know these names cold: Karring, Nyman, and Lindhe are primary authors on ALL landmark GTR studies.

STUDY 1: Karring, Nyman & Lindhe 1980

LIT J Clin Perio 7: 96-105, 1980

Title: "Healing Following Implantation of Periodontitis Affected Roots into Bone Tissue"

Method: 3 beagle dogs. 12 teeth subjected to induced periodontitis to half root length. Each root was notched (to mark the level), extracted, and implanted into bone cavities in such a way that epithelial migration was prevented. Only BONE CELLS could contact the diseased upper portion of the root.

Result: No new connective tissue attachment to root surfaces previously exposed to the oral environment. Healing characterized by root resorption and ankylosis.

Proved: Bone cells alone CANNOT produce new attachment on previously diseased root surface.

STUDY 2: Nyman et al. 1980

LIT J Clin Perio 7: 39, 1980

Title: "Healing Following Implantation of Periodontitis Affected Roots into Gingival Connective Tissue"

Method: 2 monkeys, 28 teeth subjected to experimental periodontal breakdown. Each root extracted and implanted in prepared grooves — one side facing bone, other side facing gingival CT of the repositioned flap.

Result: Side facing gingival CT → root resorption. Side facing bone → ankylosis.

Proved: Gingival connective tissue causes root resorption on contact with root surface. Must be EXCLUDED.

STUDY 3: Nyman, Karring & Lindhe 1982 — THE BREAKTHROUGH

LIT J Clin Perio 9: 257, 1982

Title: "The Regenerative Potential of the PDL"

Method: 3 monkeys. Following elevation of a mucoperiosteal flap, buccal and approximal alveolar bone was removed within an area extending from mid-root level to 2mm apical to marginal bone crest. Root surfaces were curetted. Prior to replacement of flap, a millipore filter was placed over the area — blocking epithelium + gingival CT. Only PDL cells and bone cells could reach the root. PDL faster than bone → PDL dominated.

Result: New cementum with inserting collagen fibers was observed on the curetted root surface. Controls (no filter) → LJE (repair only).

Proved: PDL cells possess the ability to reestablish CT attachment. The barrier concept WORKS.

Origin story: Nyman improvised mid-surgery — found a millipore filter in a chemistry lab drawer, cut it to fit the defect. The ENTIRE field of GTR started from rummaging through a drawer.

STUDY 4: Nyman et al. 1986

LIT J Clin Perio 13: 145, 1986

Title: "The Significance of Coronal Growth of PDL Tissue for New Attachment Formation"

Method: 4 monkeys. Angular defects produced to mid-root level around each tooth. PDL and root cementum removed to the bottom of the defect. In half the teeth, an elastic was placed TIGHTLY around the tooth at the bottom of the defect (blocking coronal PDL migration). In remaining teeth, elastic placed LOOSELY (allowing coronal migration). All roots covered by laterally displaced flaps.

Result: Tight elastic (blocked migration) → NO new attachment. Loose elastic (migration allowed) → new attachment formed.

Proved: PDL cells MUST migrate coronally along the root surface for new attachment to form. Directionality of migration is critical — PDL cells grow from apical to coronal.

Summary of the 4 studies:

Karring 1980: Bone alone → resorption/ankylosis (bone cells can't do it)

Nyman 1980: Gingival CT → root resorption (CT must be excluded)

Nyman 1982: Millipore filter → new attachment PROVED (PDL cells win with barrier)

Nyman 1986: Coronal PDL migration → necessary (block it and nothing happens)

GTR — DEFINITION
ESSAY 2 QN KEY
GUIDED TISSUE REGENERATION (GTR)
Allowing certain cell types to participate in the healing process while EXCLUDING other types, for the purpose of promoting new attachment. A physical barrier membrane is placed to BLOCK the fast cells (epithelium + gingival CT) → gives PDL cells time to migrate coronally along the root surface → new cementum + new PDL fibers + new alveolar bone = REGENERATION.

The logic chain:

1. We know epithelium is fastest → produces only repair (LJE)

2. We know gingival CT causes root resorption → must be excluded

3. We know PDL cells produce new attachment → but they're slower

4. We know bone cells cause ankylosis → also must be excluded (but they're slowest, so not usually the problem)

5. Solution: place a physical barrier that blocks epithelium and gingival CT → PDL cells win the race → new attachment

What GTR achieves when successful:

• New cementum deposited on previously diseased root surface

• New CT (Sharpey's) fibers inserting into that new cementum

• New alveolar bone forming to support the regenerated attachment

• Functional PDL space between root and bone

Clinical pearl — "tissue bubbling up": At membrane removal (4-6 weeks), you'll see tissue that has grown underneath the membrane, coronal to the outer flap. This tissue can ONLY be PDL-origin because: membrane blocked epithelium and gingival CT; bone cells are too slow for 4-6 weeks; only PDL cells could have produced this tissue in this timeframe. This is visual confirmation that GTR worked.

Goldman Dry Ice Case — The Clinical "Aha" Moment:

18-year-old patient, dry ice stuck to oral tissue → ripped off soft tissue → exposed vital bone on facial surface. Goldman did nothing (secondary intention healing). At 1 week: healing was NOT uniform — most robust tissue formation concentrated at the level of the PDL. Rest of bone surface healed slower/differently. This observation = birth of GTR concept: different cell types produce different healing at different speeds. Can we CONTROL which cells participate?

GTR SURGICAL TECHNIQUE — 8 Steps
ESSAY 2 QN KEY

8-Step GTR procedure (directly from slides):

Step 1: INITIAL INCISIONS

• Intrasulcular incisions — NOT internal bevel (preserve MAXIMUM tissue and ALL keratinized tissue)

• Generous vertical releasing incision mesial to defect — spaced well away from the defect site

• Retain the interdental papilla — do NOT excise it

• Excise all pocket epithelium from the inner surface of the flap

Step 2: FLAP REFLECTION

• Full thickness mucoperiosteal flap — reflect PAST the defect

• Must expose bone beyond defect margins (membrane needs 2-3mm overhang onto healthy bone on all sides)

Step 3: DEBRIDEMENT

• Remove ALL granulation tissue from the defect

• Thoroughly plane all root surfaces to smooth, glassy finish

• Use thin tapered diamonds where hand instruments can't reach (accessability limited areas)

Step 4: MEMBRANE SHAPING

• Cut to size by trimming the apical and lateral borders ONLY

• Preserve the open microstructure (coronal portion)

• Must cover the ENTIRE defect and extend 2-3mm apically beyond the defect onto healthy bone

Step 5: SUTURE MEMBRANE TO TOOTH

• Suture material directly around the tooth using Gore-Tex suture or Dexon suture

• Suture tension should pull the membrane snug against the tooth

• Do NOT cause edges to curl — curling leads to perforation through the flap

• This is the FIRST round of suturing

Step 6: FLAP CLOSURE

• Second round of suturing — close the flap over the membrane

• Membrane must be COMPLETELY covered by the flap

• Material should be 2-3mm below the coronal aspect of the flap

• Often requires coronally advancing ("hiking") the flap to achieve complete coverage

• Suture interproximal closures FIRST, then vertical releasing incisions LAST

Step 7: POST-OPERATIVE CARE

• CHX rinse daily — meticulous oral hygiene without brushing the surgical site

• Follow up at 2 weeks and 6 weeks

• Monitor for membrane exposure — if exposed, trim the exposed portion, apply CHX, monitor

• Membrane stays in place 4-6 weeks (clinical trials show up to 3 months if fully covered, but sufficient healing occurs by 4-6 weeks)

Step 8: MEMBRANE REMOVAL

• Anesthetize the area

• Occlusive (bottom) portion: remove with gentle tug

• Open microstructure (top) portion: if tissue has grown into it, may need a small incision for access dissection

• If membrane cannot be removed with gentle tug → small incision to dissect

• Observe the tissue underneath — "bubbling up" tissue = PDL-origin = GTR success

American vs Swedish approach:

Swedes (Karring, Nyman) were purists — bone against root = bad per their studies → no graft material, barrier only.

Americans combined bone graft WITH membrane. Works clinically because freeze-dried bone is NOT active living bone — it's a slow scaffold. PDL cells establish attachment on root surface BEFORE graft particles can cause problems.

Fleisher's approach: Give it everything on the first attempt — graft + membrane + GEM 21. "I don't know that I need it all, but I don't want to find out after the fact that I should have." Going back around a tooth = destroys some new attachment gained (second flap disrupts healing).

GTR vs GBR — The Key Conceptual Difference
ESSAY 2 QN KEY

The major conceptual difference = WHERE, not WHAT materials you use.

GTR — Around TEETH

Goal: New attachment on root surface (new cementum + inserting CT fibers)

Key cells: PDL cells — the ONLY cells that can form new cementum with inserting fibers

Membrane duration: ~4-6 weeks (sufficient for PDL cells to do their work)

Success metric: PD reduction to 2-4mm, clinical attachment gain, radiographic bone fill

Best defect type: 3-wall (most contained, best blood supply from multiple directions)

→ 3-Wall Defects
GBR — EDENTULOUS Areas

Goal: Bone VOLUME for implant placement (no root = no attachment needed)

Key cells: Osteoblasts ONLY — no root surface → no PDL → no new attachment concept

Membrane duration: Up to 6 MONTHS — bone formation is SLOW without PDL to speed things along

Success metric: Adequate bone height/width for implant placement

Indications: Immediate implant, socket preservation, ridge augmentation, sinus lift

Why GBR membranes stay longer: In GTR, PDL cells (moderate speed) produce results in 4-6 weeks. In GBR, there ARE no PDL cells (edentulous site) — only bone cells (the SLOWEST cell type). Need significantly more time.

Can a single surgery be BOTH? Yes — e.g., defect on distal of canine (GTR) + adjacent edentulous premolar socket (GBR) = both happening simultaneously with the same membrane.

"GTR = Got Teeth Remaining. GBR = Got Bone to Rebuild (no teeth)." Same materials, different goals, different biology, different timing. Classification depends on WHERE not WHAT.

• Biocompatible • Occlusive • Spacemaking • Tissue integration • Clinical manageability

Primary closure is CRITICAL for GBR — even more than GTR. Longer healing time = more exposure risk. Area of membrane exposure = area without bone formation.

GBR — CONCEPT & INDICATIONS
ESSAY 2 QN
GUIDED BONE REGENERATION (GBR)
The use of barrier membranes (with or without graft materials) to promote new bone formation in edentulous areas. A membrane excludes soft tissue cells from the healing site, creating a protected space where osteoblasts can form new bone without competition from faster-growing soft tissue cells.

GBR Indications:

1. Immediate implant placement — bone defect around freshly placed implant (dehiscence/fenestration)

2. Extraction socket preservation — prevent ridge collapse after tooth extraction

3. Ridge augmentation procedures — build up bone volume on deficient edentulous ridge

4. Sinus lift procedures — create bone height in posterior maxilla for implant placement

Fleisher's philosophy on GBR:

• Going back into an edentulous area (GBR) = no attachment to lose → readily does second procedures if needed

• Unlike GTR (where second surgery destroys some gained attachment), GBR sites are forgiving

• Primary closure is CRITICAL — exposure during the long healing period = no bone at that site

• Hard tissue (bone) > soft tissue — bone doesn't shrink, CT grafts can shrink. Rebuild with bone first; CT graft only if needed for buccal contour

Antibiotics for GBR: Yes — active infection risk from long healing period warrants prophylactic coverage.

GRAFT MATERIALS — Complete Classification
ESSAY 2 QN KEY

Source Classification — THE BIG FOUR:

GRAFT SOURCE DEFINITIONS
Autograft: Tissue transferred from one position to another within the SAME individual.
Allograft: Tissue transferred from one individual to another genetically dissimilar individual of the SAME species (human → human).
Xenograft: Tissue transferred from one species to ANOTHER species (bovine → human).
Alloplast: A synthetic graft or inert foreign body implanted into tissue.
OSTEOCONDUCTIVE
= PASSIVE scaffold. Facilitates new bone formation from EXISTING bone — essentially acting as a scaffold. Like a trellis for a vine. Most grafts are at least this. Test: does bone grow on it when placed next to existing bone? All grafts pass this test.
OSTEOINDUCTIVE
= ACTIVELY recruits + transforms undifferentiated mesenchymal stem cells INTO osteoblasts. Can induce new bone from cells OTHER than osteocytes. Way more powerful. Test: muscle pouch experiment — implant material into muscle (no bone nearby). If bone STILL forms = osteoinductive. Only autograft reliably passes. DFDBA theoretically but unreliably.
OSTEOGENIC
= Contains LIVING bone cells (osteoblasts, osteocytes, progenitor cells) that can directly form new bone. ONLY autografts (vital bone) have this property. Cannot be replicated synthetically.
1. AUTOGRAFT — Gold Standard

Same patient. ONLY graft with ALL 3 properties: osteogenic + osteoinductive + osteoconductive.

Intraoral sources (vital):

• Osseous coagulum — bone chips collected during surgery

• Tuberosity — cancellous bone with hematopoietic marrow → best osteogenic potential

• Recent extraction sites — may contain PDL cells, adding regenerative potential

• Vital cancellous bone contains hematopoietic marrow → better osteogenic potential than cortical bone

• Intra-oral vital sites do NOT cause the same resorptive problems as iliac crest

Extraoral source: Iliac crest — more volume but:

• Second surgical site (general anesthesia, hospitalization)

• Root resorption complication (too bioactive — living bone cells attack root surface)

• Freezing the harvested bone controls the resorption problem

• Rarely used in perio now — too invasive

Osteoid bone harvest trick: Extract tooth → let socket partially heal 2 weeks → harvest osteoid (newly forming) bone loaded with active bone-forming cells. Strategic extraction timing creates a harvest site.

Advantages: No rejection risk, all 3 biologic properties, no disease transmission

Disadvantages: Second surgical site, limited volume intraorally, morbidity from iliac harvest

LIT Schallhorn et al.: Showed effectiveness of iliac bone and marrow autografts. Complications included root resorption from iliac crest grafts — controlled by freezing harvested bone. Intra-oral vital grafts have varying success; cancellous > cortical for osteogenic potential.
2. ALLOGRAFT — Cadaver Bone (Same Species)

FDBA (Mineralized Freeze Dried Bone Allograft):

• Osteoconductive ONLY — scaffold function. Minerals intact, blocking BMP access

• Reliable, commonly used scaffold material

DFDBA (Demineralized Freeze Dried Bone Allograft):

• THEORETICALLY osteoinductive — demineralization process exposes BMPs (per Urist)

• BUT UNRELIABLE — batch-to-batch and bank-to-bank variability is severe

Advantages: No second surgical site, off-the-shelf availability, unlimited supply from tissue banks

Disadvantages: Variable osteoinductivity, no osteogenic cells (non-vital), disease transmission risk (small but present)

LIT Urist 1965 (Science): Made the key discovery that demineralized, lyophilized segments of bone induced new bone formation when implanted in muscle pouches in rabbits. Proposed the name "Bone Morphogenetic Protein" (J Dent Res, 1971). Demineralization of cortical allografts exposes BMP, an inductive factor known to increase bone formation.
LIT Schwartz et al. 1996: Examined 14 batches of commercially available DFDBA from 6 bone banks. Bone from 3 of 6 banks showed NO signs of osteoinductive potential. Even different lots from the SAME bank had different levels of osteoinductivity. DFDBA osteoinductivity is a gamble.

Is cancellous or cortical bone more osteoinductive? Cancellous (marrow) bone has greater osteogenic potential because it contains hematopoietic marrow with progenitor cells. Cortical grafts have less osteogenic potential.

3. XENOGRAFT — Different Species

Bio-Oss = anorganic bovine bone. The standard xenograft in periodontics.

• Osteoconductive ONLY (scaffold — no cells, no induction)

• Excellent for sinus lifts — consistent quality, abundant supply, excellent scaffold properties

• Dense vital bone forms around and through the scaffold particles

Bio-Oss Processing (know this):

• Treated at high temperatures for more than 15 hours

• Followed by several hours of cleaning with strong alkaline solutions

• Result: highly purified anorganic natural bone mineral

• Prion-free tissue — bone is classified by experts as prion-free (unlike brain, intestine, or spinal cord from cattle)

• Human and bovine bone mineral have similar microstructure

Advantages: Consistent quality, abundant supply, excellent scaffold, no second surgical site, very slow resorption (stays as scaffold long-term)

Disadvantages: Osteoconductive only, another species (some patients object), no osteoinductive potential

4. ALLOPLAST — Synthetic Materials

• Hydroxyapatite (HA)

• Tricalcium phosphate (TCP)

• Bioactive glass polymers

• Calcium sulfate (plaster of Paris)

• Calcium ceramics

All are osteoconductive ONLY. Inert biologic fillers.

"Unlikely to result in true regeneration" — directly from the slides.

Bioactive glass is NOT actually bioactive despite the name.

Advantages: No disease transmission risk, unlimited supply, consistent quality, no second surgical site

Disadvantages: Just a scaffold — no biologic activity beyond space maintenance. Cannot produce new attachment or induce bone formation.

Fleisher's practical philosophy:

• Membrane without graft? Theoretically yes (blood clot + bone cells), but graft maintains membrane position, stabilizes clot, provides scaffold. "Why not use it?"

• Fleisher's rule on extraction: "If you don't have the materials to regenerate this area, you have NO BUSINESS taking this tooth out."

• Always use Fleisher-brand FDBA (shows on slide — his name literally on the bone bank packaging)

BARRIER MEMBRANES — Types & Properties
ESSAY 2 QN KEY

Ideal Barrier Material Properties (directly from slides):

1. Ability to withstand cellular ingrowth (block unwanted cells)

2. Nutrient and fluid diffusible (allow blood supply and nutrients to reach healing tissue)

3. Excellent handling characteristics (surgeon can trim, shape, suture)

4. Minimal tissue reaction (biocompatible)

5. Absence of allergic properties

6. Resistance to infection

7. Eventual absorption after tissue repair has reached satisfactory level

Gore-Tex meets ALL EXCEPT #7 — it's non-resorbable, requiring second surgery for removal.

NON-RESORBABLE: GORE-TEX (ePTFE)

Material: PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) = medical grade stretched Teflon. Inert — previously used in vascular surgery patches and artificial ligaments. No longer made by Gore for dental; current brand = Cytoplast.

Two Structural Zones:

1. Top (coronal): Open microstructure — stretched, porous. Designed to let flap tissue grow into it (prevent recession). Trim from sides/bottom, PRESERVE this portion.

2. Bottom (body): Occlusive membrane — dense, no cell penetration. Does the actual GTR work of blocking epithelium + gingival CT.

Configurations available:

• Single tooth narrow / Single tooth wide / Wraparound / Interproximal

• Titanium-reinforced versions — for space maintenance (membrane holds its shape, doesn't collapse into defect)

• Transgingival and submerged configurations

Advantages:

• Gold standard historically — total control over timing

• Predictable barrier function — stays until YOU remove it

• Well-studied, decades of clinical data

• Space-making (especially titanium-reinforced)

Disadvantages:

• REQUIRES SECOND SURGERY for removal

• Exposure risk → bacterial contamination → infection

• If exposed → must trim or remove early

• Cost, patient morbidity from two surgeries

Duration: Clinical trials show barrier may remain up to 3 months. However, clinical observations indicate sufficient healing occurs between 4-6 weeks. If material cannot be removed with gentle tug → small incision for access dissection.

RESORBABLE MEMBRANES

Types: Collagen membranes (porcine/bovine derived), polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA).

Advantages:

• NO second surgery — membrane dissolves on its own

• Simpler for patient — one procedure

• Lower complication rate (no retrieval surgery)

Disadvantages:

• Unpredictable resorption rate — may dissolve too fast before regeneration is complete

• May not maintain space as well as ePTFE (less rigid)

• No control over timing of barrier function

Use cases:

• GBR around implants (collagen membrane + particulate graft + tacks for stabilization)

• Socket preservation

• Situations where second surgery is undesirable

Key point: Resorbable membranes' ONLY real advantage over non-resorbable = no second surgery. For everything else, non-resorbable is superior.

GBR-specific membrane requirements (from slides): Biocompatible, Occlusive, Spacemaking, Tissue integration, Clinical manageability. Stabilized with tacks (not just sutures) for GBR applications.

BIOLOGICS — Regenerative Enhancement Agents
ESSAY 2 QN KEY

Four regenerative enhancers to compare for the essay:

1. GEM 21 (rhPDGF-BB + beta-TCP) — Fleisher's Favorite

What: Completely synthetic grafting system. Combines recombinant human platelet-derived growth factor (rhPDGF-BB) with an osteoconductive matrix (beta tricalcium phosphate, beta-TCP).

Mechanism (4 steps from slides):

1. After implantation, PDGF is released from beta-TCP matrix into surrounding environment

2. PDGF binds to specific cell surface receptors on target cells, initiating a cascade of intracellular signaling pathways

3. PDGF-induced events lead to chemotaxis (directed cell migration) and mitogenesis (cell proliferation) of osteoblasts, periodontal ligament fibroblasts, and cementoblasts

4. Proliferation of these cells leads to increased matrix synthesis, resulting in formation of new alveolar bone, new periodontal ligament, and new cementum. Angiogenesis (blood vessel formation) continues.

Results: Consistent, excellent. ALL of Fleisher's impressive GTR cases used GEM 21. Minimal complications.

Key advantage over PRP: Delivers a SPECIFIC, purified, genetically engineered growth factor at known concentration — targeted + reliable. PRP just concentrates whatever's in your platelets.

GEM 21 is like sending out a GPS signal that tells stem cells "come HERE and turn into bone/PDL/cementum cells." Then gives them the scaffolding to build on.
2. EMD — Emdogain (Enamel Matrix Derivative)

Source: Harvested from porcine (pig) tooth buds.

Contains: Mixture of low molecular weight proteins: amelogenins, ameloblastin, and enamelin.

Mechanism: In vitro studies indicate EMD may influence cellular activity — PDL cells exhibit enhanced protein production and cell proliferation. May mimic developmental cementogenesis (enamel matrix proteins play a role in natural cementum formation during tooth development).

Fleisher's take: Skeptical mechanistically ("squirting gel from pig tooth buds?") but accepts the evidence: "the research shows it works pretty well."

EMD tricks the PDL cells into thinking they're back in embryonic development — "Hey, you're a tooth bud again, start building attachment apparatus!"
3. PRP — Platelet Rich Plasma

How: Draw patient's blood → centrifuge → concentrate platelets at bottom → pipette out concentrate.

Growth factors contained: PDGF, TGF-beta, EGF/TGF-alpha, IGF-1 (plasma). Concentrates platelets 330-900% above baseline.

Theory: Combine PRP gel with bone graft → local concentration of cell signaling molecules increased within wound → improved bone formation and faster healing. Bone mineral also serves to bind PDGF and localize it to wound.

Reality: Research DIDN'T bear it out. No increase in total bone formation. Faster early healing + better soft tissue closure, but NOT more bone. Logistics (phlebotomist, centrifuge, cost) may not justify marginal benefit.

Still useful as: Wound healing agent — excellent at maintaining flap closure, soft tissue management.

PRF (Platelet Rich Fibrin): Newer variation, supposedly more bioactive. "Sticky bone" = graft mixed with PRF.

PRP makes the wound heal faster and prettier, but doesn't actually give you MORE bone. Good supplementary, not a game-changer for regeneration.
4. BMP — Bone Morphogenic Proteins (Infuse/rhBMP-2)

What: A group of regulatory glycoproteins that stimulate differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts. Research focused on BMP-2, BMP-3 (osteogenin), and BMP-7 (OP1).

Discovery: Urist made the key discovery. Demineralized, lyophilized segments of bone induced new bone formation when implanted in muscle pouches in rabbits. Published in Science, 1965. Proposed the name "Bone Morphogenetic Protein" in J Dent Res, 1971.

Results: Most potent osteoinductive agent available. Dramatic ridge augmentation results.

BUT: Risk of "exuberant inflammatory response" — patient swells MASSIVELY ("blows up like a balloon"). Expensive. Dose-sensitive (too much = complications).

Fleisher sent his free Infuse samples BACK unused — GEM 21 already working great without the risks. "If what you're using works well, there's not much incentive to jump to a new product." Don't chase new products — need 10-15 years of data, not 5-6 months.

BMP is the nuclear option — most powerful but most side effects. Like using a sledgehammer when you needed a finish hammer.

GTR/GBR COMPLICATIONS — With Remedies
ESSAY 2 QN KEY
1. INFECTION

Cause: Bacterial contamination of membrane and/or graft material. Most common with membrane exposure.

Remedy:

• Antibiotics (first-line treatment)

• May need premature membrane removal if infection doesn't resolve

• Debridement of infected tissue

• Prevention: CHX rinse protocol, meticulous aseptic technique, primary closure

2. MEMBRANE PERFORATION / EXPOSURE

Cause: Sutures too tight → membrane edges curl → poke through flap. Also: thin tissue biotype, patient trauma to area.

Remedy:

• Trim exposed portion of membrane

• CHX rinse on exposed area

• Monitor closely — healing underneath can still be excellent

• If severe exposure → remove membrane early

• Prevention: Don't over-tighten membrane sutures. Ensure adequate flap coverage (2-3mm coronal overlap).

GBR note: Membrane exposure in GBR = MORE problematic than GTR. Area of exposure = area WITHOUT bone formation.

3. RECESSION / FLAP SLOUGHING

Cause: Inadequate blood supply to flap, tension on sutures, thin tissue biotype, patient manipulation of area.

Remedy:

• Ensure tension-free flap closure at time of surgery (releasing incisions past MGJ)

• If recession occurred → may need soft tissue grafting later (CTG or FGG)

• Note: Even with recession, healing underneath membrane can still be excellent — PDL cells still win the race under the protected space

4. GINGIVAL IRREGULARITIES

Cause: Tissue level disparity between outer flap and tissue that grew under the membrane. The regenerated tissue doesn't match the surrounding tissue architecture.

Remedy:

• May resolve over time as tissues mature

• Can be addressed at membrane uncovery (removal appointment)

• Gingivoplasty after healing complete to re-establish physiologic contours

Trauma from Occlusion — Definition & Diagnosis

The injury to the attachment apparatus — not the force itself. ESSAY 4 core concept. → Bone Homeostasis

ESSAY 4 Q1 What is trauma from occlusion?
Define using AAP 2001 glossary. Distinguish the INJURY (lesion in attachment apparatus) from the FORCE. Emphasize the diagnostic criterion of increasing mobility over time. Distinguish from pathologic tooth wear.
Cite: AAP Glossary of Periodontal Terms 2001 — official definition
DEFINITION — AAP 2001
Occlusal Traumatism (AAP Glossary 2001)
A term used to describe pathologic or adaptive changes which develop in the periodontium as a result of undue force produced by the masticatory muscles.
Key understanding: TFO = the INJURY to the attachment apparatus caused by excessive force. It is NOT the force itself — it is the damage the force causes. Any force applied to a tooth has the potential to produce a pathologic effect on the attachment apparatus.

The pathologic lesion: Causes loss of alveolar and crestal lamina dura → widening of the PDL → appears clinically as increasing mobility over time.

Overloading the system (too much force) can result in breakdown of one or more components:
Temporomandibular Joint (TMJ dysfunction)
Tooth (pathologic wear)
Attachment Apparatus (TFO)
A competent system is entirely asymptomatic; an incompetent system displays symptoms: TMJ dysfunction, pathologic changes in tooth morphology, and/or clinical/radiographic signs of occlusal trauma.
Think of TFO as a sprained ankle — the sprain is the injury, not the misstep that caused it. We diagnose the injury, not the force.
CRITICAL DIAGNOSTIC CRITERION
You must have INCREASING mobility over time to diagnose occlusal trauma.

The following alone are NOT sufficient to diagnose TFO:
  • Cracked teeth
  • Fractures
  • Worn teeth / abrasion
  • Widened PDL on radiograph alone
Mobility must be documented as INCREASING across appointments — a single measurement is not enough. You need serial measurements showing progression.

Mobility grading reference:
Physiologic: ≤0.2mm | Grade 1: 0.2–1.0mm | Grade 2: 1.0–2.0mm | Grade 3: >2.0mm and/or vertical displacement
Mobility measurement notation: +, 1, 1+, 2, 2+, 3
WEAR ≠ TFO
Pathologic tooth wear is NOT the same as occlusal trauma.
Each tooth has a threshold of force, beyond which an occlusal traumatic lesion develops in the attachment apparatus. In some cases, this threshold may be quite high yet may exceed the tooth's ability to resist wear.

Key distinction: Occlusal and incisal wear may occur, but mobility may or may NOT be present. The extent of dental wear may not be directly related to the extent of the lesion of occlusal trauma in the attachment apparatus.

A patient can have:
  • Extensive wear + no mobility = wear only, NOT TFO
  • Minimal wear + increasing mobility = TFO without significant wear
  • Both wear + increasing mobility = both pathologies present
A bruxer can grind teeth flat without ever developing TFO — the force threshold for wear is different from the threshold for attachment apparatus injury.
PDL Width Reference (Coolidge 1930):
Age 11–16: Crest 0.23mm, Mid-root 0.17mm, Apical 0.24mm
Age 32–50: Crest 0.20mm, Mid-root 0.14mm, Apical 0.19mm
Age 51–67: Crest 0.17mm, Mid-root 0.12mm, Apical 0.16mm
PDL narrows with age → same force may produce different effects at different ages.

Light Force vs Heavy Force

Adaptive vs non-adaptive response of the periodontium to occlusal loading. ESSAY 4

LIGHT FORCE — ADAPTIVE RANGE
Definition: Force is constant in intensity but within the adaptive range of the surrounding periodontium.

Sequence of events:
  1. Force applied → occlusal traumatic lesion develops in attachment apparatus
  2. Inflammatory response in attachment apparatus begins to resolve over time as the wound heals
  3. A permanent widening of the PDL space is established to compensate for the force
  4. Healing occurs, but the PDL remains widened
  5. Clinically: tooth demonstrates increased mobility because PDL has widened
The critical point: So long as the intensity of the force does not change → PDL remains widened but healed → mobility remains increased but is NOT increasing.

If force is REMOVED:
  • PDL returns to original width prior to force application
  • Bone apposition at the alveolar lamina dura occurs as force is removed
  • Mobility returns to pre-force baseline
  • Completely reversible
Clinical trajectory: Increasing mobility as force applied → plateaus → stable increased mobility → returns to baseline if force removed.
Like a rubber band stretching — it adapts to the load, stays stretched while loaded, but returns to original shape when released. The system found a new equilibrium.
HEAVY FORCE — EXCEEDS ADAPTIVE CAPACITY
Definition: Force exceeds the adaptive capacity of the attachment apparatus.

What happens:
  • Heavier the force → larger the resultant lesion → wider the PDL → greater the degree of mobility
  • The tooth can become increasingly mobile and never adapt
  • Mobility will continue to be increasing — no plateau, no healing
  • PDL keeps widening — the wound never resolves
This is pathologic — requires intervention.

Primary Occlusal Trauma

ESSAY 4 Q2/Q4 EXCESSIVE force on NORMAL periodontium. Reversible. Tx: equilibration. → Osseous

ESSAY 4 Q2 How do you diagnose primary TFO in a clinical setting?
Excessive force + normal support. Look for: increasing mobility, migration, fremitus. Radiographic: widened PDL, possible angular bone loss appearance. Lesion is reversible.
Cite: Reinhart, Pao, Krejeci 1984 — PDL stresses in initiation of occlusal traumatism
PRIMARY OCCLUSAL TRAUMA
Primary Occlusal Trauma
Lesion of the attachment apparatus caused by EXCESSIVE forces applied to the periodontium that has a NORMAL amount of support.
Key characteristics:
  • The bone and attachment are healthy — the force is just too much
  • Lesion is REVERSIBLE and REPAIRABLE
  • Caused by excessive force, NOT by loss of support
Clinical presentation:
  • Increasing mobility over time (documented across appointments)
  • Migration of teeth (drifting)
  • Fremitus (vibration during function)
Radiographic findings:
  • Widening of the PDL space
  • Possibly the appearance of angular bone loss
  • DO NOT confuse this with radiographic bone changes related to loss of attachment from marginal plaque-related disease (periodontitis)
Treatment: Focused on reduction of force to a level acceptable to the attachment apparatus.
  • Excessive force often can be reduced by altering the occlusion (occlusal equilibration / selective grinding)
  • Once forces are reduced to acceptable level → lesion resolves → PDL width returns to pre-force levels → mobility is reduced
Can you have primary TFO on a tooth WITH attachment loss? YES. If the force on that tooth is also excessive relative to its (reduced but stable) support, the excessive component is primary TFO. The conditions can coexist.

ESSAY 4 Q2 How do you diagnose secondary TFO?
Normal force + weakened support from periodontal disease. Must have attachment loss by definition. Usually not reversible. Treatment is splinting, not equilibration.

  • Caused by loss of adequate bone support rather than excessive occlusal force

Does TFO Cause Attachment Loss?

ESSAY 4 Q7 The most important conceptual question. Answer: NO. → Plaque-Induced Disease

ESSAY 4 Q7 Does trauma from occlusion cause attachment loss? Please explain.
NO. TFO affects the attachment APPARATUS (PDL + bone below the crest), NOT the supracrestal soft tissues (JE + CT attachment). Therefore TFO cannot initiate pocketing or recession. It does not initiate periodontal disease.
This is the conceptual core of Essay 4. Get this right and everything else follows.
TFO DOES NOT CAUSE ATTACHMENT LOSS
The definitive answer: NO.

Soft Tissue Changes — the key concept:
The effects of occlusal force on the periodontium are limited to the attachment apparatus (cementum, PDL, alveolar bone). Occlusal force does NOT affect the supracrestal soft tissues of the periodontium.

What this means: The gingival soft tissues coronal to bone — connective tissue attachment and junctional epithelium — are NOT damaged by excessive force on a tooth.

Therefore:
  • Occlusal trauma does NOT cause (initiate) attachment loss
  • Occlusal trauma does NOT cause pocketing
  • Occlusal trauma does NOT cause recession
  • TFO does NOT initiate periodontal disease
Why this distinction matters
Attachment apparatus (what TFO affects) = cementum + PDL fibers + alveolar bone — all BELOW the bone crest.
Attachment (what TFO does NOT affect) = the connective tissue attachment + junctional epithelium — the supracrestal soft tissue connection between tooth and gingiva.

TFO can widen PDL, cause bone remodeling, and increase mobility — but it CANNOT break the seal between the JE/CT attachment and the tooth surface. Only bacterial plaque-induced inflammation can do that.
TFO is like an earthquake shaking the foundation of a house (attachment apparatus) but leaving the walls and roof (supracrestal soft tissues) intact. The house sways, the foundation cracks, but the structure above ground is untouched.

Mobility vs Fremitus

ESSAY 4 Q6 Two different tests measuring two different things. Know the distinction.

ESSAY 4 Q6 Distinguish between mobility and fremitus.
Mobility = passive test with instruments (side-to-side). Fremitus = functional test with finger (vibration during occlusion). A tooth can have one without the other.
Both findings inform TFO diagnosis but measure different aspects of the problem.
MOBILITY vs FREMITUS
MOBILITY
What it is: Side-to-side movement of the tooth when pushed with instruments (typically two instrument handles, or instrument + finger).

What it tests: The width/integrity of the PDL. Wider PDL = more mobility.

How to measure: Apply lateral force with instrument handles on facial and lingual surfaces. Assess degree of horizontal displacement.

Grading (Miller Classification):
  • Physiologic: ≤0.2mm (normal)
  • Grade 1: 0.2–1.0mm horizontal
  • Grade 2: 1.0–2.0mm horizontal
  • Grade 3: >2.0mm horizontal AND/OR vertical displacement (depressibility)
Clinical notation: +, 1, 1+, 2, 2+, 3

A mobile tooth may NOT have fremitus (wide PDL but no occlusal interference — the tooth moves freely but isn't being hit during function).
FREMITUS
What it is: Vibration or movement of a tooth detected by placing a finger on the facial surface while the patient taps their teeth together in function.

What it tests: Whether the tooth is receiving excessive force DURING occlusal function. It detects occlusal prematurity or interference.

How to measure: Place fingertip on facial surface of tooth. Ask patient to tap teeth together repeatedly (centric closure) and then do lateral excursions. Feel for vibration/movement.

Significance: Fremitus indicates the tooth is being hit prematurely or excessively during function — a potential cause of TFO.

A tooth can have fremitus WITHOUT significant mobility (occlusal prematurity on a stable tooth with normal PDL — the tooth is being hit hard but hasn't adapted/injured yet).
Summary of the distinction:
Mobility = passive assessment of PDL width (the RESULT of TFO or adaptation).
Fremitus = functional assessment of occlusal forces (a potential CAUSE of TFO).

Both inform the TFO diagnosis:
  • Mobility alone doesn't tell you WHY — could be TFO, could be severe bone loss
  • Fremitus helps identify WHICH teeth are receiving excessive forces
  • Primary TFO clinically presents with: increasing mobility + migration + fremitus

TFO + Active Infection = Co-Destruction

ESSAY 4 Q3 The Glickman concept. TFO alone doesn't cause perio destruction, but TFO + active infection is synergistic. → Plaque-Induced

ESSAY 4 Q3 Describe the relationship of periodontal breakdown from periodontal disease with and without occlusal trauma.
TFO alone = no attachment loss. Perio disease alone = attachment loss. TFO + active infection = CO-DESTRUCTION (Glickman). But healthy reduced periodontium + TFO = normal response (no additional breakdown). Critical for ortho planning.
Cite: Glickman — Zone of Irritation vs Zone of Co-Destruction (unproven theory)
CO-DESTRUCTION CONCEPT
A relatively HEAVY force combined with an ACTIVE periodontal infection can be co-destructive in nature — exacerbating the destructive rate of the periodontal infection.

Research has indicated that a relatively heavy degree of force is needed to affect a co-destructive pattern in which the rate of periodontal breakdown increases due to the occlusal force component.

Clinically: This heavy force is often related to orthodontic treatment context — important consideration when doing ortho on perio patients.
Glickman's Concept (Zones)
Zone of Irritation: Supracrestal soft tissues affected by plaque → inflammation, pocket formation, attachment loss.
Zone of Co-Destruction: When TFO combines with active infection, the pattern of bone destruction is altered — may drive bone loss in different patterns (angular/vertical defects).
Note: This theory remains unproven but is the framework for understanding co-destruction.
THE CRUCIAL EXCEPTION — Healthy Reduced Periodontium
Animal studies have shown: Occlusal trauma induced in a healthy but reduced periodontium (a successfully treated periodontal case) has NO different effect on the periodontium than occlusal trauma induced on a healthy periodontium with normal bone and attachment levels.

Clinical implication: Patients with bone loss but no significant marginal inflammation or probing depth after definitive periodontal therapy respond to orthodontics in a manner similar to that of patients without bone loss or marginal inflammation.

The takeaway for ortho planning:
Active perio + TFO
= Co-destruction
= Accelerated breakdown
DO NOT do ortho
Treated (stable) perio + TFO
= Normal adaptive response
= No additional breakdown
CAN do ortho safely
TFO is only co-destructive when combined with ACTIVE infection. Once you treat the infection → the reduced periodontium is stable → TFO alone won't cause additional breakdown. Treat the perio first, THEN you can do ortho.
Four scenarios — master this grid:
1. Occlusal Trauma Alone
No additional attachment loss.
Primary or secondary type.
Treat the trauma directly.
2. Periodontal Disease Alone
Attachment loss occurs.
Treat the infection.
No occlusal intervention needed.
3. Periodontal Disease + TFO
Additional attachment loss — co-destruction.
Rate of breakdown increased.
Must treat BOTH.
4. Healthy Reduced Perio + TFO
No additional attachment loss.
Normal adaptive response.
Ortho is safe.

Treatment of Occlusal Trauma

ESSAY 4 Q4 Three-step approach: eliminate inflammation, control occlusal etiology, establish stable relationships. Plus: Posterior Bite Collapse.

ESSAY 4 Q4 Describe the treatment for the types of occlusal trauma.
Three-pronged approach: A) Eliminate inflammation first. B) Control occlusal etiology. C) Develop stable arch relationships. Treatment modalities: equilibration, ortho, prosthetics/splinting. Know Posterior Bite Collapse cascade.
Remember: etiologic agents or adverse environment does NOT guarantee a TFO lesion — individual variation in resistance and repair.
TREATMENT PROTOCOL
Three-step treatment approach for occlusal trauma:

A. Elimination of inflammation — ALWAYS treat the perio disease FIRST.
  • Plaque control, SRP, address active infection
  • Remove the inflammatory component before addressing occlusion
  • If co-destruction is occurring, eliminating inflammation removes the synergistic effect
B. Elimination or control of occlusal etiology
  • Occlusal equilibration (selective grinding to redistribute forces) — for primary TFO
  • Limited or complete orthodontics — to correct malpositioned teeth
  • Occlusal splint/night guard — to reduce parafunctional forces
C. Development of stable intra- and inter-arch relationships
  • Prosthetics (splinting) — especially for secondary TFO
  • Establish occlusal inter-relationships in harmony with neuromuscular and joint function
  • Restore posterior support if missing
Primary TFO Treatment
Reduce the force.
Occlusal equilibration (selective grinding).
Fix the bite.
Once forces acceptable → attachment apparatus heals → PDL returns to pre-force width → mobility reduces.
Reversible outcome.
Secondary TFO Treatment
Compensate for lost support.
Splinting (link mobile teeth to stable neighbors).
Cannot restore lost bone support, but can distribute forces across multiple teeth.
Usually NOT reversible.
POSTERIOR BITE COLLAPSE
Posterior Bite Collapse
A syndrome with many clinical findings — NOT a disease entity.
The cascade:
Missing posterior tooth → Super-erupted opposing tooth → Loss of contact / drifting of adjacent teeth → Loss of posterior support → Flaring of anterior teeth → Attachment loss + additional occlusal trauma

This is the cascade you need to prevent with prosthetic rehabilitation. Replacing missing posterior teeth maintains vertical dimension and prevents anterior overload.
One missing molar → domino effect through the entire arch. The posterior teeth hold up the vertical dimension; lose them and the anteriors take all the force and start to flare.
Important caveat: Etiologic agents or an adverse environment does NOT guarantee the presence or development of an occlusal traumatic lesion. Much depends on the nature of the forces and the resistance and repair capacity of the individual's system. Some patients tolerate significant occlusal discrepancies without developing TFO; others are more susceptible.

Is Mobility Disease?

ESSAY 4 Q5 NO. Increased ≠ increasing. Stable increased mobility = adaptation, no tx needed.

ESSAY 4 Q5 Is mobility disease? Does mobility always need to be treated? If not, why not? When does mobility need to be treated?
NO. A certain increase in mobility can be accepted. Increased (stable) ≠ increasing (pathologic). Treat only when increasing or when it disturbs function/comfort.
This is a conceptual trap — don't equate a physical finding with disease.
MOBILITY IS NOT DISEASE
The answer: NO. Mobility is a clinical finding, not a disease.

A certain increase in mobility can be accepted, provided the mobile tooth does not disturb:
  • Masticatory function
  • Patient comfort
The Critical Distinction
Increased mobility = the tooth is more mobile than normal, but the mobility is STABLE. It has plateaued. The system adapted. This is the result of light force or a resolved TFO lesion.

Increasing mobility = the tooth is getting MORE mobile over time. Documented progression. The system has NOT adapted. This is pathologic.
When does mobility need treatment?
  • When it is INCREASING (pathologic — the system hasn't adapted)
  • When it interferes with masticatory function
  • When it causes patient discomfort
When does mobility NOT need treatment?
  • When it is stable (increased but not increasing)
  • When the tooth functions adequately
  • When the patient is comfortable
  • When serial measurements show no progression
Mobility may INCREASE but is NOT increasing.
= Adapted, stable, healed PDL
= Light force response
= No treatment needed
Mobility INCREASES and IS increasing.
= Non-adapted, progressive, active lesion
= Heavy force or secondary TFO
= Treatment needed
A person who limps but walks fine doesn't need a wheelchair. A person whose limp is getting worse every week needs intervention. The finding (limp/mobility) isn't the disease — the trajectory matters.

SRP — When, What, Why (Q1-3)

ESSAY 3 Q1-3 Clinical/radiographic indications, SRP vs scaling distinction, goals + evidence. → SRP Details

ESSAY 3 Q1 What specific clinical and radiographic findings do you require to determine if SRP is indicated?
Clinical: BOP, PD >3mm with CAL, subgingival calculus. Radiographic: bone loss on PA radiographs. Diagnosis of periodontitis confirmed. SRP = first step for any periodontitis patient.
SRP is both diagnostic (how does the patient respond?) and therapeutic.
Q1: WHEN IS SRP INDICATED?
Clinical findings required:
  • Bleeding on probing (BOP) at sites — indicates active inflammation
  • Probing depth >3mm with clinical attachment loss (CAL) — confirms true pocketing
  • Subgingival calculus — deposits requiring instrumentation below the gingival margin
Radiographic findings required:
  • Bone loss visible on PA radiographs — confirms attachment loss/periodontitis
  • Pattern of bone loss (horizontal vs vertical) guides treatment planning
Diagnosis must be confirmed: Periodontitis (not just gingivitis). Gingivitis alone = scaling + OHI, not root planing. SRP is indicated when there is true attachment loss requiring root surface decontamination.

SRP is the FIRST step for any periodontitis patient. It is both:
  • Diagnostic: How does the patient respond? This determines next steps
  • Therapeutic: Reduces bacterial load, removes deposits, prepares roots for healing
ESSAY 3 Q2 What is root planing, and how does it differ from scaling?
Scaling = removal of deposits (calculus, plaque). Root planing = specific instrumentation of root surface to remove contaminated cementum and create biologically compatible surface. Scaling = cleaning; root planing = preparing the foundation.
These are different procedures with different objectives, though often performed together.
Q2: SRP vs SCALING
SCALING
Removes deposits from the tooth surface (calculus, plaque, stain).
Can be supragingival or subgingival.
Goal: remove extraneous material FROM the tooth.
Think: CLEANING.
ROOT PLANING
Specifically instruments the root surface.
Removes contaminated cementum.
Creates a biologically compatible root surface for healing/reattachment.
Think: PREPARING THE FOUNDATION.
Why root planing matters beyond scaling: Endotoxins (LPS) from gram-negative bacteria become embedded in cementum. Simple scaling removes surface deposits but leaves the contaminated cementum behind. Root planing removes this contaminated layer, exposing a clean, hard, smooth root surface that allows the soft tissue to heal against it.
ESSAY 3 Q3 What are the goals of root planing, and what evidence exists to suggest it is successful?
Goals: reduce subgingival microbial load, remove endotoxin, reduce inflammation, allow tissue shrinkage and reattachment. Success measured by PD reduction, CAL stability/gain, BOP resolution.
Cite: Lindhe 1984 — high OH patients maintained PD reductions over 5 years. Axelsson & Lindhe 1981 — prevention of further attachment loss with recall.
Q3: GOALS + EVIDENCE
Goals of root planing:
  • Reduce subgingival microbial load
  • Remove endotoxin-contaminated cementum
  • Reduce inflammation (allow host response to settle)
  • Allow tissue to shrink (resolution of edema → PD reduction)
  • Allow soft tissue reattachment/readaptation to clean root surface
  • Create conditions for potential new attachment (long JE)
Evidence of success — measured by:
  • PD reduction (tissue shrinkage from resolved inflammation)
  • CAL stability or gain (tissue readaptation to root)
  • BOP resolution (inflammation resolved)
  • Smooth, hard root surfaces on re-evaluation
LIT Lindhe 1984: Patients with high standard of home care maintained CAL + PD reductions more consistently than patients with poor plaque control over 5 years. Home care compliance is the key variable.
LIT Axelsson & Lindhe 1981: Professional recall program + patient OHI → prevented further attachment loss. Demonstrated SRP + maintenance = effective long-term strategy.

What Triggers Surgical Therapy? (Q4)

ESSAY 3 Q4 What specific clinical findings determine if additional therapy is indicated beyond non-surgical intervention? Explain the rationale for surgical periodontal therapy.
Persistent PD ≥5mm + BOP after Phase I re-evaluation. Caffesse 1986: 65% inadequate debridement at ≥5mm. Lindhe: critical PD = 4.2mm. Surgery provides direct access, bone modification, tissue repositioning, regenerative material placement.
Cite: Caffesse 1986 — 65% inadequate debridement. Lindhe — critical probing depth 4.2mm.
FINDINGS THAT TRIGGER SURGERY
After Phase I (SRP + re-evaluation at 4-6 weeks), surgery is indicated when:
  • Persistent PD ≥5mm + BOP = sites that did not respond to closed debridement
  • Remaining subgingival calculus that could not be reached with closed instrumentation
  • Furcation involvement requiring direct access
  • Angular/vertical bone defects amenable to regeneration
The rationale — why SRP alone is insufficient:
LIT Caffesse 1986: Demonstrated 65% inadequate debridement at probing depths ≥5mm with closed (non-surgical) instrumentation. The deeper the pocket, the less effective blind debridement becomes — root anatomy, furcations, and pocket depth all limit instrument access.

LIT Lindhe — Critical Probing Depth: Identified 4.2mm as the critical probing depth. Above this threshold, surgical therapy provides better outcomes than SRP alone. Below it, SRP is generally sufficient.
What surgical therapy provides that SRP cannot:
  1. Direct access for thorough debridement (visualization of root surfaces)
  2. Ability to modify bone architecture (osteoplasty, ostectomy)
  3. Ability to reposition tissue for pocket elimination
  4. Ability to place regenerative materials (bone grafts, membranes, biologics)
Objectives of periodontal surgery:
  1. Gain access for effective removal of etiologic factors following SRP
  2. Reduce pocket depth — decrease places bacteria adhere — make it more maintainable

Adjunctive Antimicrobials (Q5)

ESSAY 3 Q5 Systemic (amox + metro, azithromycin, Periostat) and local (Arestin, Atridox, Perio Chip). CHX + SLS interaction. → Chemotherapeutics

ESSAY 3 Q5 What rationale do you have for the use of adjunctive antimicrobials (systemic and locally administered) in patient care? Give specific clinical examples.
Rationale: when local mechanical debridement alone is insufficient. Systemic for aggressive/refractory cases. Local for non-responsive sites. CHX rinse with SLS precaution. Know specific agents and indications.
Clinical examples: aggressive periodontitis (systemic abx), persistent isolated sites (LAAs), post-surgical adjunct (CHX rinse).
RATIONALE FOR ANTIMICROBIALS
When are antimicrobials indicated? When local mechanical measures (SRP) are not sufficient to control the infection. This includes:
  • Aggressive/rapidly progressive forms of periodontitis
  • Refractory periodontitis (disease progression despite adequate SRP + compliance)
  • Non-responsive sites after Phase I therapy
  • As adjuncts to surgical therapy
Key principle: Antimicrobials are always adjunctive — they supplement, never replace, mechanical debridement.
SYSTEMIC ANTIMICROBIALS
Amoxicillin + Metronidazole
Used in tandem or serially.
Indication: Aggressive/refractory periodontitis.
Amox targets aerobes; Metro targets anaerobes.
Together = broad-spectrum coverage of perio pathogens.
Most common systemic regimen for perio.
Azithromycin
Effective against anaerobes and gram-negative bacilli.
Concentrates in periodontal tissues at levels significantly higher than normal gingiva.
Effective adjunctive therapy for increasing attachment levels in aggressive periodontitis.
Dose: 500mg loading dose, then 250mg/day for 5 days.
Periostat (Doxycycline 20mg)
Sub-antimicrobial dose doxycycline.
NOT an antibiotic at this dose — it's a host modulation agent.
Inhibits collagenase (MMP) activity.
Reduces host-mediated tissue destruction.
Taken BID for extended periods.
LOCAL ANTIMICROBIALS (LAAs)
Indication: Non-responsive sites after SRP — specific sites with PD ≥5mm + BOP that didn't respond to mechanical debridement alone. Placed subgingivally at targeted sites.

Arestin (Minocycline microspheres)
Powder form placed subgingivally.
Sustained release over 14-21 days.
No removal needed — bioresorbable.
Atridox (Doxycycline hyclate 10%)
Gel form, solidifies in pocket.
Sustained release over 7 days.
Bioresorbable polymer.
PerioChip (Chlorhexidine gluconate 2.5mg)
Biodegradable gelatin chip.
Placed into pocket, dissolves over 7-10 days.
Antiseptic, not antibiotic.
Actisite (Tetracycline fiber)
Non-resorbable — requires removal at 10 days.
Placed around tooth, packed into pocket.
Older product, less commonly used.
CHX (CHLORHEXIDINE / PERIDEX)
Gold standard oral antiseptic. 0.12% CHX gluconate rinse (Peridex/PerioGard).

Mechanism: Cationic molecule — binds to negatively charged bacterial cell walls and oral mucosa. Substantivity: remains active on surfaces for hours after application.

CRITICAL INTERACTION — SLS:
CHX is cationic. SLS (sodium lauryl sulfate) in toothpaste is anionic. They form inactivating salts when combined. Even a 30-minute interval between SLS toothpaste and CHX rinsing significantly reduced the antiplaque effect.

Patient instruction: Rinse with CHX at least 30+ minutes after brushing with SLS-containing toothpaste. Better yet — use a non-SLS toothpaste or rinse at different times of day.

Side effects: Staining (brown), taste alteration, increased calculus formation.

Listerine: Essential oils (thymol, eucalyptol, menthol, methyl salicylate). OTC alternative. Less effective than CHX but no staining. No SLS interaction.
LIT Harper & Robinson 1987: SLS-CHX interaction — 30-minute gap still showed reduced efficacy. | Greenstein 1992: CHX substantivity and plaque reduction overview.

ESSAY 3 Q6 What clinical outcomes from non-surgical and surgical therapy are you seeking to observe to determine if therapy has been successful?
PD reduction to ≤3-4mm. BOP resolution. Stable/improved CAL. No suppuration. Radiographic bone stability or fill. Smooth hard root surfaces. Patient maintaining with home care. Enrolled in SPT q3mo.

CLINICAL OUTCOMES DEFINING SUCCESS
Successful therapy is defined by:
  • PD reduction to ≤3-4mm — shallow, maintainable probing depths
  • Resolution of BOP — no active inflammation at treated sites
  • Stable or improved CAL — no further attachment loss, ideally some gain
  • No suppuration — no purulent exudate from pockets
  • Radiographic evidence of bone stability or fill
  • Smooth, hard root surfaces on re-evaluation probing
  • Patient able to maintain with effective home care
  • Patient enrolled in SPT q3mo with stable findings over time
The maintenance requirement: Successful therapy is NOT a one-time event. Success is measured over time — stable results maintained through supportive periodontal therapy. Without recall, even the best surgical outcomes will relapse.
LANDMARK LITERATURE ON MAINTENANCE
LIT Rosling 1976: With regular recall, patients maintained surgically reduced PD and gained attachment. Demonstrated that surgical outcomes are durable IF maintained.
LIT Nyman 1977: Without recall, PD rebounded regardless of surgical technique used. The best surgery in the world fails without maintenance. Technique doesn't matter if the patient disappears.
LIT Lindhe 1984: High oral hygiene patients maintained results over 5 years. Patient compliance with home care is the strongest predictor of long-term success.
LIT Axelsson & Lindhe 1981: Professional recall program prevented further attachment loss long-term. The combination of professional maintenance + patient home care = optimal outcomes.
LIT Isidor & Karring 1986: Professional cleaning q3mo compensated for variations in home care. Even patients with imperfect home care maintained stability when seen every 3 months professionally. This is the basis for the q3mo SPT interval.

Pseudopocket: gingival margin coronal to CEJ + PD >3mm BUT no CAL — probe doesn't extend past CEJ. True pocket: probe extends past CEJ = CAL confirmed. Must locate gingival margin relative to CEJ. Neither probing NOR radiograph alone is sufficient.
Fleisher emphasis: "They must identify the location of the gingival margin relative to the CEJ." — This is the ONLY way.
Q9: PSEUDOPOCKET vs TRUE POCKET
PSEUDOPOCKET (Gingival Pocket / False Pocket)

Definition: Gingival margin is coronal to CEJ + PD >3mm, BUT there is no clinical attachment loss.

What's happening: The tissue is enlarged/swollen (gingival hyperplasia, inflammation causing swelling), creating a deeper sulcus. But the probe does NOT extend past the CEJ — the base of the pocket is at or above the CEJ.

Radiograph: Shows normal bone levels. No evidence of bone loss.

Treatment:
  • Address cause of tissue excess (improved OH, resolve medication-induced hyperplasia)
  • Gingivectomy if needed
  • Monitor
This is NOT periodontitis.
TRUE POCKET (Periodontal Pocket)

Definition: Probe extends past the CEJ = clinical attachment loss confirmed.

What's happening: The junctional epithelium has migrated apically along the root. The connective tissue attachment has been destroyed. There is true loss of periodontal support.

Radiograph: Shows bone loss. Pattern may be horizontal or vertical.

Subtypes:
  • Suprabony pocket: Bottom of pocket is ABOVE the bone crest
  • Infrabony pocket: Bottom of pocket is BELOW the bone crest
This IS periodontitis.
THE KEY POINT — PD ALONE IS NOT SUFFICIENT:
Two patients can both probe 6mm, but for completely different reasons:
  • Patient A: margin 3mm coronal to CEJ, PD 6mm → CAL = 6 - 3 = 3mm CAL
  • Patient B: margin at CEJ, PD 6mm → CAL = 6mm CAL
  • Patient C: 2mm recession past CEJ, PD 4mm → CAL = 4 + 2 = 6mm CAL
You MUST locate the gingival margin relative to the CEJ. Record as positive (recession) or negative (margin coronal to CEJ). This is the only way to calculate true CAL.

Neither probing NOR radiograph alone is enough. Need both to confirm pocket type and bone loss pattern.
LIT Fleisher directive: "They must identify the location of the gingival margin relative to the CEJ. This is the ONLY way we should be telling our students to assess and record it. Every one of us MUST do it this way."
ESSAY 3 Q10 Define and explain the differences between: a) Loss of attachment, b) Attachment apparatus, c) Attached gingiva. How do you assess each clinically?
Three different concepts that sound similar but are completely distinct. Know the precise definitions and clinical assessment methods.
These are frequently confused. The essay tests whether you understand the terminology precisely.
Q10: ATTACHMENT TERMINOLOGY
a) Loss of Attachment (CAL)

Definition: Distance from the CEJ to the base of the pocket. Represents destruction of the connection between tooth and periodontium.

How to measure:
If recession present: CAL = recession + PD
If margin coronal to CEJ: CAL = PD - distance margin is coronal
If margin at CEJ: CAL = PD

What it tells you: How much support has been LOST — the historical record of disease activity.
b) Attachment Apparatus

Definition: The structures that attach the tooth to bone:
  • Cementum
  • PDL fibers
  • Alveolar bone
What it tells you: This is what TFO affects and what periodontal disease destroys. It is the structural support system below the bone crest.

Assessment: Radiographs (bone levels), mobility testing (PDL integrity), probing (attachment loss extent). → TFO affects apparatus only
c) Attached Gingiva

Definition: The band of keratinized tissue that is firmly bound to underlying bone/periosteum.

How to measure:
Width = distance from sulcus base (not gingival margin) to the mucogingival junction (MGJ).

Alternatively: Total KT width - sulcus depth = attached gingiva width.

Key distinction: Different from total keratinized tissue (KT) width. KT includes the free gingiva; attached gingiva does NOT.
Diagnostic framework — putting it together:
Healthy reduced periodontium: Attachment loss present (recession or bone loss), but PD ≤3mm, no BOP, no active inflammation. Diagnosis: healthy but reduced. No periodontitis — just history of past disease or recession.

Reduced periodontium with inflammation: Attachment loss + recession + PD ≤3mm + BOP/inflammation = gingivitis on a reduced periodontium. Not periodontitis yet.

Periodontitis: PD >3mm + inflammation + attachment loss = active disease requiring treatment.

Recession Classification -- Miller 1985 to Cairo RT (2017)

The 2017 classification replaced Miller with Cairo Recession Types. The logic: interproximal attachment = blood supply for graft. Lost IP = no blood = graft dies at that level.

MILLER 1985 -- ORIGINAL CLASSIFICATION (REPLACED)
Miller Class I
Marginal tissue recession does NOT extend to MGJ
No IP attachment loss (bone or soft tissue)
100% coverage possible
Miller Class II
Marginal tissue recession extends to or beyond MGJ
No IP attachment loss
100% coverage possible
Miller Class III
Recession extends to/beyond MGJ
IP attachment loss present BUT coronal to apical extent of facial recession
OR malpositioning of teeth
Partial coverage only
Miller Class IV
Recession extends to/beyond MGJ
Severe IP attachment loss -- extends APICAL to the facial recession
AND/OR severe malpositioning
Root coverage NOT predictable
CAIRO RT (2011/2017) -- CURRENT CLASSIFICATION
replaced Miller -- adopted in 2017 World Workshop
RT1 (= Miller I + II)
Recession WITHOUT loss of interproximal attachment
IP CEJ is clinically not detectable at both mesial and distal aspects
100% root coverage achievable
IP attachment intact = blood supply available for graft = graft will survive
Example: Buccal recession at upper left canine. Buccal CAL = 3mm. IP CEJ not detectable (no IP CAL). The papilla is healthy and tall -- graft has blood supply from both sides.
RT2 (= Miller III)
Recession WITH loss of interproximal attachment
IP CAL ≤ Facial CAL (less or equal)
PARTIAL coverage possible -- amount limited by IP bone level
Example: Buccal recession at upper left canine. Buccal CAL = 4mm. IP CAL = 3mm. Some papilla loss -- blood supply partially compromised. Graft can only survive as far as remaining IP bone.
RT3 (= Miller IV)
Recession WITH loss of interproximal attachment
IP CAL > Facial CAL (greater than facial)
Root coverage NOT predictable
Example: Buccal recession at upper left lateral incisor. Buccal CAL = 6mm. IP CAL = 8mm. IP attachment is so far gone there is no blood supply foundation for a graft.
KEY LOGIC -- WHY IP ATTACHMENT DETERMINES PROGNOSIS
Interproximal attachment = the blood supply foundation for the graft. The IP bone level is the ceiling for how much root coverage you can achieve. A graft placed over denuded root can only survive if blood vessels from the IP papilla can reach it. Lost IP bone = no vessels = graft necrosis at that level.
Quick Conversion Table:
Miller I + II (no IP loss, any MGJ relation) = RT1 = 100% coverage
Miller III (IP loss ≤ facial) = RT2 = partial coverage
Miller IV (IP loss > facial) = RT3 = unpredictable coverage

Recession Etiology -- Complete List

From Dr. Pani's slides. Every single cause of gingival recession. Connects to etiology elimination (must address before surgery).

CATEGORY 1: TRAUMA
Tooth brushing trauma -- most common cause, aggressive horizontal scrubbing
Flossing trauma -- improper snapping floss through contacts
Factitious injury -- self-inflicted: nail scratching/picking at gingiva, foreign objects
Poorly designed partial dentures -- clasps or rests causing tissue trauma
Direct trauma from malocclusion -- tooth-to-tissue contact
Chemical trauma -- aspirin burns (holding tablets against tissue), caustic agents
CATEGORY 2: PATHOLOGICAL BONE LOSS
Periodontal disease -- chronic inflammation leads to bone loss, then soft tissue follows
Associated with treatment of periodontal disease -- post-surgical recession is expected after pocket reduction
Smoking -- vasoconstriction, impaired healing, accelerated bone loss
CATEGORY 3: LOCAL PLAQUE RETENTION FACTORS
High muscle attachment -- pulls tissue away from tooth during function
Frenal pull -- aberrant frenum attachment exerts tension on marginal gingiva
Subgingival margins / Biologic Width violation -- restorations too close to bone
Calculus -- both supra- and sub-gingival
CATEGORY 4: ORTHODONTIC MOVEMENTS
Excessive proclination -- labial tipping beyond cortical plate
Arch expansion -- buccal movement through bone envelope
KEY MECHANISM (Steiner 1981, Karring 1982):
Bodily tooth movement in a labial direction towards and through the cortical plate of the alveolar bone:
1. Initial thinning of the bone plate
2. No bone formation will take place in front of the tooth
3. A labial bone dehiscence is therefore created
4. Soft tissue over the dehiscence = vulnerable to recession
The tooth moves through the "envelope" of bone. Once it exits, there's no bone to support the tissue. The resulting dehiscence is the underlying cause, and any subsequent insult (brushing, inflammation) triggers recession.
LIT Steiner et al. 1981: Documented bone resorption with labial tooth movement. | Karring et al. 1982: Confirmed no bone regeneration labial to proclinated teeth.
CATEGORY 5: UNDERLYING BONY DEHISCENCE
Pre-existing dehiscence (anatomic variant or pathologic) -- no bone covering the root surface facially. Tissue is "tented" over root with no bony support. Any insult precipitates recession.
Correction possibility: Orthodontic movement lingually can reverse the process:
Movement lingually leads to increase in thickness of labial soft tissue, increase in gingival height, decrease in recession/bone dehiscence.

Free Gingival Graft (FGG) -- Complete Technique & Healing

Goal: INCREASE KERATINIZED TISSUE. Best KT gain. The workhorse procedure when the problem is insufficient KT, not esthetics. KT decision tree: limited KT + no bone loss = FGG.

FGG: TECHNIQUE -- STEP BY STEP
Recipient Site Preparation:
1. Split thickness incision is made at the mucogingival junction (MGJ)
2. Vertical incisions may be made mesial/distal to the area
3. The flap may be sutured to the depth of the vestibule (secures the apical tissue away)
4. Prepared recipient bed = periosteum left intact (blood supply for graft!)
Donor Site (Palate):
1. Foil template or probe used to measure the amount of donor tissue needed
2. Graft should be taken 2mm apically from the gingival margin of teeth (avoids recession at donor site)
3. Graft thickness: 0.75-1.25mm
4. Too thin = won't survive (insufficient CT for vascularization)
5. Too thick = hits greater palatine artery (hemorrhage risk)
Placement:
1. Place graft at recipient site with NO dead space (graft must contact periosteum for plasmatic diffusion)
2. Secure with sling sutures
3. Apply pressure (wet gauze, 5 min) to adapt graft and express fibrin
FGG HEALING -- 3 PHASES
Phase 1: Initial/Plasmatic
Days 0-3
Plasmatic circulation sustains graft
Epithelium of FGG degenerates
Fibrin clot forms between graft and bed
The graft is surviving on passive diffusion of plasma from the recipient bed -- like a sponge soaking up nutrients. No blood vessels yet.
Phase 2: Revascularization
Days 2-11
Anastomosis between blood vessels of recipient bed and those in the graft
Blood circulation is re-established
Capillary proliferation
Fibrous union between graft and bed
Re-epithelialization of graft begins
New blood vessels are growing into the graft from below. This is the critical phase -- if the graft lifts off or dead space forms, these connections break and the graft dies.
Phase 3: Tissue Maturation
Days 11-42
Normal vascular system established by day 14
Maturation of epithelium
Graft takes on characteristics of donor tissue (keratinized)
LIT Gargiulo & Arocha 1967: Described FGG healing phases. | Oliver et al. 1968: Confirmed revascularization timeline. | Nobuto et al. 1988: Detailed vascular anastomosis process.
ADVANTAGES:
Rapid and predictable
Not as technically challenging
May be more easily combined with a frenectomy
Best for increasing KT zone
DISADVANTAGES:
Poor color match / esthetics (looks like a white patch -- donor palate tissue is different color)
Two surgical sites (recipient + palate)
Post-operative discomfort (palatal donor site is painful)
KEY LITERATURE -- KT REQUIREMENTS
Lang & Loe 1972: A minimum of 2mm of KT (1mm attached + 1mm free gingiva) is needed to maintain gingival health.
Maynard & Wilson 1979: If there is good OH, a lack of attached gingiva does NOT result in recession. However, 5mm of KT is ideal around restorations (2mm free + 3mm attached).
Serino et al. 1994: 3mm recession worsened 67% of the time. 4mm recession worsened 98% of the time.
Chambrone & Tatakis 2016: Untreated facial recession with good OH is highly likely to increase over time. KT and/or greater gingival thickness decrease the likelihood of recession increase.
Ericsson & Lindhe 1984: Subgingival restorations placed in areas with narrow KT may favor recession in presence of subgingival plaque.

Connective Tissue Graft + Coronally Advanced Flap (CTG + CAF)

Gold standard for ROOT COVERAGE and ESTHETICS. Goal: cover exposed root with tissue that matches color. Split-full-split combined flap design.

CTG + CAF: TECHNIQUE
Recipient Site:
1. Split thickness incisions
2. Horizontal sulcular incisions
3. Intact interproximal tissue -- do NOT cut through papillae
4. Flap reflection beyond MGJ for mobility (must advance coronally without tension)
Donor Site (Palate) -- Two Approaches:
A. Double incision (with epithelium): Two parallel incisions into palate, remove tissue block, then trim epithelium off the graft
B. Single incision (without epithelium): Trap-door approach -- one incision through epithelium, dissect CT from underneath. Preferred -- less morbidity, palatal tissue stays as bandage
CTG Placement Options:
1. Completely covered (flap over entire graft) -- allows thin grafts to survive, better esthetics. PREFERRED for root coverage
2. Partially covered (some graft exposed) -- maintains the MGJ and allows for keratinization from exposed CT
3. Sutured to the interdental papilla + flap coronally positioned and sutured to graft and IP papilla
LIT Langer & Langer 1985: Described the subepithelial CTG technique. Established the procedure as the standard for root coverage with predictable esthetics.
CAF REQUIREMENTS (BERNIMOULIN 1981) -- ALL MUST BE MET
1. Minimum tissue thickness of 0.8mm -- thinner flap won't survive coronal repositioning
2. Shallow recession (<4mm) -- deep recession = less predictable
3. Miller Class I / RT1 -- no IP attachment loss
4. Keratinized attached tissue >3mm -- must have tissue to advance
5. Minimal to no flap tension -- tension = flap retraction = graft exposure = failure
6. Flap should be placed 2.5mm coronal to CEJ -- to account for post-operative shrinkage
7. Split-full-split design (3mm split zones mesial/distal) combined flap
LIT Bernimoulin et al. 1975/1981: Established CAF technique and requirements. | Bernimoulin et al. 1975: Coronally advanced FGG (original concept).
HEALING OF PEDICLE SOFT TISSUE GRAFTS (WILDERMAN & WENTZ 1965)
Adaptation Stage
Days 0-4
Thin fibrin layer forms on laterally positioned flap
Epithelium of transplanted tissue proliferates and reaches tooth surface coronally
Proliferation Stage
Days 4-21
Fibrin layer between tooth and flap is invaded by connective tissue
Days 6-10: fibroblasts appear on the root surface
Thin collagen fibers adjacent to root surface
Apical proliferation of epithelium (from coronal)
Attachment Stage
Days 27-28
Thick collagen fibers inserted into a layer of cementum
True connective tissue attachment begins
Maturation Stage
2-3 months
Continuous formation of collagen fibers inserted into cementum apically
Full maturation of new attachment
CTG ADVANTAGES:
Good color blend (covered by patient's own tissue)
Can increase zone AND thickness of KT
Predictable root coverage in Miller Class I, II (RT1)
CTG DISADVANTAGES:
Requires adequate vestibular depth
Two surgical sites
Post-operative discomfort
Papilla on either side of treated tooth may be altered cosmetically
TREATMENT HIERARCHY FOR ROOT COVERAGE (Cortellini & Pini Prato 2012):
Best root coverage: CAF+CTG > CAF+EMD > CAF+BM > CAF+ADM > CAF alone
Best KT gain: CAF+CTG >> all others
Best esthetics: CAF + thin CTG = CAF + EMD
Best long-term stability: CAF+CTG > all others
Key prognostic factors: Thick KT, Miller I/II, papilla morphology, non-smoker, maxillary > mandibular
For thick residual KT: CAF alone may suffice. For thin residual KT: CAF + graft (CTG/EMD/ADM/PRP) needed.

Pedicle Grafts -- Laterally Repositioned, Double Papilla, Semilunar

Tissue moved from ADJACENT site with its blood supply intact (pedicle = attached base). Alternative to free grafts when donor tissue is available next to the defect.

ROTATIONAL FLAPS
LATERALLY REPOSITIONED FLAP (Grupe & Warren 1956)
Technique:
1. Split thickness incision on papilla distal or mesial to the affected site, extending beyond the MGJ
2. The flap is allowed to slide over to the adjacent recipient site
3. Requirement: Pedicle flap should have adequate zone of KT (thickness AND quantity) adjacent to the area of recession
Mechanism: Flap gains nutrition from the wide base of the pedicle flap AND from the prepared recipient site of periosteum surrounding the denuded root area
Best for: Covering small areas of recession with adequate adjacent donor tissue
LIT Grupe & Warren 1956: Original description. | De Waal H et al. 1988: Refinements to technique.
DOUBLE PAPILLA FLAP (Cohen DW & Ross SE 1968)
Technique:
1. No destruction of interdental papilla on either side of recession
2. Split thickness flap
3. The papilla from each side is moved on the facial/lingual aspect of the recipient tooth
4. Two papillae are sutured together over the recession
Advantage: Dual blood supply -- vessels from both papillae feed the graft simultaneously
Best for: Isolated recession on single tooth with healthy papillae on both sides
LIT Cohen DW, Ross SE 1968: Original description of double papilla flap technique.
ADVANCED FLAPS
KEY PRINCIPLE: A mucosal flap can be raised beyond the MGJ and moved coronally because of the elasticity of the alveolar mucosa. Single or multiple defects can be covered. Can be used in conjunction with enamel matrix derivative (EMD), autogenous grafts, or allografts.
SEMILUNAR CORONALLY POSITIONED FLAP (Tarnow 1986)
Technique:
1. Apical semilunar incision made 2mm away from papilla (sufficient distance for vascularity)
2. Intrasulcular incisions
3. Partial thickness flap created
4. Flap coronally advanced to cover recession
5. Creating a new papilla -- equal distances maintained

ADVANTAGES:
No shortening of the vestibule
Good color blend
Papilla medially and distally remain unchanged cosmetically
Rapid and predictable

DISADVANTAGES:
Requires adequate zone AND thickness of KG
Does NOT increase the zone of KG
Does not account for "hidden recession"

INDICATIONS:
Gingival recession with minimal sulcus depth present
Presence of adequate zone of KG
Recession around full coverage restorations within the esthetic zone
LIT Tarnow 1986: Described semilunar technique. | Allen & Miller 1989: Coronally advanced flap with citric acid root conditioning.
CORONALLY ADVANCED FGG (Bernimoulin et al. 1975)
Two-stage procedure: Stage 1 = FGG placed to increase KT. Stage 2 (after healing) = coronally advance the grafted tissue.
Combines the KT-building advantage of FGG with the root coverage capability of the CAF.

Alloderm (Acellular Dermal Matrix) -- CAF with ADM

Alternative to CTG when palatal harvest is not possible or patient declines second surgical site. CRITICAL: must be completely covered.

WHAT IS ALLODERM?
Composition: Human dermis made of a matrix of collagen, elastin, vascular channels, and protein that supports its revascularization.
Processing: Epidermis and cells have been removed to prevent tissue rejection and graft rejection WITHOUT damaging the underlying matrix structure.
Result: An acellular scaffold that the body can repopulate with its own cells and blood vessels.
CRITICAL RULE: ALLODERM MUST BE COMPLETELY COVERED BY FLAP
If Alloderm is exposed, it NECROTIZES -- turns necrotic and smells terrible.
This is the KEY difference from CTG: CTG can survive partial exposure (it has living connective tissue with its own cells). Alloderm CANNOT (it is acellular -- depends entirely on host vasculature for repopulation).
This means the flap must achieve complete coverage without tension. If there is ANY doubt about achieving coverage, use CTG instead. Flap must be closed completely
Technique:
1. Hydrate Alloderm for 15-20 minutes before placement
2. SRP of the teeth in the affected site
3. Prepare the recipient site 3mm apically to the papilla tip
4. Leave one papilla intact
5. Place Alloderm and suture for stability
6. Place sling sutures
7. Ensure complete flap coverage -- no exposure allowed
CONTRAINDICATION
Allergy to gentamicin -- Alloderm is processed with gentamicin. Must ask about antibiotic allergies before using.
Alloderm vs CTG Comparison:
Alloderm: No second surgical site (no palatal harvest), less morbidity, unlimited tissue available, BUT must be covered, slightly lower root coverage outcomes, higher cost
CTG: Better color match, can survive partial exposure, gold standard outcomes, BUT requires palatal donor site, limited by palatal tissue availability, more post-op discomfort

Root coverage ranking (Cortellini & Pini Prato 2012): CAF+CTG > CAF+ADM
KT gain ranking: CAF+CTG >> CAF+ADM
Other CAF Combinations:
CAF + Collagen Membrane -- barrier approach
CAF + EMD (Enamel Matrix Derivative) -- Emdogain for regeneration
CAF + Tissue Engineered Grafts -- platelet rich plasma (PRP), human fibroblast-derived dermal substitute

PALATAL HARVESTING -- ANATOMY OF DONOR SITE (Reiser et al. 1996)
Tissue Harvesting Depths by Palatal Vault Type:
The distance from the CEJ to the greater palatine artery determines how much tissue you can safely harvest:
SHALLOW PALATE
Greater palatine artery is at 7mm from CEJ
HIGHEST RISK -- very little tissue available
Must harvest conservatively
Thin, narrow band of harvestable tissue
AVERAGE PALATE
Greater palatine artery is at 12mm from CEJ
Moderate amount of tissue available
Standard harvest depth
Most common anatomy encountered
HIGH-VAULTED PALATE
Greater palatine artery is at 17mm from CEJ
MOST tissue available
Generous harvest possible
Lower risk of arterial damage
EXAM CRITICAL: 7mm (shallow) / 12mm (average) / 17mm (high-vaulted) = the safe harvesting depths. These numbers are consistently tested. Remember: graft should be taken 2mm apically from the gingival margin of the teeth to avoid recession at the donor site.
KEY ANATOMIC LANDMARKS
Greater Palatine Foramen:
Located approximately 2mm from the posterior border of the hard palate (junction of horizontal plate of palatine bone and posterior hard palate)
Transmits the greater palatine artery and nerve (V2 branch)
The greater palatine artery is the PRIMARY blood supply to palatal tissue
Artery runs anteriorly from the foramen in a groove on the hard palate
DANGER: Cutting too deep during harvest risks transecting this artery
If cut: apply pressure, may need suture ligation or electrocautery
Incisive Foramen (Nasopalatine Foramen):
Located in the anterior midline of the hard palate, behind the maxillary central incisors
Transmits the nasopalatine nerve and vessels (sphenopalatine artery terminal branch)
Landmark for the anterior limit of palatal harvesting
Tissue anterior to this is thin and difficult to harvest
MANDIBULAR LANDMARKS (for lower arch surgery)
Mental Foramen:
Located on the buccal surface of the mandible, typically between premolars (below #20 or between #20-21)
Transmits the mental nerve and vessels
AVOID during MG surgery on the mandible -- can cause permanent paresthesia of lower lip/chin if damaged
Identified by palpation or radiograph before surgery
Mandibular Foramen:
Located on the medial surface of the mandibular ramus
Landmark for the inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) block
The IAN enters here before running through the mandibular canal
Relevant for mandibular MG surgery under block anesthesia
Lingual Nerve:
Runs along the floor of the mouth, in close proximity to the lingual surface of mandibular molars
Careful with lingual flaps in the posterior mandible -- the lingual nerve can be superficial (as shallow as 2-3mm below the alveolar crest in the 3rd molar region)
Damage = numbness/altered sensation of the anterior 2/3 of the tongue
GRAFT HARVESTING SAFE ZONE -- SUMMARY

Palatal Tissue -- Unique Properties

The palate is unique: ALL keratinized tissue, no MGJ, no elastic mucosa. This limits what procedures can be done ON the palate (vs FROM the palate as a donor).

PALATAL TISSUE PROPERTIES
Key Facts:
1. ALL palatal tissue is keratinized -- there is NO mucogingival junction (MGJ) on the palate
2. Cannot perform APF (apically positioned flap) on the palate -- there is no elastic alveolar mucosa to stretch apically. The palatal tissue is tightly bound to underlying bone (mucoperiosteum).
3. Requires paramarginal incision to thin tissue -- cannot simply reflect a full-thickness flap
4. Blood supply: Greater palatine artery -- runs anteriorly from the greater palatine foramen along the palatal vault
5. Tissue is dense, fibrous, keratinized masticatory mucosa
CLINICAL IMPLICATION
When you need to address palatal tissue (e.g., palatal crown lengthening, palatal pocket reduction), you use a paramarginal/internal bevel incision to thin the tissue rather than an APF. The tissue is "thinned" in place rather than repositioned, because there is no elastic mucosa to allow repositioning.

This is why the palate is primarily used as a DONOR site (for FGG and CTG) rather than a RECIPIENT site for MG procedures.
Palate as Donor Site -- What You Get:
For FGG: Full-thickness epithelialized graft. Contains epithelium + CT. Will keratinize at recipient site (tissue retains donor characteristics).
For CTG: Subepithelial connective tissue only. Better color match at recipient because recipient epithelium grows over it. Palatal wound heals faster because epithelium stays in place (trap-door technique).
Healing of donor site: Secondary intention. May use collagen plug, periodontal dressing, or stent to protect. Greater palatine artery provides rapid healing blood supply.
KT Around Implants (Wennstrom & Derks 2012):
Same question as natural teeth: Do we need KT around implants?
Evidence suggests adequate KT around implants facilitates plaque control and reduces soft tissue inflammation. Lack of KT may contribute to peri-implant mucositis progression. Peri-implant disease

Acute conditions requiring immediate management. Diagnosis, differential, and initial treatment for each. Connects to NSPT/SRP.

I. ACUTE GINGIVAL DISEASES
A. Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis (NUG) / Periodontitis (NUP)
Also known as: Vincent's Infection, trench mouth
Clinical features:
- Surface necrosis and inflammation characterized by greyish-white pseudomembrane
- Ulcerates and necroses the papilla and gingival margins
- Punched-out papilla -- pathognomonic finding
- Interproximal craters
- Involved areas are very painful
- Spirochetes predominate

NUG vs NUP (MacCarthy & Claffey, J Clin Perio 1991):
NUG = confined to gingiva (papillae). NUP = extends to deeper periodontal structures with loss of attachment.

Risk factors (Barnes et al., J Perio 1973):
- Poor oral hygiene (87% of NUG patients vs 40% of controls)
- Existing gingivitis
- Smoking
- Stress / Emotional stress
- Decreased host resistance
- Malnutrition
- Mean age: 20 years (young adult population)

Histology (Listgarten, J Perio 1965): 4 distinct layers (from tooth surface progressing into tissue):
1. Bacterial zone -- bacteria and spirochetes
2. Band of PMNs (polymorphonuclear neutrophils)
3. Zone of tissue necrosis (dominated by spirochetes)
4. Zone of spirochete infiltration

Treatment:
CAUTION: Do NOT aggressively debride -- tissue is extremely fragile, scaling will ensure tissue loss
Very gentle debridement + systemic antibiotic: metronidazole (drug of choice for anaerobes/spirochetes), OR penicillin, tetracycline
Rx: Chlorhexidine (Peridex) rinse
Alternative approach to minimize tissue destruction
B. Acute Herpetic Gingivostomatitis
Primarily in children
Duration: 7-10 days
Sudden onset of high fever + lymphadenopathy
Gingiva becomes red, swollen, tender
Mucosal tissue red with multiple vesicles
Incubation period: 10-14 days
Can mimic severe gingivitis, NUG
Treatment: Palliative measures. Antiviral agents (acyclovir) if initiated within first 3 days can reduce duration of symptoms.
C. Pericoronitis
Inflammation of gingiva around crown of incompletely erupted tooth
Most often in mandibular 3rd molar area
Overlying tissue (operculum) is red, swollen, extremely tender
Pain may radiate to the ear
Treatment: Irrigation + antibiotics if fever present. If fluctuant operculum: I&D (incision and drainage)

II. PERIODONTAL ABSCESS
Definition (Ranney 1977): An acute destructive process in the periodontium resulting in localized collections of pus communicating with the oral cavity through the gingival sulcus or other periodontal site, and NOT arising from the tooth pulp.

Key distinction -- Gingival Abscess vs Periodontal Abscess:
Gingival abscess: Lesion of marginal or interdental gingiva. Often in previously disease-free site. Confined to papilla. Caused by foreign body forced into sulcus. Smooth, shiny surface. Fluctuant within 24-48 hours.
Periodontal abscess: Associated with pre-existing periodontal pocket. Pocket orifice becomes occluded (foreign object or superficial healing) preventing drainage of purulent material.

Clinical Features:
Most common symptom: PAIN
Tender to touch/chewing, tender to percussion
Frequently mobile
May extrude from socket ("feels high" to occlusion)
Purulent exudate
Regional lymphadenopathy, possible fever
Swelling may extend to face and neck
Red to reddish-blue tissue

Microbiology (Newman & Sims, J Perio 1978):
66% of flora are gram negative
65% are anaerobic
P. gingivalis and P. intermedia found in 90% of abscesses (Hafstrom et al., J Perio 1994)

Statistic: 85% of toothaches are pulpal; <15% are periodontal abscesses
Differential Diagnosis:
Periapical abscess: Always nonvital (but periodontal abscess CAN be associated with nonvital tooth). Different probing pattern.
Acute pulpitis: Responsive to vitality test. No swelling or purulence.
Incomplete tooth fracture: Pain on biting, thermal sensitivity, possible increased probing.
Lateral periodontal cyst: Well-defined radiolucency on lateral root surface. Usually NOT painful. Usually NOT associated with increased probing.
Osteomyelitis: Infection of bone. Rapid onset, pain, no new increased probing. Radiographic changes diagnostic.
CLOSED APPROACH:
Scaling and root planing
Limited access
Inability to place regenerative material
For less severe cases
OPENED APPROACH:
Treating the "hot" lesion surgically
Complete visualization of defect extent
Better access for removal of all causative agents
Ability to place regenerative material
Significant potential for repair if treated appropriately

Penicillin | Amoxicillin | Tetracycline | Clindamycin | Azithromycin
Anaerobic cultures may guide selection. Flora is predominantly anaerobic and gram-negative.

What was formerly "aggressive periodontitis" is now typically:
Stage III or IV (severity) + Grade C (rapid rate of progression)
With a molar-incisor pattern distribution (if localized form)

- Destruction exceeds expectations given biofilm deposits
- Patterns suggestive of periods of rapid progression
- ≥2mm bone loss over 5 years (direct evidence)
- % bone loss / age >1.0 (indirect evidence)
- Early onset disease (young patient with severe destruction)

- ≥10 cigarettes/day

Management Approach:
1. Scaling and Root Planing -- foundation of treatment SRP
2. Systemic antibiotics (ADJUNCTIVE) -- critical for aggressive forms:

4. Surgery if needed after re-evaluation Systemic antibiotics detail

COMPONENTS OF PERIODONTAL DIAGNOSIS
Three components:
1. Disease Type (soft and hard tissue) -- based on classification system
2. Extent -- localized (<30% of sites) vs generalized (>30% of sites)
3. Severity -- Slight (1-2mm CAL), Moderate (3-4mm CAL), Advanced (≥5mm CAL or furcation involvement)

STAGING (I-IV) = SEVERITY & COMPLEXITY
Key diagnostic criterion: Interdental CAL at site of greatest loss, or radiographic bone loss

Stage I: Initial -- CAL 1-2mm, max PD ≤4mm, mostly horizontal bone loss. No tooth loss due to perio.
Stage II: Moderate -- CAL 3-4mm, max PD ≤5mm, mostly horizontal bone loss. No tooth loss due to perio.
Stage III: Severe -- CAL ≥5mm, PD ≥6mm, bone loss extending to middle or apical 1/3 of root. Tooth loss ≤4 teeth due to perio. Furcation involvement II-III, moderate ridge defects.
Stage IV: Advanced -- Same severity as Stage III PLUS need for complex rehabilitation. Tooth loss ≥5 due to perio. Bite collapse, flaring, drifting, <20 remaining teeth.

Extent: Localized (<30%), Generalized (≥30%), or Molar-Incisor pattern

Clinicians should initially assume Grade B and seek evidence to shift to A or C.

- No loss over 5 years (direct evidence)
- % bone loss / age <0.25
- Heavy biofilm deposits with low levels of destruction
- Non-smoker, normoglycemic

Grade B (Moderate):
- <2mm loss over 5 years
- % bone loss / age 0.25 to 1.0
- Destruction commensurate with biofilm deposits
- <10 cigarettes/day

Grade C (Rapid):
- ≥2mm loss over 5 years
- % bone loss / age >1.0
- Destruction EXCEEDS expectations given biofilm -- patterns suggestive of periods of rapid progression

ESSAY 1 CROWN LENGTHENING — Full Answer Framework
ESSAY 1 — QUESTION 1: Define Biologic Width. Why is it important for crown lengthening?

Opening statement: Biologic width (now called supracrestal attachment) is the combined width of the junctional epithelium (~1mm) and the connective tissue attachment (~1mm) that occupies the space between the base of the gingival sulcus and the alveolar bone crest, totaling approximately 2mm.

Key points to include:

1. Gargiulo, Wentz & Orban (1961, J Periodont 42:8) established these dimensions through cadaver studies — the foundational measurements for all BW concepts.

2. From bone crest coronally: ~1mm CT attachment + ~1mm JE = ~2mm BW. Add ~1mm sulcus = ~3mm from bone crest to gingival margin.

3. The required distance from restorative margin to bone crest = ± 4mm (2mm BW + 1mm sulcus + ~1mm ferrule/impression clearance).

4. BW is non-negotiable — the body WILL re-establish it. If violated by a restorative margin → chronic inflammation → the body resorbs bone until it gets its 2mm back → margin exposure → treatment failure.

5. Crown lengthening surgically creates the needed 4mm clearance by moving bone and/or tissue apically.

Close with: Crown lengthening is a restoratively driven periodontal procedure whose entire rationale rests on respecting biologic width. Without understanding BW, there is no basis for the procedure.

ESSAY 1 — QUESTION 2: You remove a temporary restoration and see bleeding and ulceration. How do you determine if CL is necessary or if another problem exists?

Opening: Before concluding CL is needed, rule out other causes of bleeding/ulceration around a temporary restoration.

Other causes to rule out:

1. Plaque-induced gingivitis from inadequate oral hygiene around the temporary

2. Open margins on the temporary (overhanging or short margins trapping bacteria)

3. Excess cement or temporary material subgingivally (foreign body reaction)

4. Allergic reaction to temporary material

5. Occlusal trauma on the temporary (trauma from occlusion)

How to distinguish:

1. Probe carefully around the restoration

2. Take a PA radiograph

3. Measure the distance from the restorative margin to the alveolar bone crest

4. If the margin is within 2mm of bone → biologic width violation → CL is indicated

5. If the margin is 3-4mm from bone and inflammation persists → the cause is one of the five above → fix that cause first

Close with: CL should only be performed after other causes have been ruled out AND biologic width invasion has been confirmed. The simplest explanation should be investigated first before committing to surgery.

ESSAY 1 — QUESTION 3: When is gingivectomy alone sufficient vs when is flap + bone removal required? (Coslet Classification)

Opening: The Coslet 1977 classification of altered passive eruption answers this question directly. It uses two variables: (1) amount of keratinized tissue and (2) position of alveolar bone relative to the CEJ.

Type 1A — Lots of KT + Bone at NORMAL position:

→ Gingivectomy alone is sufficient. The bone is already in its normal position (2-3mm apical to CEJ). The excess tissue is the only problem. External bevel gingivectomy removes the excess, exposing the anatomic crown without violating BW.

Type 1B — Lots of KT + Bone AT THE CEJ:

→ Gingivectomy FAILS. Clinically looks identical to 1A but the bone crest is at the level of the CEJ. If you perform gingivectomy alone, you expose the bone and violate BW. Must perform flap surgery with ostectomy to reposition the bone apically and create the required 4mm distance from the intended margin to the new bone crest.

Type 2A — Minimal KT + Bone at NORMAL position:

→ CAUTION. Bone position is appropriate but performing gingivectomy would remove the limited keratinized tissue, creating a mucogingival defect. May require KT augmentation (FGG) before or concurrent with the crown lengthening procedure. An apically positioned flap preserves existing KT while exposing crown.

Type 2B — Minimal KT + Bone AT THE CEJ:

→ MOST COMPLEX. Double problem: bone too high AND insufficient KT. Requires osseous surgery (ostectomy) to move bone apically AND tissue management to preserve or augment the limited KT. Combined flap approach. Highest complication risk. May require staged procedures.

Close with: The Coslet classification demonstrates that the surgical approach is determined by two critical variables. Only Type 1A permits gingivectomy alone. All other types require more complex management. A radiograph or bone sounding is ESSENTIAL before surgery to distinguish 1A from 1B — they appear clinically identical.

ESSAY 1 — QUESTION 4: How long should you wait before placing the final restoration? Rationale?

Opening: The recommended healing time is 6-8-12 weeks or more, depending on location and complexity.

Rationale:

1. The biologic width needs time to re-establish its stable dimensions (~2mm) above the new bone crest position.

2. The gingival margin position is UNSTABLE during healing — it will migrate as the tissues mature and the BW re-establishes.

3. If the final restoration is placed too early, the margin position hasn't settled → the margin will shift as BW re-establishes → exposed margins, re-inflammation, or submerged margins with pocket formation.

Posterior vs Anterior:

Posterior teeth are more forgiving — 6-8 weeks often sufficient. Anterior teeth require LONGER (12+ weeks) because esthetic concerns demand that tissue be fully mature and stable before committing to a final margin position.

Creeping attachment and gingival rebound:

Goldman 1964 and Matter & Cimasoni 1976 documented creeping attachment — 0.5-1.2mm of coronal gingival margin migration over 12 months following surgery. Van der Velden 1982 documented gingival rebound — regeneration of interdental soft tissues following denudation. Both phenomena mean the gingival margin will move coronally over time, which must be accounted for in margin placement.

Consequences of insufficient healing:

BW re-establishment continues post-restoration → bone resorption under the crown → margin exposure → pocket formation → treatment failure → requires redoing the crown AND possibly redoing the CL.

Close with: Patience is critical. The relationship of the alveolar crest to the base of the gingival sulcus is stable and predictable in the presence of optimal plaque control, but only after sufficient healing time has elapsed.

ESSAY 1 — QUESTION 5: List 3 complications of crown lengthening and their remedies.

Complication 1 — Violation of adjacent teeth:

Bone removal to create 4mm distance on the target tooth inevitably affects neighboring teeth. Osseous recontouring requires blending into adjacent bone levels, which means removing supporting bone from healthy teeth and potentially creating iatrogenic defects.

Remedy: Pre-surgical planning with periapical radiographs to assess how much bone removal is needed and which adjacent teeth will be affected. Consider orthodontic forced eruption as an alternative when adjacent teeth would be significantly compromised. Inform the patient of risks to adjacent teeth before surgery.

Complication 2 — Root sensitivity:

Exposed root surfaces post-surgery result in dentin hypersensitivity. Cementum is removed during root planing, and newly exposed root surfaces have open dentinal tubules, leading to sensitivity to thermal, osmotic, and tactile stimuli.

Remedy: Desensitizing agents including fluoride varnish and potassium nitrate toothpaste. Time — sensitivity usually improves as cementum re-mineralizes and dentinal tubules occlude naturally. Resin bonding agents for persistent cases.

Complication 3 — Esthetic compromise:

Especially concerning in the anterior region. Results include: longer clinical crowns, visible root surface, tissue asymmetry between treated and untreated teeth, and potential "black triangles" from papilla loss. The smile line may reveal exposed roots with color mismatch between enamel and cementum/dentin.

Remedy: Careful pre-surgical planning with esthetic analysis. Consider orthodontic forced eruption as an alternative for anterior cases. Gingival prosthetics (pink porcelain on restorations) in severe cases. Fleisher's principle: NO resective surgery in the anterior unless full prosthetic reconstruction is planned.

Close with: Alternatives include orthodontic forced eruption (moves tooth coronally bringing bone and tissue, avoiding damage to adjacent teeth) and deep margin elevation (composite placed to elevate a subgingival margin to a supragingival position, avoiding surgery entirely).

ESSAY 2 GTR / GBR — Full Answer Framework (9 Sub-Questions)
ESSAY 2 — Q1: Define GTR. What is the ultimate goal?

Opening: The ultimate goal in periodontics is to reestablish a periodontium to its original state of health — moving from disease to UNCOMPROMISED health, not merely compromised health.

Four key terms (define each):

1. Reattachment — reconnecting surgically separated HEALTHY tissue. Routine after flap surgery at edges beyond the defect.

2. Repair — long junctional epithelium (LJE). The body's DEFAULT healing. Epithelium migrates down root, adheres with hemidesmosomes. No CT fiber insertion. Protective but not ideal.

3. New Attachment — CT fibers inserting into PREVIOUSLY DISEASED root surface. The goal of GTR.

4. Regeneration — the biologic process by which the architecture and function of lost tissue is COMPLETELY returned: new cementum + new PDL + new bone (Melcher, 1969).

GTR Definition: Allowing certain cell types to participate in the healing process while EXCLUDING other types, for the purpose of promoting new attachment. A physical barrier blocks fast cells (epithelium + gingival CT) → gives PDL cells time to migrate coronally → new cementum + new PDL + new bone = regeneration.

Assessment methods: Probe (unreliable), radiograph (shows bone not CT), re-entry (see bone directly), histology = ONLY definitive method (Cite: Nyman 1982 — histologic confirmation of new cementum with inserting collagen fibers).

ESSAY 2 — Q2: Describe the landmark studies that led to the development of GTR.

Study 1 — Karring, Nyman & Lindhe 1980 (J Clin Perio 7: 96-105):

Method: 3 beagle dogs, 12 teeth with induced periodontitis to half root length. Roots extracted and implanted into bone cavities — epithelial migration prevented, only bone cells could contact diseased root.

Result: No new CT attachment — root resorption and ankylosis.

Proved: Bone cells alone CANNOT produce new attachment.

Study 2 — Nyman et al. 1980 (J Clin Perio 7: 39):

Method: 2 monkeys, 28 teeth. Roots placed in grooves — one side facing bone, other facing gingival CT.

Result: Gingival CT side → root resorption. Bone side → ankylosis.

Proved: Gingival CT cells cause root resorption and MUST be excluded.

Study 3 — Nyman, Karring & Lindhe 1982 (J Clin Perio 9: 257) — THE BREAKTHROUGH:

Method: 3 monkeys. Bone removed mid-root to 2mm apical of crest. Root surfaces curetted. Millipore filter placed over defects — blocked epithelium and gingival CT. Only PDL cells and bone cells could reach root.

Result: New cementum with inserting collagen fibers on curetted root surface — TRUE NEW ATTACHMENT confirmed histologically. Controls → LJE only.

Proved: PDL cells possess the ability to reestablish CT attachment. The barrier concept works.

Study 4 — Nyman et al. 1986 (J Clin Perio 13: 145):

ESSAY 2 — Q1: Define GTR. What is the ultimate goal?

Opening: The ultimate goal in periodontics is to reestablish a periodontium to its original state of health — moving from disease to UNCOMPROMISED health, not merely compromised health.

Four key terms (define each):

1. Reattachment — reconnecting surgically separated HEALTHY tissue. Routine after flap surgery at edges beyond the defect.

2. Repair — long junctional epithelium (LJE). The body's DEFAULT healing. Epithelium migrates down root, adheres with hemidesmosomes. No CT fiber insertion. Protective but not ideal.

3. New Attachment — CT fibers inserting into PREVIOUSLY DISEASED root surface. The goal of GTR.

4. Regeneration — the biologic process by which the architecture and function of lost tissue is COMPLETELY returned: new cementum + new PDL + new bone (Melcher, 1969).

GTR Definition: Allowing certain cell types to participate in the healing process while EXCLUDING other types, for the purpose of promoting new attachment. A physical barrier blocks fast cells (epithelium + gingival CT) → gives PDL cells time to migrate coronally → new cementum + new PDL + new bone = regeneration.

Assessment methods: Probe (unreliable), radiograph (shows bone not CT), re-entry (see bone directly), histology = ONLY definitive method (Cite: Nyman 1982 — histologic confirmation of new cementum with inserting collagen fibers).

ESSAY 2 — Q2: Describe the landmark studies that led to the development of GTR.

Study 1 — Karring, Nyman & Lindhe 1980 (J Clin Perio 7: 96-105):

Method: 3 beagle dogs, 12 teeth with induced periodontitis to half root length. Roots extracted and implanted into bone cavities — epithelial migration prevented, only bone cells could contact diseased root.

Result: No new CT attachment — root resorption and ankylosis.

Proved: Bone cells alone CANNOT produce new attachment.

Study 2 — Nyman et al. 1980 (J Clin Perio 7: 39):

Result: Gingival CT side → root resorption. Bone side → ankylosis.

Proved: Gingival CT cells cause root resorption and MUST be excluded.

Study 3 — Nyman, Karring & Lindhe 1982 (J Clin Perio 9: 257) — THE BREAKTHROUGH:

Method: 3 monkeys. Bone removed mid-root to 2mm apical of crest. Root surfaces curetted. Millipore filter placed over defects — blocked epithelium and gingival CT. Only PDL cells and bone cells could reach root.

Result: New cementum with inserting collagen fibers on curetted root surface — TRUE NEW ATTACHMENT confirmed histologically. Controls → LJE only.

Proved: PDL cells possess the ability to reestablish CT attachment. The barrier concept works.

Essay 4 Framework — Occlusal Trauma

ESSAY 4 Q1 What is trauma from occlusion?
Hit these points:
- AAP 2001 definition: pathologic or adaptive changes in the periodontium from undue force
- TFO = the INJURY (lesion in attachment apparatus), not the force itself
- Pathologic lesion → loss of lamina dura → PDL widening → increasing mobility over time
- The diagnostic criterion is INCREASING mobility documented across appointments
- Distinguish from pathologic tooth wear (wear ≠ TFO — different thresholds)
- Overloading can break down TMJ, tooth, or attachment apparatus — TFO specifically = attachment apparatus injury
Cite: AAP Glossary of Periodontal Terms 2001 — official definition.
ESSAY 4 Q2 How do you diagnose the different types of trauma from occlusion in a clinical setting? What are you looking for exactly?
Hit these points:
- Primary TFO: EXCESSIVE force + NORMAL support. Look for: increasing mobility, migration, fremitus. Radiographic: widened PDL, possible angular bone loss appearance (don't confuse with perio disease). Lesion is reversible
- Secondary TFO: NORMAL force + WEAKENED support (from perio disease). Must have prior attachment loss. Usually NOT reversible. Tooth can't withstand even normal forces
- For both: MUST document increasing mobility over time (serial measurements)
- Key: you can have primary TFO even on a tooth WITH attachment loss (if force is also excessive)
Cite: Reinhart, Pao, Krejeci 1984 — PDL stresses in initiation of occlusal traumatism. J Perio Res 19:238.
ESSAY 4 Q3 Describe the relationship of periodontal breakdown from periodontal disease with and without occlusal trauma.
Hit these points:
- TFO alone: NO attachment loss. Only affects attachment apparatus, not supracrestal soft tissues
- Perio disease alone: attachment loss occurs from plaque-induced inflammation
- TFO + active perio infection: CO-DESTRUCTION (Glickman) — heavy force + active infection exacerbates destructive rate. Often seen in ortho context
- Healthy reduced periodontium + TFO: NORMAL adaptive response — no different from TFO on healthy full periodontium. Animal studies confirm. Ortho is safe after successful perio treatment
- The four scenarios grid: (1) TFO alone, (2) Perio alone, (3) TFO + active perio, (4) Treated perio + TFO
Cite: Glickman — Zone of Irritation vs Zone of Co-Destruction (unproven but tested framework).
ESSAY 4 Q4 Describe the treatment for the types of occlusal trauma.
Hit these points:
- Three-step approach: A) Eliminate inflammation first (always), B) Control occlusal etiology, C) Develop stable arch relationships
- Primary TFO: Reduce the force → occlusal equilibration (selective grinding), ortho, occlusal splint. Lesion heals, PDL returns to normal, mobility reduces. Reversible
- Secondary TFO: Compensate for lost support → SPLINTING (distribute forces across multiple teeth). Equilibration alone won't work — forces are already normal. Usually not reversible
- Treatment may necessitate: equilibration, limited/complete orthodontics, prosthetics/splinting
- Posterior Bite Collapse: syndrome (not disease) — missing posterior → super-eruption → drifting → loss of posterior support → anterior flaring → additional attachment loss. Prevent with prosthetic rehabilitation
- Caveat: adverse environment does NOT guarantee TFO — individual resistance and repair capacity vary
Key: always treat inflammation first, then address the occlusal component.
ESSAY 4 Q5 Is mobility disease? Does mobility always need to be treated? If not, why not? When does mobility need to be treated?
Hit these points:
- NO — mobility is a clinical finding, not a disease
- A certain increase in mobility can be accepted if tooth doesn't disturb function or comfort
- INCREASED ≠ INCREASING. Increased = stable, adapted, no tx needed. Increasing = progressive, pathologic, needs tx
- Treat when: (1) mobility is INCREASING (pathologic), (2) interferes with masticatory function, (3) causes patient discomfort
- Don't treat when: mobility is stable (increased but not increasing), tooth functions adequately, patient is comfortable
- Mobility grading: Physiologic ≤0.2mm, Grade 1: 0.2-1mm, Grade 2: 1-2mm, Grade 3: >2mm + vertical
Key concept: the trajectory matters more than the measurement. Document over time.
ESSAY 4 Q6 Distinguish between mobility and fremitus.
Hit these points:
- Mobility: Side-to-side movement when pushed with instruments. Passive test. Tests PDL width/integrity (the RESULT of TFO or adaptation)
- Fremitus: Vibration/movement detected by finger on facial surface while patient taps teeth in function. Functional test. Tests whether tooth receives excessive force DURING occlusion (potential CAUSE of TFO)
- A tooth can have mobility WITHOUT fremitus (wide PDL but no occlusal interference)
- A tooth can have fremitus WITHOUT significant mobility (prematurity on stable tooth)
- Primary TFO presents with: increasing mobility + migration + fremitus (all three)
Key: different tests, different things measured, different clinical significance.
ESSAY 4 Q7 Does trauma from occlusion cause attachment loss? Please explain.
Hit these points:
- NO. TFO does NOT cause attachment loss, pocketing, or recession
- Effects of occlusal force are LIMITED to the attachment apparatus (PDL + bone below crest)
- Supracrestal soft tissues (JE + CT attachment) are NOT damaged by excessive force
- The gingival soft tissues coronal to bone are NOT affected by TFO
- TFO does NOT initiate periodontal disease
- Distinguish: attachment apparatus (cementum + PDL + bone = what TFO affects) vs attachment (JE + CT = what TFO does NOT affect)
- TFO can cause bone remodeling, PDL widening, increased mobility — but CANNOT break the supracrestal tissue seal
- Only plaque-induced inflammation can cause attachment loss
This is the conceptual core of Essay 4. The distinction between attachment apparatus and supracrestal attachment is everything.
