Key Terms — Everything Explained

OFP — Orofacial Pain
Any pain in the face, mouth, or jaws.
This is the umbrella term for ALL pain in the face region. A toothache is OFP. Jaw soreness is OFP. A burning tongue is OFP. Migraine that hits your face is OFP. Our whole presentation is about how there are many different CAUSES of orofacial pain, and if you only check for one cause (like TMD), you miss the others.
TMD — Temporomandibular Disorders
Pain and dysfunction of the jaw muscles and the jaw joint (TMJ).
TMD is the most common non-dental cause of face pain. It makes your jaw hurt, your muscles sore, and can cause clicking, locking, or limited opening. The pain is usually a dull ache in the cheek/temple/jaw area that gets worse when you chew, yawn, or clench. It's diagnosed by history + pressing on the muscles and joints to reproduce the pain. TMD matters in our presentation because screening tools are really good at detecting it — but they mistake other types of face pain for TMD too.
DC/TMD — Diagnostic Criteria for TMD
The gold-standard clinical exam protocol for diagnosing TMD, published in 2014.
This is the "official" way to diagnose TMD. A trained examiner asks about your pain history, then presses on your jaw muscles and joints, asks you to open wide, and checks if those maneuvers reproduce your familiar pain. It's considered the best reference standard — meaning when studies want to know if a screener works, they compare the screener's results against what the DC/TMD exam finds. Throughout our slides, when we say "validated against DC/TMD," we mean the screener was checked against this thorough hands-on exam.
PIFP — Persistent Idiopathic Facial Pain
Chronic face pain where no cause can be found despite thorough examination.
This is the diagnosis you give when everything else has been ruled out. The patient has daily face pain for months, but their teeth are fine, their jaw joint is fine, their nerves are fine, their sinuses are fine. Nothing explains it. It's called "idiopathic" because we don't know why it happens. The problem? Because it's a diagnosis of exclusion (you can only diagnose it by ruling everything else out first), patients who DON'T get a full workup never get this diagnosis. Instead, they get misdiagnosed with "dental pain" and undergo unnecessary root canals and extractions. That's why Slides 3 and our PICO exist — these patients need a FULL assessment, not a narrow one.
AO — Atypical Odontalgia (also called Persistent Dentoalveolar Pain)
A type of PIFP where the pain feels like it's coming from a tooth or tooth socket, but there's nothing actually wrong with the tooth.
Imagine having a constant toothache — but every X-ray, every test, every exam shows the tooth is perfectly healthy. That's AO. Because the pain genuinely feels dental, patients go to the dentist, get a root canal, still hurt, get the tooth extracted, STILL hurt. The Xiao study (Study 5) found 73% of AO patients had unnecessary dental procedures done to them. This is the condition that most powerfully argues for our PICO: if dentists only do a dental exam or TMD exam, they'll never figure out AO. Only a complete assessment that rules out ALL possible causes can arrive at this diagnosis.
Sensitivity
The test's ability to correctly identify people who HAVE the condition.
If 100 people have TMD, and the screener catches 85 of them, the sensitivity is 85%. High sensitivity means the test rarely misses sick people. In our slides, most screeners have HIGH sensitivity (85-99%) for TMD — meaning they're good at catching TMD when it's there. The problem isn't sensitivity. The problem is specificity (see below). When you present, you can say: "The screener catches almost everyone who has TMD — that's sensitivity."
Specificity
The test's ability to correctly identify people who do NOT have the condition.
If 100 people DON'T have TMD, and the screener correctly says "no TMD" for 52 of them, the specificity is 52%. That means 48 people without TMD were told they might have it (false positives). Low specificity is the entire problem in our presentation. The TMD screener has 97% specificity in clean research settings but only 52% in real endodontic patients. Why? Because tooth pain and TMD pain FEEL the same to patients. They check the same boxes on the questionnaire for either condition. When you present, you can say: "The screener wrongly flags almost half of non-TMD patients — that's the specificity problem."
AUROC — Area Under the ROC Curve
A single number (0 to 1) that summarizes overall test accuracy, combining both sensitivity and specificity.
Think of it as a grade for the whole test. 0.5 = the test is no better than a coin flip (F grade). 0.7 = decent but not great (C+). 0.8 = good (B+). 0.9+ = excellent (A). In Slide 5, we show the same screener going from AUROC 0.98 (A+) in clean research to 0.71 (C+) in real endo patients. That dramatic drop IS our argument for why screeners aren't enough.
PPV and NPV — Predictive Values
PPV: if you test positive, what's the chance you actually have it? NPV: if you test negative, what's the chance you're actually clear?
These matter more in clinical practice than sensitivity/specificity because they answer what the PATIENT wants to know: "My test came back positive — do I actually have this?" A PPV of 68% means there's roughly a 2-in-3 chance you actually have TMD if you screened positive. An NPV of 75% means if you screened negative, there's a 75% chance you truly don't have TMD. On Slide 7, Daline found NPV 75% for the 6Q screener — meaning if a patient screens negative, the endodontist can feel fairly confident TMD isn't the issue.
3Q/TMD — Three Screening Questions for TMD
Three simple yes/no questions asked during routine dental checkups in Sweden.
The questions are: (1) Do you have pain in your temple, face, jaw, or jaw joint once a week or more? (2) Do you have pain weekly when you open your mouth or chew? (3) Does your jaw lock or get stuck weekly? If you answer yes to ANY of them, you're "3Q-positive" and should get a full TMD exam. In our presentation, we show this tool works great in general dental offices (Slide 4, Lovgren 2016) but loses accuracy in specialist pain clinics (Slide 6, Lovgren 2018) because specialist patients have more complex pain.
TMD Pain Screener (6Q / 3Q versions)
A 6-item questionnaire developed by Gonzalez in 2011 specifically to screen for painful TMD.
This is the main screening tool we track across Slides 4, 5, 6, and 7. It asks about: how long jaw/temple pain lasts, morning jaw stiffness, and whether 4 jaw activities (chewing, opening, clenching, talking) change the pain. Each answer gets 0, 1, or 2 points. Score of 3 or higher = positive screen for TMD. The key thing: it was developed and tested on people who were ALREADY sorted into "has TMD" and "doesn't have TMD" groups, with tooth pain patients excluded. When you take the same screener and give it to real-world patients whose pain hasn't been sorted yet, it gets much less accurate. That's the whole arc of Slides 4-7.
Myalgia & Arthralgia
Myalgia = muscle pain. Arthralgia = joint pain. Both are types of TMD.
In the jaw, myalgia means the chewing muscles (masseter, temporalis) are painful — they hurt when pressed or used. Arthralgia means the TMJ (the hinge joint in front of your ear) is inflamed and painful. In the Daline study (Slide 7), 72% of TMD patients had BOTH at the same time — meaning both their muscles AND their joints were in pain. This is important because it shows these patients had widespread masticatory system involvement that a tooth-focused exam would completely miss.

SLIDE 2 — OUR CLINICAL QUESTION (PICO) ~45 sec
SAY THIS
Here's the clinical question we set out to answer, structured in PICO format. • • • P — Population: patients who come in with orofacial pain. That could be a toothache, jaw soreness, face pain, anything in the facial region.
I — Intervention: performing a full clinical assessment — meaning you don't just check one thing. You check the teeth, the jaw muscles, the jaw joint, the nerves, and even psychosocial factors like stress and anxiety. The whole picture.
C — Comparison: doing only a TMD exam. Just checking the jaw muscles and joint, and nothing else.
O — Outcome: do you get a more accurate diagnosis of what's actually causing the pain? • • •
We're basically asking: if a patient walks in with face pain, do you get a better answer by looking at EVERYTHING, or by only looking at the jaw?

Understanding the PICO

PICO is just a way to organize a clinical question. P = who are we talking about, I = the approach we think is better, C = the approach we're comparing against, O = what we hope is different. It forces you to be specific about what you're studying.
"Full clinical assessment" = thorough pain history (when did it start, what makes it better/worse, what does it feel like) + dental exam (pulp testing, X-rays, percussion) + TMD exam (muscle palpation, joint assessment, range of motion) + neurological screening (cranial nerves, sensation testing) + psychosocial screen. Basically: everything.
"TMD exam alone" = ONLY palpating the jaw muscles and TMJ, checking jaw opening. Ignores the teeth, ignores the nerves, ignores possible idiopathic causes.

SLIDE 4 — CAN QUESTIONNAIRES CATCH TMD? ~1.5 min
SAY THIS
So the first thing we investigated: can screening questionnaires — simple paper forms patients fill out — identify who has TMD? We looked at four studies. • • • Gerstner 1994 — created a 13-question form about jaw pain and jaw function. When comparing TMD patients to healthy people with no symptoms, it worked beautifully: up to 98% sensitivity and 100% specificity. But when they compared TMD patients to people with tension-type headaches, the form couldn't tell them apart. Both groups answered the questions the same way.
Great at catching TMD versus perfectly healthy people. Useless at telling TMD from headache.
• • • Macfarlane 2004 — surveyed over 1,500 people in the UK, clinically examined 126 of them, and built a statistical model that could sort face pain into three broad categories: muscle/soft tissue pain (7%), tooth-related pain (7%), and nerve/blood vessel pain (6%). Useful for big population studies but not precise enough for individual diagnosis. • • • Lovgren 2016 — tested the 3Q/TMD — just three yes/no questions about weekly jaw pain, pain on chewing, and jaw locking — in over 7,800 adults at routine dental checkups in Sweden. 74% of people who screened positive actually had TMD when examined, versus only 16% of negatives. Quick, free, easy, and worked well for flagging who needs a closer look. • • • Gonzalez 2011 — this is the big one. Developed the TMD Pain Screener using rigorous psychometric methods across 504 participants. 99% sensitivity, 97% specificity. Near-perfect numbers. But — they deliberately excluded anyone with tooth pain from their main analysis. When they did check a separate group of patients with toothaches, 26–29% of them falsely screened positive for TMD. • • • The pattern across all four: screeners are excellent at catching TMD, but they consistently struggle to tell TMD apart from other types of face pain.
Questionnaires can detect TMD. But they can't tell you whether the pain is ACTUALLY from TMD or from something else that feels similar (tooth pain, headache, nerve pain).

All 4 Studies Simplified

Study 1 — Gerstner (1994)

13-question form. Tested on TMD patients, headache patients, and healthy people. Separated TMD from healthy easily but couldn't separate TMD from headache at all.

Shows the limitation exists from the very beginning: screeners are biased toward TMD and miss similar conditions.

MEDIUM — old study, but established the core problem early on
Study 2 — Macfarlane (2004)

UK population survey. Created a formula using 6 questionnaire answers to predict what type of face pain someone has. Moderate accuracy (kappa 0.5, which means agreement 50% beyond chance). Only study that tries to classify ALL orofacial pain types, not just TMD.

Shows questionnaires CAN sort pain types broadly but not precisely enough for individual patients.

MEDIUM
Study 3 — Lovgren (2016)

The 3Q/TMD validation in general dental practice. 7,831 people screened, 300 examined with DC/TMD. If you screened negative, there was a 92-99% chance you truly didn't have TMD (excellent negative predictive value). Very practical for a dental office wanting to know who to refer.

Best evidence that 3Q/TMD works in general practice for ruling OUT TMD. Sets up the contrast with Slide 6 where the same tool struggles in specialist settings.

HIGH
Study 8 — Gonzalez (2011)

THE original TMD Pain Screener paper. Used advanced psychometric methods (item response theory, factor analysis) to boil 49 pain questions down to the best 6. Tested against the expanded RDC-TMD protocol with calibrated examiners at 3 universities. The numbers (99% Se, 97% Sp) look incredible — but the study was designed with clearly separated groups and no tooth pain in the analysis.

This is the screener everyone cites. Its limitation — that it was validated in ideal conditions without tooth pain overlap — is the foundation of our entire Slide 5 argument.

The Three Studies Side by Side

Gonzalez (2011) — "Lab Conditions"

Groups were pre-selected: people KNOWN to have TMD vs people KNOWN to be healthy. Tooth pain excluded entirely. Of course the numbers are great — it's like testing a metal detector in a room where you already know where the metal is.

Alonso (2017) — "Getting Harder"

n=71. Now TMD patients vs patients with actual toothaches (confirmed by endodontist). The screener still catches TMD patients well (Se 92%), but it also incorrectly flags 41% of tooth pain patients as having TMD (Sp 59%). The two conditions just look too similar on paper.

Daline (2024) — "Real World"

n=100 consecutive endo patients. Nobody was pre-sorted. Many had BOTH conditions simultaneously. Sp fell to 52% — basically a coin flip for correctly clearing non-TMD patients. The examiner was dual-board-certified in both orofacial pain and endodontics, so the reference diagnosis was very trustworthy.

Why does this happen? Tooth pain and TMD pain both cause: aching in the jaw area, pain when chewing, pain on waking, and tenderness when you press. A 6-question paper form asks about exactly these things. The patient marks "yes" whether the cause is a tooth or a muscle. The form can't see inside the patient — only a clinical exam can.
SLIDE 6 — SCREENERS MEET THE REAL WORLD ~1.5 min
SAY THIS
Three more studies drive this point home. • • • Daline 2024 again — remember, 100 endo patients, 54% actually had TMD even though they all came in saying "my tooth hurts." The screener alone: AUROC 0.71, which is decent but not great. But here's the interesting part: when they added just one more piece of clinical information — asking the patient to rate their current pain intensity on a 0-to-10 scale — accuracy jumped to 0.81. That one simple question helped the tool sort patients much better. The message: even small amounts of real clinical data improve screening dramatically.
Over half of patients who came in for a root canal evaluation actually had jaw muscle/joint pain. A simple screener plus a pain rating could flag most of them before anyone picks up a drill.
• • • Lovgren 2018 — they took the same 3Q/TMD questions from 2016 and used them in a specialist orofacial pain clinic in Amsterdam with 449 referred patients. These are tougher cases — patients sent by other doctors because their pain is hard to figure out. Sensitivity stayed high (0.83-0.94), but specificity dropped dramatically to 0.41-0.55. Why? Because patients referred to a specialist pain clinic often have things like nerve damage, atypical odontalgia, or chronic dental pain — conditions where they genuinely hurt in the jaw area but it's NOT TMD. They all answer "yes" to the pain questions. However, when patients answered "no" to all three questions, TMD was reliably ruled out — that's still clinically useful. • • • Alonso's thesis at University of Minnesota tested two different screeners side by side — one for dental pain, one for TMD — in patients with confirmed tooth pain versus confirmed TMD. Neither screener had good enough specificity to reliably tell the two conditions apart. • • • We call this the Screening Paradox: these questionnaires are sensitive — they catch TMD patients well. But because orofacial pain conditions overlap so heavily in their symptoms, a positive screen still can't tell you the exact diagnosis. You need a hands-on, comprehensive clinical assessment to get there.
The Screening Paradox: the test goes off, but you still don't know what's causing the alarm. Screeners detect. Only full workups diagnose.

If You Get Questions

  • "What exactly is the DC/TMD exam?" — It's the gold-standard clinical protocol for diagnosing TMD. A trained examiner takes a pain history, then physically tests jaw opening, lateral movements, and presses on 20 muscle sites and 4 joint sites to see if they reproduce the patient's familiar pain. Published in 2014, used worldwide in research and clinical settings.
  • "Why is specificity so low in endo patients?" — Because tooth pain and TMD pain produce the same symptoms from the patient's perspective. Both cause aching in the jaw area, both hurt when you chew, both can cause morning stiffness. A paper questionnaire can't physically distinguish where pain originates — only a hands-on exam can do that.
  • "So what should a dentist actually DO?" — Use the 6-question TMD Pain Screener plus a pain intensity rating (0-10) as part of the intake paperwork. If the patient screens positive, do a focused TMD exam (palpate muscles, check jaw range of motion) before committing to any irreversible dental procedure. If the dental exam is completely negative, consider referring to an orofacial pain specialist rather than treating empirically.
  • "What's the difference between myalgia and arthralgia?" — Myalgia = muscle pain (the big chewing muscles: masseter and temporalis). Arthralgia = joint pain (the TMJ itself, the hinge in front of your ear). Having both means the entire chewing system is involved, not just one structure.
  • "Why does the 8% matter so much if it's a small number?" — Because the consequences are irreversible. A root canal on a healthy tooth leads to retreatment, then extraction, then implant — all for a tooth that was fine. And the patient STILL has pain because the real cause (TMD) was never treated. Multiply 8% by the millions of root canals done annually and it's a huge number of patients.

Your Anchor Study

Study 7 — Daline et al. (2024) • J Endodontics • UNC Chapel Hill

100 consecutive patients seeking endodontic treatment. All had tooth pain in the last 30 days. Cross-sectional design. The examiner was board-certified in BOTH orofacial pain AND endodontics, and was blinded to screener results. Every patient got a full DC/TMD exam AND a full endodontic workup (cold test, electric pulp test, percussion, periapical radiographs, the works).

Key results: 54% had TMD. 46% had both endo and TMD. 20% — TMD contributed to chief complaint. 8% — TMD was the only cause. 72% of TMD patients had both muscle AND joint pain. Screener alone: AUROC 0.71. With pain intensity: AUROC 0.81. Authors conclude: a positive screen MUST be followed by comprehensive TMD examination.
