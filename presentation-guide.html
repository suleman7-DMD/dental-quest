Key Terms You Must Know

OFP = Orofacial Pain — any pain in the face, mouth, or jaws
TMD = Temporomandibular Disorders — pain/dysfunction of jaw muscles & TMJ
DC/TMD = Diagnostic Criteria for TMD — the gold-standard clinical exam protocol (2014)
PIFP = Persistent Idiopathic Facial Pain — chronic face pain with NO identifiable cause (diagnosis of exclusion)
AO = Atypical Odontalgia — phantom tooth pain; subtype of PIFP localized to teeth/sockets
Sensitivity = Can the test catch people who HAVE the disease? (true positive rate)
Specificity = Can the test correctly clear people who DON'T have it? (true negative rate)
PPV = Positive Predictive Value — if you test positive, what's the chance you actually have it?
NPV = Negative Predictive Value — if you test negative, what's the chance you're actually clear?
AUROC = Area Under the ROC Curve — overall accuracy score (0.5 = coin flip, 0.7–0.8 = good, >0.9 = excellent)
3Q/TMD = Three yes/no screening questions for TMD (Swedish, used in routine dental checkups)
TMD Pain Screener = 6-item (or 3-item) validated questionnaire by Gonzalez et al. (part of DC/TMD system)
RCT = Root Canal Treatment (endodontic context, not randomized controlled trial here)

Think of sensitivity like a metal detector — does it beep when metal is there? Specificity = does it stay quiet when there's no metal? High sensitivity + low specificity = the detector beeps at everything, including bottle caps.

SLIDE 2 — Our Clinical Question (PICO)   (~45 seconds)
Your Script
Here's our clinical question in PICO format.

Population: patients presenting with orofacial pain — that's any pain in the face, mouth, or jaws.
Intervention: a full clinical assessment — meaning a comprehensive workup that looks at dental, TMD, neurological, and musculoskeletal causes.
Comparison: a TMD exam alone — a limited, isolated evaluation.
Outcome: more accurate diagnosis of the actual pain etiology.

So the question is: In patients with orofacial pain, does performing a full clinical assessment, compared to a TMD exam alone, lead to a more accurate diagnosis?

PICO explained simply

PICO is a framework for asking clinical research questions. P = who are we studying, I = what's the new approach, C = what's the current/standard approach, O = what do we hope improves.

"Full clinical assessment" = history + dental exam + TMD exam + cranial nerve screen + neurovascular check + psychosocial factors. Basically everything.

"TMD exam alone" = just checking the jaw muscles and TMJ, ignoring other possible causes of the pain.

SLIDE 3 — Why This Matters   (~1 minute)
Your Script
Before we look at the evidence, let me show you why this question matters clinically. These are real numbers from our literature review.

Patients with persistent idiopathic facial pain and atypical odontalgia wait an average of nearly 35 months — almost 3 years — before getting a correct diagnosis.

One hundred percent of these patients received at least one incorrect diagnosis before the right one.

And 73% of atypical odontalgia patients underwent unnecessary, irreversible dental procedures — root canals, extractions — on teeth that were never the problem.

The core issue is this: orofacial pain is not one disease. It spans odontogenic, TMD, neuropathic, vascular, and idiopathic causes. When clinicians rely on a narrow exam — just TMD, or just dental pathology — conditions like PIFP, atypical odontalgia, and tension-type headache get missed. Patients cycle through multiple providers, getting ineffective medications and irreversible procedures, while their pain persists.
Narrow exams cause years of misdiagnosis, unnecessary surgery, and patient suffering.

HIGH WEIGHT — largest study on PIFP misdiagnosis
Study 4 — Hassona et al. (2019)

34 PIFP patients in Jordan. 19.3 month avg delay. 3.7 providers consulted. 73.5% unnecessary procedures. Intraoral pain = longer delay (22.6 vs 16.1 months). None were ever referred to an OFP specialist.

SLIDE 4 — Can Questionnaires Catch TMD?   (~1.5 minutes)
Your Script
So our first question was: can screening questionnaires identify TMD? We reviewed four studies.

Gerstner 1994 developed a 13-item questionnaire. It showed excellent numbers — up to 98% sensitivity and 100% specificity — for separating TMD patients from healthy controls. But it could not differentiate TMD from tension-type headache. Great at catching TMD, blind to non-TMD causes.

Macfarlane 2004 used a population-based approach with over 1,500 people in the UK. Using six questionnaire variables, they built a statistical model that sorted OFP into three broad categories: musculoligamentous, dentoalveolar, and neurological/vascular. Useful for epidemiology, but needs further clinical validation.

Lovgren 2016 validated the 3Q/TMD — three simple yes/no questions about weekly jaw pain, pain on function, and jaw locking — in over 7,800 Swedish adults. 74% of positives actually had TMD versus only 16% of negatives. Simple, cost-effective, valid for flagging.

Gonzalez 2011 developed the TMD Pain Screener — both 3-item and 6-item versions. In validation: 99% sensitivity, 97% specificity, AUC 0.98–0.99. Near perfect. But — they excluded tooth pain patients entirely. When they separately tested an odontogenic pain group, 26–29% scored positive. That's a significant false-positive rate.

The key pattern: screening tools excel at detecting TMD, but they struggle to differentiate it from non-TMD orofacial pain.

Studies on this slide

Study 1 — Gerstner (1994)

13-item questionnaire. n=307 (TMD, headache, controls). Great at TMD vs healthy. Failed at TMD vs headache. ROC analysis.

MEDIUM — foundational but old, no gold-standard reference
Study 2 — Macfarlane (2004)

UK population survey, n=1510. Used multinomial logistic regression with 6 predictors. Could sort OFP into 3 broad types. Kappa=0.5 (moderate).

MEDIUM — population-based but moderate accuracy, needs validation
Study 3 — Lovgren (2016)

7,831 Swedish adults screened with 3Q/TMD at routine checkups. 300 examined with DC/TMD. 74% of positives had TMD. NPV excellent (0.92–0.99) in general practice.

HIGH — large real-world sample, validated against DC/TMD
Study 8 — Gonzalez (2011)

The ORIGINAL TMD Pain Screener paper. n=504. Psychometric development from 49 items. 99% Se, 97% Sp. AUC 0.98–0.99. But excluded tooth pain patients from primary analysis. 26–29% false positive in odontogenic group.

HIGH — gold standard development paper, but clean-population limitation is critical for our argument
3Q/TMD = 3 questions: (1) Weekly pain in temple/face/jaw? (2) Weekly pain on opening/chewing? (3) Weekly jaw locking? Answering yes to any = "3Q-positive."
TMD Pain Screener = 6 questions: pain duration, jaw stiffness on waking, and whether 4 jaw activities change the pain. Score 0–7. Cutoff of 3+ = positive screen.
SLIDE 5 — The Specificity Problem   (~1.5 minutes) — MOST IMPORTANT SLIDE
Your Script
This is the most important table in our presentation. We're tracking the same 6-question TMD Pain Screener across three studies with three different populations.

Gonzalez 2011 — clean research groups, pre-sorted diagnoses, tooth pain excluded: 99% sensitivity, 97% specificity, AUROC 0.98–0.99. Beautiful numbers.

Fonseca Alonso 2017 — now we put TMD patients head-to-head against odontogenic pain patients. Same screener. Sensitivity stays decent at ~92%, but specificity crashes to ~59%. Why? Because the symptom overlap between tooth pain and TMD is enormous. Patients can't tell the difference, and neither can the questionnaire.

Daline 2024 — real-world endodontic patients with actual toothaches. Sensitivity 85%, specificity drops to 52%, AUROC 0.71. And 46% of these patients had DUAL etiology — both endo AND TMD pain at the same time.

The takeaway: as patient complexity increases, specificity collapses. This screener was designed in clean populations. In the real world where patients have overlapping pain, it loses nearly half its specificity. A positive screen means "this could be TMD, or it could be tooth pain mimicking TMD." Only a full clinical assessment can resolve that ambiguity. A screener flags — it doesn't diagnose.
Same test, different populations = dramatically different accuracy. Specificity collapses from 97% to 52% when real patients replace research subjects.

The three studies compared

Gonzalez (2011) — "Clean"

n=504. Groups were pre-sorted: TMD pain, healthy controls, nonpainful TMJ, headache. Tooth pain excluded. Se 99%, Sp 97%.

HIGH quality but artificial conditions
Alonso (2017) — "Mixed"

n=71. TMD vs odontogenic only. Se ~92%, Sp ~59%. Patients with dual diagnoses excluded. Still, overlap killed specificity.

MEDIUM — small sample, thesis, but clinically revealing
Daline (2024) — "Real World"

n=100 consecutive endo patients. Se 85%, Sp 52%, AUROC 0.71. 54% had TMD. 46% had BOTH endo + TMD. Examiner was dual-board-certified (OFP + endo).

HIGH — most clinically relevant, consecutive real patients
Why does specificity drop? Because tooth pain and TMD pain feel similar to patients. They both cause aching in the jaw area, pain on chewing, and pain on waking. Patients endorse the screener items even when the problem is purely dental.
AUROC 0.71 = "useful" accuracy (like a C+ grade). 0.81 (with added pain intensity) = "very good" (B+). The original 0.98 was like an A+, but only in an artificial setting.
SLIDE 6 — Screeners Meet the Real World   (~1.5 minutes)
Your Script
Three more studies reinforce this.

Daline 2024 again — 100 endo patients, 54% actually had painful TMD but came in for a "toothache." The screener alone had useful but limited accuracy. But here's the interesting part: when they added just one more data point — the patient's current pain intensity rating — AUROC jumped from 0.71 to 0.81. Even a small addition of clinical information meaningfully improves the screen. The takeaway here is that over half of "toothaches" in endo may actually be TMD. A screener can flag them before irreversible treatment.

Lovgren 2018 — same 3Q/TMD questions from 2016, but now tested in a specialist OFP clinic in Amsterdam with 449 referred patients. Sensitivity stayed high, but specificity dropped dramatically because these referred patients have neuropathic pain, atypical odontalgia, chronic dental pain — they all answer "yes" to pain questions but DON'T have TMD. However, negative responses reliably ruled OUT TMD, which is clinically useful.

Alonso's thesis at University of Minnesota tested both the dental pain questionnaire and the TMD screener head-to-head in odontogenic versus TMD patients. Neither had acceptable specificity. The symptom overlap was just too great.

This is what we call the Screening Paradox: screeners are sensitive — they catch TMD well. But orofacial pain etiologies overlap so much that a positive screen still can't tell you the exact diagnosis. A comprehensive clinical assessment remains essential for definitive differential diagnosis.

Studies on this slide

Study 7 — Daline (2024)

Screener + pain intensity NRS = AUROC 0.81. Key stat: 8% of endo patients had TMD as the SOLE source of their "tooth pain." They had nothing wrong with the tooth.

HIGH — first to validate screener IN endo patients with tooth pain
Study 9 — Lovgren (2018)

3Q/TMD in specialist Amsterdam clinic. n=449 referred patients. Se 0.83–0.94 but Sp only 0.41–0.55. Q3 (locking): Se 0.48 but Sp 0.96. In specialist settings, screeners direct the differential, not confirm diagnosis.

HIGH — shows population-dependence of diagnostic accuracy
Study 11 — Alonso (2016 thesis)

n=71 (34 odontogenic, 37 TMD). DePaQ: Se 85%, Sp 11%. TMD Screener: Se 92%, Sp 59%. Neither acceptable for differentiating the two conditions.

MEDIUM — small thesis study, but directly tests our question
The Screening Paradox in plain English: A screener is like a smoke detector. It goes off when there's smoke, but it can't tell you if it's a kitchen fire, a candle, or burnt toast. You still need to go look. Same thing here — the screener says "something's going on with the jaw area" but can't distinguish TMD from dental pain from PIFP.
SLIDE 7 — The Endodontic Blind Spot   (~1.5 minutes) — YOUR CLOSER
Your Script
I want to end my section with what I think is the most clinically striking finding. This is Daline 2024 from UNC — 100 consecutive patients seeking endodontic treatment for tooth pain.

54% had painful TMD on DC/TMD exam. More than half.
46% had DUAL etiology — both endodontic AND TMD pain at the same time.
In 20%, TMD contributed to their chief complaint.
And in 8%, TMD was the sole source of their "tooth pain." There was nothing wrong with the tooth.

Think about what that means clinically. Without a TMD assessment, an endodontist performing a root canal on that 8% will see a "failed" root canal — not because the procedure failed, but because the wrong pain source was treated.

Even at AUROC 0.81 — the best accuracy they could achieve — the study explicitly states that a positive screen must be followed by a comprehensive DC/TMD examination. The screener is a triage tool, not a diagnosis.

And one more number: 72% of TMD-positive patients had BOTH myalgia AND arthralgia — meaning chronic, multi-structural masticatory pain that a tooth-focused exam would never detect.

This is the endodontic blind spot — and it perfectly sets up why a full clinical assessment, not just a dental workup, not just a TMD check, is the only way to get the diagnosis right.

I'll hand it off to [next person] who will walk you through the clinical exam evidence and the human cost data.
54% of "toothache" patients actually had TMD. 8% had nothing wrong with the tooth at all. Without a full assessment, you treat the wrong thing.

The key study

Study 7 — Daline et al. (2024)

J Endodontics, UNC Chapel Hill. 100 patients, cross-sectional. Examiner was board-certified in BOTH OFP and endodontics. DC/TMD exam + full endo workup on every patient. Blinded to screener results.

54% TMD prevalence. 46% dual etiology. 20% TMD contributed to chief complaint. 8% TMD was sole source.

72% of TMD+ patients had both myalgia AND arthralgia (multi-structural).

Screener alone: AUROC 0.71. With pain intensity: 0.81. Study says positive screen still requires full DC/TMD exam.

HIGHEST WEIGHT in your section — most clinically impactful, real consecutive patients, dual-board examiner
Myalgia = muscle pain in the masticatory muscles. Arthralgia = pain in the TMJ itself. Having BOTH means the whole masticatory system is involved, not just one structure.
Why 8% matters so much: If you do a root canal on a tooth that has no pathology, the patient still has pain afterward. They come back. You retreat. Still pain. Eventually the tooth gets extracted. Still pain. This is the "diagnostic odyssey" — all because the jaw was never examined.

Potential Q&A

  • "What's DC/TMD?" — Gold-standard criteria from 2014. Requires: (1) jaw pain in last 30 days, (2) pain modified by function, (3) familiar pain reproduced on opening or palpation.
  • "Why is specificity so low in endo?" — Patients can't distinguish tooth vs jaw pain. Both cause aching, both hurt on chewing, both cause morning stiffness. The questionnaire items overlap.
  • "What should we do clinically?" — Use 6Q screener + pain NRS as intake triage. If positive, do DC/TMD exam BEFORE any irreversible procedure. If dental workup is negative, expand differential to PIFP, neuropathic pain, etc.
