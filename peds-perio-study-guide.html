🦷 Pediatric Periodontology – Comprehensive Exam Study Guide

Peds Dentistry | D3 Spring 2026 | Dr. Pani | AAP/2017 Classification

I. Core Concepts & Big Picture

  • Gingivitis is found in early childhood; prevalence peaks at puberty
  • Advanced forms of periodontal disease are generally an adult problem, BUT aggressive periodontitis is found in teenagers
  • Periodontal disease may increase during adolescence due to lack of motivation for oral hygiene
  • The pediatric periodontium is in constant change due to exfoliation and eruption — what's "normal" varies with age (Baer & Benjamin, 1974)

The 4 Structures of the Periodontium

  1. Gingiva (free/marginal, attached, interdental)
  2. Periodontal Ligament (PDL)
  3. Root Cementum
  4. Alveolar Bone

III. Periodontal Changes Associated with Normal Tooth Development

A. Eruption Cyst

  • May appear during eruption of primary OR permanent teeth
  • Fluctuant mass, may be filled with blood
  • Bluish or deep red enlargement of gingiva over erupting tooth
  • Most common: primary first molars and permanent first molars

B. Primary Tooth Exfoliation

  • Sulcus depth increases as JE migrates down resorbing root
  • Minor traumatic changes: compression, ischemia, hyalinization of PDL
  • Changes in JE permeability → exfoliating tooth more susceptible to inflammation
  • Changes in occlusion; malalignment can contribute to periodontal trauma

C. Malocclusion & Periodontal Status

  • Association between teeth malposition/crowding and gingival inflammation
  • Can cause: gingival enlargement, discoloration, ulceration, deep pockets/pseudopockets
  • Gingival health can be restored with orthodontic correction
  • BUT: failure to align teeth does NOT necessarily affect periodontal disease later in life

D. Mucogingival Problems in Children

  • Prevalence: 1-19% of children
  • May start in primary dentition (developmental aberrations, thin periodontium)
  • High frenum attachment
  • Recession commonly seen: buccal of mandibular permanent incisors and maxillary canines (secondary to rotations/labial positioning from space problems)
  • Also from: orthodontic appliances, factitious habits

VI. Classification of Periodontal Diseases

💡 Context: The 2017 World Workshop updated the 1999 Armitage classification. Your professor ran through these quickly expecting you to have foundations. Here's what you need for the peds context.

A. Major Categories (2017 Classification)

  1. Periodontal Health & Gingival Diseases/Conditions
    • Gingival health (intact or reduced periodontium)
    • Gingivitis: dental biofilm-induced
    • Gingival diseases: non-dental biofilm induced
  2. Periodontitis (now a single category — no more "chronic" vs "aggressive" distinction in 2017, but your lecture teaches both frameworks)
  3. Necrotizing Periodontal Diseases (NUG, NUP)
  4. Periodontitis as a Manifestation of Systemic Disease
  5. Other Conditions: Abscesses, endo-perio lesions, mucogingival deformities, traumatic occlusal forces, tooth/prosthesis-related factors

⚠ KEY DISTINCTION — Chronic vs. Aggressive:
Chronic: Destruction consistent with local factors (lots of plaque/calculus = lots of damage). Slow progression. Adults mainly.
Aggressive: Destruction inconsistent with local factors (little plaque = lots of damage). Rapid. Young/healthy patients. Familial. A.a./P.g.

⚠ CLASSIC TEST QUESTION: Circumpubertal patient with bone loss around first molars and incisors, otherwise healthy, little plaque = Localized Aggressive Periodontitis. The first molar/incisor pattern is the hallmark.

VII. Dental Plaque-Induced Gingival Diseases

A. Gingivitis Associated with Dental Plaque Only

  • Gingival inflammation present; no bone loss or clinical attachment loss
  • Prevalence increases with age → peaks at puberty
  • Initial histologic change is NOT clinically detectable → signs become more obvious as it progresses
  • Begins at gingival margin, can spread throughout gingival unit
  • Clinical signs: changes in contour, color, and consistency
  • Local contributing factors: tooth anatomic factors, dental appliances/restorations, orthodontic appliances

B. Gingival Diseases Modified by Systemic Factors

ModifierKey Details
Puberty-associated gingivitis Increased sex hormones (progesterone, estrogen) → increased blood circulation to gingiva → inflammation may develop with very small amounts of plaque
Menstrual cycle-associated Gingival changes prior to ovulation
Diabetes mellitus-associated Impaired immune response; exaggerated inflammation
Leukemia-associated gingivitis Leukemic cell infiltration in gingiva; suppressed immune response; increased bleeding
💡 Puberty Gingivitis PEARL: The key concept is that hormonal changes cause an exaggerated inflammatory response to even small amounts of plaque. It's not that kids have more plaque at puberty — it's that the same amount of plaque causes a bigger reaction due to hormones.

C. Gingival Diseases Modified by Medications

Three drug classes cause drug-influenced gingival enlargement:

  1. Phenytoin (anti-convulsant)
  2. Cyclosporine (immunosuppressant)
  3. Calcium channel blockers (hypertension)
🧠 Memory Aid: "P-C-C" = Phenytoin, Cyclosporine, Calcium channel blockers → all cause gingival enlargement.

D. Gingival Diseases Modified by Malnutrition

Ascorbic acid deficiency (Vitamin C → Scurvy)

  • Weakness, anemia, bruising
  • Gingival bleeding and gingival enlargement

VIII. Non-Plaque-Induced Gingival Diseases

CategoryExamples & Key Details
Viral origin Herpes virus: primary herpetic gingivostomatitis, recurrent oral herpes, varicella-zoster
Genetic origin Hereditary gingival fibromatosis — proliferative fibrous overgrowth of attached gingiva, marginal gingiva, and interdental papilla
Systemic manifestations Erythema multiforme — A type of hypersensitivity reaction that occurs in response to medications, infections, or illness; the exact cause is unknown. The disorder is believed to involve damage to the blood vessels of the skin with subsequent damage to skin tissues. "target"/"iris"/"bull's eye" lesions; sudden onset; high recurrence; may have vesicles/bullae; usually symmetrical. ⚠ Erythema Multiforme Key Features: Target/bull's eye lesions, sudden onset, high recurrence, symmetrical distribution, vesicles/bullae possible. Stevens-Johnson = severe systemic form with extensive mucous membrane involvement.

IX. Necrotizing Periodontal Diseases (NUG & NUP)

NUG and NUP are possibly different stages of the same infection. Both involve diminished systemic resistance to bacterial infection. Key organisms: fusiform bacteria, spirochetes, P. intermedia.

Predisposing Factors Pathway (From Slides)

Psychological Stress & Anxiety + Pre-Existing Gingivitis

↓

Increased Corticosteroid Production → Immunosuppression

↓

Increased Bacteria Growth and Invasion

↓

NUG → NUP → Necrotizing Stomatitis → Noma

Also contributing: HIV Infection → Immunosuppression; Deficient Nutrition; Viral Infections

NUG — Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis
  • Fetid breath (hallmark)
  • Pain
  • Interdental necrosis — "punched-out" papillae
  • Gingival bleeding
  • Pseudomembrane formation
  • No bone loss / no CAL

Predisposing factors: emotional stress, poor diet, cigarette smoking, HIV infection

NUP — Necrotizing Ulcerative Periodontitis
  • Everything in NUG PLUS:
  • Bone loss
  • Clinical attachment loss (CAL)
  • Deep pockets
  • Soft tissue necrosis

Most commonly in: HIV infection, severe malnutrition, immunosuppression

⚠ NUG vs. NUP: The KEY difference is bone loss and CAL. NUG = gingival only (no bone loss). NUP = bone loss + CAL. Both have punched-out papillae, fetid breath, pain, pseudomembranes.

X. Abscesses of the Periodontium

General CLINICAL SIGNS AND SYMPTOMS (All Periodontal Abscesses)

  • Swelling, suppuration, visible redness
  • Extrusion of involved tooth, loosening
  • Tenderness to even slight percussion
TypeDefinitionKey Features
Gingival Abscess Localized purulent infection of the marginal gingiva or interdental papilla Localized, painful, rapidly expanding; acute inflammatory response to foreign bodies; red swelling with smooth, shiny surface
Periodontal Abscess Localized purulent infection in periodontal pocket Associated with existing periodontal disease / deep pockets
Pericoronal Abscess Localized purulent infection around crown of a partially erupted tooth Usually mandibular third molars; red swollen gingival flap; pain; difficulty swallowing; severity associated with increasing Gram-negative anaerobes
💡 Gingival Abscess Tip: Think foreign body (popcorn hull, etc.) → rapid, smooth, shiny, localized swelling on marginal gingiva or papilla. This is the classic scenario.

High-Yield Distinguishing Features

⚠ KNOW THESE ASSOCIATIONS:
• Palmar-plantar keratosis + severe periodontitis = Papillon-Lefèvre
• Oculocutaneous albinism + periodontitis = Chediak-Higashi
• Short lower incisor roots + early perio = Down Syndrome
• Absence of root cementum + premature exfoliation of anteriors = Hypophosphatasia
• Defective collagen + fragile mucosa + gingival recession = Ehlers-Danlos (vEDS, Vascular type)
• Severe early periodontitis + lack of attached gingiva + C1r serine protease = Ehlers-Danlos (pEDS, Periodontal type)
• Gingival fibrous overgrowth (no inflammation) = Hereditary Gingival Fibromatosis
• Rapidly progressive perio + commonly fatal = Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency
• Cyclical episodes + neutropenic episodes = Cyclic Neutropenia
• Resembles NUP + biopsy needed = Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis
• Decreased alkaline phosphatase = Hypophosphatasia
💡 Unifying Theme: Almost all these genetic conditions involve either (1) neutrophil dysfunction (neutropenia, impaired chemotaxis, impaired phagocytosis) or (2) connective tissue defects (collagen, cementum). The neutrophil-related ones tend to present with severe/aggressive periodontitis + ulcerations. The CT-related ones tend to present with fragility + premature tooth loss.

XII. Local Factors, Mucogingival Conditions & Tooth-Related Factors

Local Tooth-Related Factors

  • Tooth anatomic factors
  • Dental restorations/appliances
  • Cervical root resorption

Mucogingival Deformities and Conditions Around Teeth

  • Gingival recession (facial or lingual, interproximal)
  • Lack of keratinized tissue
  • Aberrant frenum/muscle position
  • Gingival excess (pseudopocket, inconsistent gingival margin, excessive display, gingival enlargement)
  • Decreased vestibular depth
  • Abnormal color

Occlusal Trauma

  • Primary occlusal trauma: excessive force on normal periodontium
  • Secondary occlusal trauma: normal force on weakened/reduced periodontium

XIII. Periodontal Management of Pediatric Patients

Periodontal Exam

  • Clinical/radiographic evaluation
  • Complete charting at full permanent dentition
  • Bleeding score, plaque score, periodontal scores

Management Protocol

  1. OHI (oral hygiene instruction) — foundation of everything
  2. Comprehensive periodontal exam as part of routine dental visits
  3. Scaling / debridement / polishing
  4. Scaling and root planning if indicated
  5. If advanced periodontitis observed → look for systemic disease; refer for medical evaluation
  6. Early diagnosis of aggressive periodontitis
  7. Assessment of habits (parafunctional, factitious injuries, smoking)
  8. Assess plaque-retentive restorations/appliances
  9. Caries control
  10. Regular recalls
⚠ CRITICAL: If you see advanced periodontitis in a child or teenager → it's NOT just "they don't brush." You must consider systemic disease and refer for medical evaluation. Early diagnosis of aggressive periodontitis is essential.

XIV. High-Risk Behaviors in Adolescents

A. Use of Tobacco

  • Associated with alcohol and illicit drug use; acts as "gateway drug"
  • People who begin smoking at an early age → more likely to develop severe addiction
  • Smokeless tobacco: can cause oral/pharyngeal cancers and noncancerous oral conditions; leads to nicotine addiction

Smokeless Tobacco — Four Distinct Clinical Lesions:

  1. Hyperkeratotic or erythroplakic lesions of oral mucosa
  2. Gingival inflammation or periodontal disease
  3. Combination of oral mucosal lesions + periodontal inflammation
  4. Cervical erosion of the teeth

B. Intraoral Jewelry (Piercings)

  • Increased risk for mucogingival problems (recession)
  • Increased risk for chipped teeth
  • Increased risk for intraoral infections (tongue)
  • Localized tissue overgrowth
  • Metal hypersensitivity
  • Nerve damage
  • Local inflammation

C. Dental Phobia

Important consideration when treating kids and adolescents — can lead to avoidance of care and worsening periodontal status.

D. Oral Hygiene Habits

Adolescents often lack motivation → periodontal disease may increase. Key consideration for management and counseling.

XV. Parent Advice — PREVENTION IS KEY

  • Establish good dental hygiene habits early: Brush child's teeth, toothpaste at 12 months; use floss when teeth in contact.
  • Serve as a good role model by practicing good dental hygiene habits themselves.
  • Schedule regular dental visits: family checkups, periodontal evaluations, and cleanings.
  • Check child's mouth for the signs of periodontal disease: bleeding gingiva, swollen and bright red gingiva, gingival recession, halitosis.
