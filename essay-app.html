PERIO II FINAL — ESSAYS

01 Crown Lengthening
Q1 — Explain biologic width in depth
Biologic width is the zone of root surface between the crest of alveolar bone and the base of the sulcus where the connective tissue and junctional epithelium attach. Gargiulo et al. (1961, J Periodont 42:8) measured this in a histologic study of human cadaver jaws. The averages: CT attachment ~1.07 mm, junctional epithelium ~0.97 mm, sulcus depth ~0.69 mm. Biologic width = CT + JE = ~2.04 mm. Total from bone crest to gingival margin = ~2.73 mm.

These are averages with significant variability — some patients have more, some less. You cannot predict one tooth's biologic width from adjacent teeth.

When a restorative margin is placed too close to the bone crest and invades this zone, the body responds with chronic inflammation and unpredictable bone resorption as it tries to re-establish its buffer. The clinical goal is 3-4 mm between the restorative margin and bone crest.
Q2 — Bleeding/ulceration upon temp removal: how to determine if CL is needed?
Biologic width violation is only ONE possible cause. Others include: excess subgingival cement, an overextended or ill-fitting temp, food impaction from lack of contact, and poor oral hygiene. All produce identical findings.

Next steps — process of elimination:
1. Remove all excess cement.
2. Evaluate the temp — if poorly adapted, remake it with proper contours and contacts.
3. Reinforce oral hygiene instruction.
4. Have the patient return in a few weeks.

If the tissue still shows inflammation after eliminating all other causes, biologic width violation is confirmed.

Radiographs are NOT reliable for diagnosing biologic width invasion — they only show ~50% of bone (interproximal). Buccal and lingual bone are invisible. Clinical assessment supersedes radiographic measurement.
Q2b — Indications and contraindications
Indications: Tooth decay at or below the gingival margin. Tooth fracture below the gingival margin (with adequate remaining support). Teeth with excessive wear. Teeth with insufficient interocclusal space. Delayed passive eruption.

Contraindications: Poor crown-to-root ratio. Loss of periodontal support from adjacent teeth. Location of the furcation. Inability to keep clean after restoration. Better alternative treatments exist. Poor long-term prognosis.
Q3 — Gingivectomy vs. flap with bone removal
Depends on two things: how much keratinized tissue (KT) is present, and where bone sits relative to the CEJ.

Lots of KT + bone at normal position (1-2 mm apical to CEJ) → Gingivectomy alone works.

Lots of KT + bone AT the CEJ → Gingivectomy alone will FAIL. Tissue regenerates when bone is right at the CEJ. Must raise a flap and reduce bone.

Minimal KT + bone at normal position → Apically positioned flap. Gingivectomy would remove all KT, creating a mucogingival problem.

Minimal KT + bone AT the CEJ → Most complex case. Requires both osseous surgery and careful tissue management.

In most crown lengthening situations, bone is too close, so most cases require a flap with osseous surgery.
Q4 — Healing time before restoration
After crown lengthening, tissue at the bone crest migrates coronally over time (creeping attachment / gingival rebound). Approximately 2 mm of coronal migration is expected.

Posterior: 6-8 weeks — margin position is less critical outside the esthetic zone.

Anterior: Minimum 3 months (up to 16 weeks) — exact gingival margin position determines the esthetic outcome.

If you restore too early: Tissue continues creeping coronally after the restoration is placed. The gingival margin covers the restoration margin, causing visible margin lines, esthetic failure, and potentially needing to redo the restoration.
Q5 — 3 complications and remedies
1. Bleeding: Rare with adequate suturing. Home: tea bag (tannin promotes coagulation). Office: additional sutures under local anesthesia.

2. Pain and swelling: NSAIDs reduce both inflammation and pain. Ice for at least 24 hours.

3. Root sensitivity: Exposed root surfaces with open dentin tubules. Week 1: periodontal pack protects the area. After pack removal: desensitizing agents (e.g., SuperSeal — potassium oxalate that occludes tubules). Long-term: the final restoration covers the exposed root.
REF: Gargiulo et al. 1961 (J Periodont 42:8) — biologic width measurements
02 Guided Tissue / Bone Regeneration
Q1 — Define and explain GTR
The ultimate goal in periodontics is to reestablish a periodontium to its original state of health. To achieve that, new attachment must take place. Regeneration = the biologic process by which the architecture and function of lost tissue is completely returned (Melcher, 1969). This is different from repair, which produces a long junctional epithelial adhesion — not true attachment.

After surgery, 4 tissue types compete to repopulate the root surface. Without interference, epithelium (fastest) wins and you get repair. GTR uses a physical barrier membrane placed over the bony defect to block the fast-migrating epithelium and gingival CT, giving the slower PDL cells time to repopulate and produce true regeneration — new cementum, new PDL, new bone.
Q2 — Major studies proving GTR
Karring et al. 1980: 3 dogs, 12 teeth with induced periodontitis. Roots implanted into bone cavities with epithelium blocked. Result: NO new CT attachment — only resorption and ankylosis. → Bone alone can't produce new attachment.

Nyman et al. 1980: 2 monkeys, 28 teeth. Roots implanted half in bone, half covered by gingival CT. Result: gingival CT side caused resorption. → Gingival CT must be excluded.

Nyman et al. 1982: 3 monkeys. Bone removed mid-root to 2 mm apical to marginal crest. Root surfaces curetted. Millipore filter placed as barrier. Result: new cementum with inserting collagen fibers. → PDL cells CAN re-establish attachment when given opportunity through barrier exclusion.

Isidor et al. 1986: 4 monkeys, angular defects. Tight elastic on half the teeth (blocks PDL migration), loose on rest. Tight = NO new attachment. Loose = new attachment. → PDL cells must migrate coronally for regeneration.
Q2a — Tissue interactions with root surface
Epithelium (fastest) → long junctional epithelium = repair, not regeneration.
Gingival CT (fast) → root resorption = most destructive outcome.
Bone cells (slowest) → ankylosis = bone fuses directly to root, no PDL space.
PDL cells (moderate) → new cementum + new collagen fibers = true new attachment. This is the goal.

The membrane blocks the two fast cell types while allowing PDL cells access from the defect base.
Q3 — Bone grafting materials
Osteoconductive = scaffold that facilitates new bone growth from existing bone. Osteoinductive = induces new bone from undifferentiated cells.

Autograft: Same patient. Intraoral (tuberosity, extraction sites) or extraoral (iliac crest). The ONLY graft with osteogenic + osteoinductive + osteoconductive properties. Cancellous > cortical for osteogenic potential. Disadvantage: second surgical site, limited volume. Iliac grafts can cause root resorption (controlled by freezing).

Allograft: Human cadaver bone. FDBA = mineralized, osteoconductive only. DFDBA = demineralized, theoretically osteoinductive (exposes BMPs) but reliability is inconsistent. Advantage: no second surgical site.

Xenograft: Another species. Bio-Oss (anorganic bovine bone) — processed at high temps >15 hours, prion-free. Osteoconductive only, excellent scaffold.

Alloplast: Synthetic (hydroxyapatite, tricalcium phosphate, calcium sulfate, bioactive glass). Inert biologic fillers, osteoconductive only. Unlikely to result in true regeneration.
Q4 — GBR vs GTR + when GBR is indicated
GTR = around a TOOTH. 4 cell types compete. Goal: new attachment (new cementum + PDL + bone). PDL cells are the key.

GBR = in EDENTULOUS areas. Only 3 cell types (no PDL). Goal: new bone formation only.

GBR indications: Immediate placement of implants, extraction sites, ridge augmentation procedures.
Q7 — Complications and remedies
Infection: Antibiotics, debridement, possible membrane removal.
Membrane exposure/perforation: Most common. Minor: trim material + CHX. Extensive: remove.
Recession or flap sloughing: Prevention = tension-free primary closure, proper flap design.
Gingival irregularities: Gingivoplasty after tissue maturation.

Membrane must be completely covered by flap, 2-3 mm below the coronal aspect. If not removable with a gentle tug, a small incision may be needed.
Q8 — Regenerative barrier types
Ideal barrier properties: withstands cellular ingrowth, nutrient/fluid diffusible, excellent handling, minimal tissue reaction, no allergic properties, resists infection, eventual absorption after repair.

Non-resorbable (Gore-Tex / ePTFE): Medical-grade stretched Teflon. Titanium-reinforced for space-making. Stabilized with tacks. Advantage: predictable, timing control. Disadvantage: second surgery to remove, infection risk.

Resorbable (collagen membranes, synthetic polymers): Advantage: no second surgery, more tissue compatible. Disadvantage: less predictable resorption timing.
Q9 — Growth factors, PRP, EMD, BMPs
PRP (Platelet Rich Plasma): Concentrates platelets by 330-900%. Contains PDGF, TGF-B, EGF/TGF-alpha, IGF-1. Improves wound healing and faster healing. Disadvantage: equipment needed, technique sensitive.

GEM 21 (rhPDGF-BB on beta-TCP): PDGF released from matrix, binds to cell receptors, triggers chemotaxis and mitogenesis of osteoblasts, PDL fibroblasts, and cementoblasts → new bone, new PDL, new cementum.

EMD (Enamel Matrix Derivative / Emdogain): From porcine tooth buds — contains amelogenins, ameloblastin, enamelin. PDL cells show enhanced protein production and proliferation. "Osteopromotive" when combined with DFDBA. NOT osteoinductive on its own.

BMPs (Bone Morphogenetic Proteins): Regulatory glycoproteins that differentiate mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts. Research focused on BMP-2, BMP-3 (osteogenin), BMP-7 (OP-1). Advantage: rapid new bone, truly osteoinductive. Disadvantage: exuberant inflammatory response.

New attachment can only be confirmed through histologic evaluation — not probing, radiographs, or re-entry surgery.
REF: Nyman et al. 1982 (J Clin Perio 9:257) — PDL regenerative potential / millipore filter study
03 Non-Surgical / Surgical Therapy
Q1 — Clinical/radiographic findings indicating SRP
SRP is indicated when you find: bleeding upon probing (active inflammation), increased probing depths associated with clinical attachment loss, radiographic bone loss, and subgingival calculus. A patient has periodontitis when one or more sites show BOP, increased PD with CAL, and possibly radiographic bone loss.
Q2 — Root planing vs. scaling
Scaling = removal of soft and hard deposits (plaque and calculus) from the tooth surface — crown and root.

Root planing = removal of diseased root surfaces to create a smooth, non-contaminated root surface. It removes contaminated cementum and has nothing to do with calculus. Two distinct procedures.
Q3 — Goals and evidence of success
The goal is to remove diseased root surface, disrupt the subgingival microbial flora, and create a non-contaminated surface that promotes healing.

Harper and Robinson (J Clin Perio, 1987) showed SRP reduces motile microbes and spirochetes, but this effect is temporary — bacteria recolonize. This is the fundamental rationale for 3-month maintenance.

SRP is both a definitive treatment for mild-to-moderate periodontitis AND an initial phase before surgery for more severe cases. Which role it served is determined at re-evaluation 4-6 weeks later.

Complete calculus removal is rarely attained and somewhat unrealistic. At depths of 5 mm+, clinicians inadequately debride root surfaces 65% of the time.
Q4 — When is additional therapy needed? Rationale for surgery?
At the 4-6 week re-evaluation after SRP, if sites still show persistent probing depths of 5 mm+ with BOP, additional therapy is indicated.

The objectives of periodontal surgery: (1) gain access for effective removal of etiologic factors following SRP, and (2) reduce pocket depth, making it more maintainable for the patient.
Q5 — Adjunctive antimicrobials + clinical examples
Since complete debridement of deep pockets is unrealistic, adjunctive antimicrobials help eliminate remaining bacteria.

Systemic: Periostat (20 mg doxycycline 2x/day) — sub-antimicrobial dose. Does NOT kill bacteria — inhibits matrix metalloproteinases, slowing tissue destruction. For aggressive disease: amoxicillin + metronidazole or azithromycin.

Local delivery (placed into non-responsive pockets at re-evaluation): Arestin (minocycline microspheres), PerioChip (2.5 mg CHX), Atridox (doxycycline gel).

Examples: (a) Localized deep pockets not improving after SRP → Arestin. (b) Furcation involvement on a molar that can't be cleaned → Arestin. (c) Young patient with molar-incisor aggressive pattern → SRP + systemic antibiotics.

CHX is cationic; toothpaste contains anionic sodium lauryl sulfate which neutralizes it. Wait minimum 30 minutes between brushing and CHX rinse.
Q6 — Clinical outcomes sought
Measurable endpoints: loss or gain in clinical attachment levels, probing depths, gingival inflammation, bleeding upon probing, changes in subgingival microflora.

Non-surgical success: Decreased PD with attachment gains, resolution of BOP, decreased inflammation.

Surgical success adds: Restoration of physiologic gingival and osseous contours, minimal residual PD allowing complete debridement during maintenance and effective daily home care. Shallow gingival sulcus = prerequisite for adequate plaque control and maintenance.
Q7 — 75-year-old scenario + treatment + 30yo
Patient explanation: "We found pocket depths of 5-7 mm with bleeding around your teeth. This means you have periodontitis — gum disease where the bone and tissue around teeth has been lost, creating deep pockets where bacteria hide. Brushing can't reach these deep pockets. If untreated, this can lead to tooth loss. We recommend a deep cleaning called scaling and root planing to remove the bacteria and diseased root surfaces. After that, we need to see you every 3 months to keep bacteria from coming back."

Important: Gingival margin is 1-2 mm CORONAL to CEJ → there is a pseudopocket component. True CAL ≠ PD here. A 7 mm pocket with margin 2 mm coronal = 5 mm true CAL.

Treatment: Diagnose with generalized moderate-severe periodontitis. SRP + OHI. Re-evaluate 4-6 weeks after last quadrant. If pockets resolve to 3-4 mm with no BOP → SRP was definitive → 3-month maintenance. If 5 mm+ persists with BOP → surgery.

30-year-old? Same initial treatment. But this degree of disease in a 30yo = much more aggressive pattern (likely Grade C). May warrant systemic antibiotics and potentially earlier surgical intervention.
Q8 — Assumptions
We assume: (1) the disease will progress without intervention, (2) the patient will comply with treatment and maintenance, and (3) the benefits outweigh the risks. Without maintenance, all surgical gains are lost — surgery creates a maintainable environment, it does not cure the disease.
REF: Harper & Robinson 1987 (J Clin Perio) — SRP reduces microbes temporarily, rationale for 3-month maintenance
04 Occlusal Trauma
Q1 — What is trauma from occlusion?
Occlusal traumatism = pathologic or adaptive changes in the periodontium from undue force produced by the masticatory muscles (AAP Glossary, 2001). It is exclusively a lesion of the attachment apparatus — PDL, alveolar bone, cementum.

The pathologic lesion causes loss of alveolar and crestal lamina dura → widening of PDL → appears clinically as increasing mobility over time.

Any force applied to a tooth has the potential to produce a pathologic effect. But an etiologic agent does NOT guarantee a lesion — depends on force nature and the individual's resistance and repair capacity.
Q2 — How to diagnose the different types
You must have increasing mobility over time. Cracked teeth, wear, fractures alone are NOT enough — those are tooth morphology changes, not attachment apparatus.

Light force (adaptive range): Lesion resolves as wound heals. PDL widens permanently to compensate. Mobility is increased but NOT increasing. If force removed → bone apposition → PDL returns to original width → mobility normalizes.

Heavy force (exceeds adaptive capacity): Tooth becomes increasingly mobile and never adapts. Mobility keeps increasing = active occlusal trauma.

Primary: Excessive forces on normal support. Reversible. Signs: increasing mobility, migration, fremitus. Radiographically: widened PDL, possible angular bone loss appearance.

Secondary: Normal forces on weakened support (periodontal disease). Usually NOT reversible. Must have attachment loss to have secondary OT. However, you can still have primary OT even with attachment loss if sufficient support remains.
Q3 — Periodontal breakdown with and without occlusal trauma
Without OT: Attachment loss and bone loss from plaque-driven inflammation alone.

With OT: The combination of heavy force + ACTIVE periodontal infection can be co-destructive, exacerbating the destructive rate. Glickman & Smulow (1962) proposed that trauma redirects inflammatory exudate from the gingival unit to the attachment apparatus.

The co-destructive environment requires Force + ACTIVE INFLAMMATION — not just force + bone loss. Breakdown is still predominantly caused by plaque; occlusal trauma is minor but aggravating.

Animal studies showed: occlusal trauma on a healthy but reduced periodontium (treated case) has no different effect than on a healthy periodontium with normal bone. It's the active inflammation that matters.
Q4 — Treatment
Step A (FIRST): Eliminate inflammation. Must come first because inflammation itself causes mobility. Resolve it to determine how much mobility is from TFO vs active infection.

Step B: Eliminate or control occlusal etiology.

Step C: Develop stable intra- and inter-arch relationships.

Treatment may require: occlusal equilibration, orthodontics, and/or prosthetics (splinting).

Primary: Reduce force → equilibration. Once reduced, lesion resolves, PDL returns to pre-force width, mobility decreases.

Secondary: Splinting to compensate for lost support that left the tooth unable to withstand even normal forces.
Q5 — Is mobility disease? When to treat?
Mobility is NOT inherently disease. A certain increase in mobility can be accepted, provided the tooth does not disturb masticatory function or patient comfort.

What matters: stable vs progressive. Stable increased mobility (same grade over months) = adapted, not active trauma. Progressive (increasing over time) = active trauma, needs treatment.

Grading: Physiologic ≤0.2 mm. Grade 1 = 0.2-1.0 mm. Grade 2 = 1.0-2.0 mm. Grade 3 = >2.0 mm and/or vertical displacement.
Q6 — Mobility vs. fremitus
Mobility = displacement when the operator applies force with two rigid instruments. Operator-generated.

Fremitus = vibration/movement when the patient bites down or functions. Place gloved finger on facial of maxillary teeth while patient taps into occlusion and grinds through excursions. Patient-generated functional test.

Both on the same tooth = bad sign — patient's own forces are causing pathologic movement.
Q7 — Does TFO cause attachment loss?
No. The effects of occlusal force are limited to the attachment apparatus. The gingival soft tissues coronal to bone (CT attachment and JE) are NOT damaged by excessive force. Therefore, occlusal trauma does not cause (initiate) attachment loss, pocketing, or recession.

However, when OT exists alongside ACTIVE periodontal disease, it can aggravate and exacerbate the rate of attachment loss already being caused by plaque inflammation.
REF: Glickman & Smulow 1962 — co-destruction theory (OT redirects inflammatory exudate to attachment apparatus)

04 Occlusal Trauma
Q1 — What is TFO?
Pathologic or adaptive changes in the periodontium from undue masticatory force (AAP 2001). Exclusively a lesion of the attachment apparatus (PDL, bone, cementum). Causes loss of lamina dura → widened PDL → increasing mobility over time. Any force CAN produce a lesion, but doesn't guarantee one.
Q2 — How to diagnose
Must have increasing mobility over time. Cracked teeth, wear, fractures alone are NOT enough — those are tooth changes, not attachment apparatus.

Light force: Within adaptive range. PDL widens but heals. Mobility increased but NOT increasing. Reversible if force removed.
Heavy force: Exceeds adaptive capacity. Mobility keeps increasing. Never adapts.

Primary: Excessive force + normal support. Reversible. Signs: increasing mobility, migration, fremitus. X-ray: widened PDL.
Secondary: Normal force + weakened support. Usually NOT reversible. Must have attachment loss.
Q3 — Perio breakdown with/without OT
Without: Attachment loss from plaque-driven inflammation alone.
With: Heavy force + ACTIVE infection = co-destructive — exacerbates breakdown rate. Glickman & Smulow (1962) proposed OT redirects inflammatory exudate to the attachment apparatus. Breakdown still predominantly from plaque; OT is minor but aggravating. Key: it's Force + ACTIVE INFLAMMATION that causes co-destruction, not force + reduced bone. OT on a treated reduced periodontium has no extra effect.
Q4 — Treatment
A) Eliminate inflammation FIRST — inflammation itself causes mobility, must resolve to see true TFO contribution.
B) Address occlusal etiology — Primary: equilibration. Secondary: splinting.
C) Develop stable arch relationships — equilibration, orthodontics, prosthetics.
Q5 — Is mobility disease?
No. Increased but STABLE mobility is acceptable if no function/comfort issues. Only PROGRESSIVE (increasing) mobility needs treatment. Grades: Physio ≤0.2mm. G1=0.2-1mm. G2=1-2mm. G3=>2mm and/or vertical.
Q6 — Mobility vs fremitus
Mobility = operator applies force with two instruments. Operator-generated.
Fremitus = movement when patient bites down. Finger on facial, patient taps/grinds. Patient-generated.
Both on same tooth = bad sign.
Q7 — Does TFO cause attachment loss?
No. Force only affects the attachment apparatus. Supracrestal soft tissues (CT attachment + JE) are NOT damaged by force. Cannot initiate attachment loss, pocketing, or recession. But CAN aggravate existing active periodontal disease.
