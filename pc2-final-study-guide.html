OBJ A1

Understand the Skeletal Anatomy of the Thorax

The Landmark Sequence (How to Navigate the Chest)

The professor walked through this as a top-down palpation exercise. This sequence is the foundation for everything in both cardiac and pulmonary exams:

Suprasternal Notch→ Manubrium→ Sternal Angle (Angle of Louis)→ 2nd Rib→ Count Interspaces
  • Suprasternal Notch: Divot at base of neck, above sternum. Starting point for everything.
  • Manubrium: Top segment of sternum from notch down to first indentation.
  • Sternal Angle (Angle of Louis): Indentation where manubrium meets body of sternum. Go slightly lateral → feel hard structure = 2nd rib. This is your anchor for counting.
  • Interspaces are named for the rib ABOVE: Interspace 2 = below 2nd rib, Interspace 3 = below 3rd rib, etc. Critical for cardiac auscultation.

Rib Attachments

RibsAttachmentType
1–7Directly to sternumTrue ribs
8, 9, 10Each attaches to rib above (8→7, 9→8, 10→9)False ribs
11, 12Attach posteriorly ONLY — not anteriorlyFree floating
  • Xiphoid Process: Inferior tip of sternum. Don't compress during CPR → can break off → pneumothorax.
  • Costochondral Angle: Where costal cartilage meets ribs at inferior margin of rib cage.
  • 1st rib hard to find — sits under clavicle. That's why you use the sternal angle → 2nd rib method.
As you count interspaces, move fingers LATERALLY outward. Staying too medial → lose track because ribs 8–10 don't reach the sternum.
OBJ A2

Understand the Surface Anatomy of the Chest and Back

Anterior Chest Lines

  • Midsternal Line (MSL): Straight down middle of sternum. Well-defined, easy to find.
  • Midclavicular Line (MCL): Drops from midpoint of clavicle. "Not as well defined" — can be slightly off.
  • Anterior Axillary Line: Drops from anterior axillary fold.

Lateral Chest

  • Anterior Axillary, Middle Axillary, and Posterior Axillary lines — used to describe lesion/abnormality locations.

Posterior (Back)

  • C7 identification: Flex neck forward → most prominent spinous process = C7. If two prominent = C7 and T1.
  • Scapula overlies the 7th rib.
  • Vertebral Line along spinous processes; Scapular Line from inferior angle of scapula.
  • Ribs 11–12 attach posteriorly but NOT anteriorly (free floating).
OBJ A3

Describe the Lobes of the Lungs & Where to Auscultate Each Area

LungLobesWhere Heard
Left (2 lobes)LUL, LLLUpper: apices (post.); Lower: most of back
Right (3 lobes)RUL, RML, RLLUpper: apices; Middle: ANTERIOR & slightly lateral ONLY; Lower: most of back
The Right Middle Lobe is ONLY heard from the anterior chest and maybe slightly lateral. "If somebody has a pneumonia consolidated in the right middle lobe, if you're not listening over it, you're not gonna hear it." — Prof repeated this point TWICE across the lecture.

Bronchial Tree

  • Trachea → Carina → R & L Main Stem Bronchi
  • Carina: Heavily innervated → triggers cough reflex to prevent aspiration into lungs.
  • Right main stem bronchus is wider, shorter, more vertical (almost a straight shot). Left goes off at more of a right angle.
Aspirated foreign bodies preferentially go to the RIGHT lung — the right bronchus is a straighter shot. Classic MCQ.

Alveolar Anatomy

  • Progressive branching: main stem → lobar → segmental → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles → respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → sacs → alveoli
  • Each alveolus surrounded by a capillary network. ~200 million alveoli per lung.
  • Extremely thin barrier between capillary and alveolus allows gas exchange.
OBJ A4

Describe the Differences Between Internal and External Respiration

TypeDefinitionWhere
External RespirationExchange of O₂ and CO₂ between the organism and the external environmentLungs (alveoli ↔ capillaries)
Internal Respiration (Cellular)Utilization of O₂ by cells; cells produce CO₂Tissue level (cells ↔ capillaries)

Gas Exchange Principle

Gases move from HIGH partial pressure → LOW partial pressure. It's not zero on either side — as long as there's a "delta" (difference), diffusion occurs.

  • Deoxygenated blood (high CO₂, low O₂) → lungs → O₂ is HIGH in alveolus, LOW in capillary → O₂ diffuses IN
  • CO₂ is HIGH in capillary, LOW in alveolus → CO₂ diffuses OUT → exhaled
  • Oxygenated blood returns via pulmonary veins → left heart → body
Prof explicitly connected this to N₂O: "When we talk about nitrous oxide, it's gonna be the same thing. High partial pressure N₂O given to patients, nothing in bloodstream, so quick uptake." Partial pressure gradients = rapid N₂O uptake.

Room Air Composition

21% O₂, ~78% N₂, trace CO₂. Professor quizzed the class on this directly.

Steps of Respiration (5 steps from slide)

  • 1. Exchange of air between atmosphere and lungs (ventilation)
  • 2. O₂ and CO₂ alveolar exchange by diffusion
  • 3. O₂ and CO₂ transport via pulmonary and systemic circulations
  • 4. O₂ and CO₂ exchange in tissue capillaries
  • 5. Cellular utilization (cellular respiration)

Paper bag for hyperventilation = NO LONGER RECOMMENDED. Correct management = calm the patient down. Reason: if you misdiagnose and the patient is actually hypoxic or has a cardiac/pulmonary problem, the bag blocks needed oxygen.

2. Palpation

Four things: Pain, Abnormalities, Expansion, Fremitus

Expansion technique: Hands on patient's back, thumbs at midline, pinch skin (~8 cm apart). Patient breathes in → watch for symmetrical expansion. DON'T press hard and restrict movement.

Fremitus = palpable vibration. Place ball of hand on back, patient says "99." Compare side to side using the 1-1, 2-2, 3-3, 4-4 pattern. Must be over lung fields, NOT over bone.

Fremitus alone isn't the most reliable exam, but it directs you where to focus your auscultation. Increased or decreased on one side = investigate further.

3. Percussion

Technique: Non-dominant hand middle finger flat on chest (pleximeter). Strike at the DIP joint. Dominant hand middle finger flicks the wrist — tap tap. Thumb/palm/other fingers OFF the body.

SoundMeaningProf's Analogy
ResonantAir-filled space = normal lung"Puffed-out cheeks" — hollow sound
DullSolid/fluid-filled = abnormal (or below diaphragm)"Percuss your thigh" — flat, no sound

Above diaphragm = resonant (normal). Below diaphragm = dull (no lung). You can test lung expansion: percuss to dullness → patient breathes in → percuss lower — if resonant, lungs expanded.

4. Auscultation

Normal breath sounds: Vesicular (soft, low-pitched, most lung fields) and Bronchial (louder, over trachea/large airways — abnormal if heard peripherally).

Adventitious sounds = shouldn't be there (see Abnormal Findings section below).

The Ladder Pattern (Used for BOTH Percussion AND Auscultation)

Side-to-side comparison: 1-1, 2-2, 3-3 — always comparing same level, left vs. right.

OBJ A9

Pulmonary Findings: Asthma, Pneumonia, Pneumothorax, Emphysema & CHF

Asthma (Prof spent the MOST time on this — full case)

Definition: Obstructive pulmonary disease. Constriction of bronchi + inflammation/mucus secretions (mucus plugging).

What to ask an asthmatic patient (Mr. Smith case):

  • Triggering factors: Stress, allergen, weather, exercise-induced
  • Frequency — well-controlled (inhaler few times/year) vs. ER visits last 3 nights (unstable)
  • Duration — inhaler resolves it? Hospital treatment? Admitted?
  • Medications — "Do you have your inhaler with you?"
  • Day vs. night attacks — nighttime = worse prognosis
  • Hospitalizations — nebulized? steroids? intubated? (= severe)
  • ROS: Dyspnea, chest tightness, cough
  • Superimposed URI over asthma → can trigger attack
50% of childhood asthma resolves on its own. Adult-onset asthma usually does NOT — "those are the ones I get more concerned about."

Physical exam findings: Tachypnea, labored breathing, expiratory wheezing, occasionally productive cough, PFT: FEV₁/FVC.

FEV₁ >80% = good sign. "We could spend hours on PFTs, but we're not going to." Just know FEV₁ >80% and the abbreviation.
Asthma + anxiety patient → N₂O can help reduce stress (a trigger). N₂O is NOT contraindicated in asthma.

OBJ B1

Heart Anatomy, Valves & Blood Flow

Orientation

  • Anteriorly: Mostly right ventricle visible. Then RA, pulmonary artery. Small portion of LV on left. LA NOT visible from front.
  • Base = superior (top); Apex = inferior (bottom)
  • PMI (Point of Maximal Impulse) = 5th interspace, midclavicular line. If displaced down and laterally → cardiomegaly.

Complete Blood Flow Pathway

Body→ SVC / IVC / Coronary Sinus→ Right Atrium→ TRICUSPID→ Right Ventricle→ PULMONIC→ Pulmonary Artery→ Lungs
Lungs→ 4 Pulmonary Veins→ Left Atrium→ MITRAL→ Left Ventricle→ AORTIC→ Aorta→ Body

Coronary sinus drains the heart's own venous blood into the right atrium. 4 pulmonary veins (2 from each lung) return oxygenated blood to the left atrium.

OBJ B2

Systole, Diastole & What Makes S1 & S2

VentriclesAtria
Systole (S1→S2)CONTRACTINGRelaxing / filling
Diastole (S2→S1)RELAXING / fillingContracting

S1 — "Lub" — Beginning of Systole

  • Ventricles contract → pressure rises → exceeds atrial pressure
  • Mitral + Tricuspid valves CLOSE
  • S1 is mostly heard from the Mitral valve (higher pressure left side)

Mid-Systole

  • Pressure continues to rise → exceeds aortic/pulmonary pressure → Aortic + Pulmonic valves OPEN (silently)
  • Blood ejected to body and lungs

S2 — "Dub" — End of Systole

  • Ventricle finishes ejecting → pressure drops → aortic/pulmonary pressure exceeds ventricular
  • Aortic + Pulmonic valves CLOSE
  • S2 is mostly heard from the Aortic valve

Key Principles

  • Heart sounds = valves CLOSING, not opening. Valves open silently.
  • S1 = Mitral + Tricuspid close (mostly Mitral) = START of systole
  • S2 = Aortic + Pulmonic close (mostly Aortic) = END of systole

Adventitious Cardiac Sounds: S3, S4, Splitting of S2

SoundTimingPhaseCausePathologic?

THE SEQUENCE: S4 → S1 → S2 → S3. Both S3 and S4 are in diastole but at opposite ends. S4 = before S1 (late diastole). S3 = after S2 (early diastole). Prof said: "They asked this on my oral surgery boards."

Splitting of S2

  • Occurs during inspiration — more blood fills RV → takes longer to eject → pulmonic valve closes slightly later than aortic
  • NOT pathologic — normal physiologic finding
  • On expiration, sounds come back together
OBJ B4

Conduction System & ECG Alignment with Heart Sounds

Conduction Pathway

SA Node (pacemaker)→ Internodal + Interatrial→ AV Node (DELAY)→ Bundle of His→ R & L Bundle Branches→ Purkinje Fibers
  • AV node delay = gives time for atria to finish contracting and filling ventricles before ventricles fire.
  • SA node modulated by ANS — sympathetic speeds up, parasympathetic slows down.
Defibrillation ≠ "jumpstarting" the heart. It depolarizes the heart so the SA node can hopefully resume normal pacing on its own.

ECG Waves

WaveRepresentsAligned Heart Sound
P waveAtrial depolarizationS4 may be heard here
QRS complexVentricular depolarizationS1 falls here

Depolarization ≠ Contraction. "Doesn't mean it's contracting, it's depolarized first before it contracts." A patient can have organized ECG activity but NO pulse = PEA (Pulseless Electrical Activity) = emergency.

OBJ B6

How to Take a Pulse and Blood Pressure

Pulse Assessment

ParameterDetails
RateCount 30 sec × 2. Normal 60–100 bpm. Brady <60, Tachy >100.
RhythmRegular, Irregular, or Irregularly Irregular (= A-fib)
AmplitudeBounding (fever/exercise) vs. Thready (fainting)
Bruit / ThrillThrill = FELT (palpable turbulence); Bruit = HEARD (auscultated turbulence)

When to use which pulse: Radial = routine/conscious. Brachial = BP cuff stethoscope placement. Carotid = unconscious patient / BLS.

Trained athletes can have HR in the 40s and be fine. Sedentary person with HR in the 40s = "probably has a heart block."

Blood Pressure Technique

  • Palpate brachial artery → inflate cuff until pulse gone → pump +20 mmHg more
  • Slowly release → first sound = SYSTOLIC (turbulent flow through compressed artery)
  • Keep listening → sound disappears = DIASTOLIC (free flow, no turbulence)
  • Keep listening 10–20 mmHg past diastolic → check for auscultatory gap (sound may temporarily disappear then return)

Common mistake: Cuff/bladder too small → falsely elevated BP.

Elective procedure cutoff: BP < 160/100. Emergency can push a little higher.
OBJ B7

Heart Murmurs — Location, Cardiac Cycle & THE VALVE LOGIC

Heart murmur = abnormal sound caused by turbulent blood flow.

"I guarantee you you're getting a question like, is this a systolic murmur..." — Prof literally confirmed a murmur MCQ will be on the exam.

6 Mechanisms of Murmurs

  • 1. Flow across a partial obstruction (stenotic valve)
  • 2. Flow across a valvular irregularity (e.g., endocarditis)
  • 3. Increased flow through normal structures (e.g., anemia of pregnancy = physiologic murmur)
  • 4. Flow into a dilated chamber (cardiomyopathy)
  • 5. Regurgitant flow across an incompetent valve
  • 6. Shunting (e.g., dialysis shunt)

THE RULE

Valve supposed to be OPEN + you hear a murmur = STENOSIS (not opening fully, obstructing flow)

Valve supposed to be CLOSED + you hear a murmur = REGURGITATION (leaking backward)

OBJ B8

Echocardiogram

Echocardiogram = diagnostic test of choice for heart murmurs. Echo (ultrasound) + cardiogram (heart).

Provides information about:

  • 1. Valves of the heart (calcification, stenosis, regurgitation)
  • 2. Ejection fraction (force of contraction)
  • 3. Motion and thickness of the heart walls

Post-MI patient questions to ask: How long ago was the MI? Have they had an echo since? Have they had a stress test?

Objective 4

Exchange of N₂O in Alveoli → Circulatory System

Principle: Gases diffuse from high pressure to low pressure across the alveolar membrane.

Uptake (start of case): N₂O inhaled → alveolus has HIGH N₂O partial pressure → blood has ZERO N₂O → N₂O rushes rapidly from alveolus → blood → tissues/brain. As blood saturates, diffusion rate decreases → equilibrium.

Elimination (end of case): Switch to 100% O₂ → alveolus has ZERO N₂O → blood is RICH in N₂O → N₂O rushes out of blood → alveolus → exhaled out. This is where diffusion hypoxia can occur.

Pharmacology: Blood-gas solubility coefficient = 0.47 at 37°C. Only ~0.06% metabolized — rest exhaled unchanged through lungs. Remains unchanged in blood. Small quantities needed to reach required blood concentrations.

Solubility determines speed:

  • Totally insoluble: Doesn't enter blood at all
  • Relatively insoluble (N₂O): Rapid equilibrium → fast onset + fast recovery ("quick on, quick off")
  • Highly soluble: Very slow to reach effective concentrations

Objective 7

Concentration Effect & Secondary Gas Effect

Concentration Effect ("Vacuum-Type Effect"): Higher the concentration of gas inhaled → more rapid increase in arterial tension. Only significant at >70% → not clinically relevant in dental office (machines cap at 70%). Know the definition, understand it's not important for dental N₂O.

Second Gas Effect: When N₂O is combined with another volatile inhalation agent during GA, N₂O's rapid uptake from the alveolus creates a "vacuum" that pulls the second gas along → you can use lower concentrations of the more potent/dangerous volatile agent.

Think of a puddle of water (volatile agent) and wind (N₂O). At 5–10 mph the puddle evaporates slowly. At 30–40 mph it goes up much faster. Higher N₂O concentration = more wind = pulls the second gas quicker.

Only relevant during general anesthesia, not dental N₂O alone. Advantage: minimizes required concentration of the other agent.

Objective 8

Diffusion Hypoxia & How to Prevent It

🚨🚨 High Yield — Know the Full Mechanism

When: Occurs at termination of sedation.

Mechanism: Turn off N₂O → switch to O₂ → alveolus has little/no N₂O → blood still RICH in N₂O → N₂O rushes from blood → alveolus → dilutes the O₂ in the alveolus → patient gets insufficient O₂ → hypoxia.

Prevention: 100% O₂ for 3–5 minutes post-procedure.

"Hangover" symptoms: Headache, Nausea, Dizziness, Lethargy

If symptoms persist: Keep giving O₂. Most recover in 5 minutes; some take 10–15 minutes. "You should walk out of here the same way you walked in, except for the procedure."

Nasal hood = open system — patient also breathes some room air around mask edges, so "100% O₂" from machine ≠ 100% inspired O₂. But it's enough to prevent diffusion hypoxia.

  • Manifold connects 2 tanks — when one empties, other kicks in automatically
  • Copper piping, silver solder, different pipe sizes per gas
  • Tested with nitrogen for leaks (not water). Requires certified installer + fire department sign-off.
  • More expensive upfront; cost-effective long-term for frequent use

Portable Gas Delivery System:

  • Uses E tanks (small). Metal framework. Flexible — move room to room.
  • Less initial expense ($3K–$13K depending on features)
  • Digital vs. Manual flowmeters: Digital auto-calculates O₂/N₂O split when you change %. Manual requires constant adjustment of both gases.
  • Some machines display % O₂ (not % N₂O) — must convert: 65% O₂ = 35% N₂O

Flowmeter — Accutron specifics:

  • Must turn O₂ on FIRST → auto-starts at 3 L/min
  • Max N₂O on the machine = 7 L → 3/(3+7) = minimum 30% O₂ guaranteed
  • Pediatric limitation: can't get below 6 L/min total flow at 50% N₂O
  • O₂ Flush: Rapid 100% O₂ delivery
  • Room Air Override: If all gas stops → patient breathes ambient air through valve
  • Outspout: Gas to patient. Downspout: Reservoir bag connection.

Do NOT extend tubing beyond standard length — increases dead space and environmental N₂O exposure.

Breathing Apparatus:

  • Nasal hood: Standard for dentistry. Scented (cherry, bubble gum, vanilla). Disposable. Different sizes. MUST possess scavenging capability.
  • Face mask: Not practical for most dental work (covers mouth). Useful for short pediatric procedures (cycling on/off). Required for positive pressure ventilation (nasal hood won't work — gas escapes through nose).

Scavenging System:

  • Removes exhaled N₂O from environment via central suction
  • Required suction rate: 45 L/min — check green zone indicator throughout case
  • 4-hose system: valve closes suction on inhalation (full gas delivery), opens on exhalation (suctions away N₂O)
  • 2-hose system (low-profile): continuous suction on one side — simpler, easier to sterilize, less bulky
  • Exhaust vent must NOT be near HVAC intake

Reservoir Bag (3 functions):

  1. Supplemental gas: Buffer for deep inspirations (tall patients sighing in)
  2. Respiratory monitoring: Small movements = patient breathing (don't expect dramatic excursions)
  3. Flow assessment: Bag flattens = patient overbreathing → increase flow. Bag over-expands = too much flow → decrease.

PPV with bag: Must switch to face mask (not nasal hood). Remove nasal hood → connect face mask → switch to O₂ → squeeze bag.

Pre-oxygenation (3–5 min at start): Use this time to calibrate proper flow rate.

1. Safety Features Article

Donaldson M, Donaldson D, Quarnstrom F. JADA 2012;143:134–143

Background & Key Findings

N₂O–O₂ delivery systems are limited to a maximum of 70% N₂O / 30% O₂, ensuring the patient receives at least 9% more oxygen than ambient air (21%). The most important safety consideration is prevention of hypoxia.

The authors reviewed the 12 safety features built into N₂O delivery systems. Despite these features, dentists have reported incidents of hypoxia from incorrect equipment installation or equipment damage.

  • Incorrect installation → primarily a risk with centralized systems (gas lines plumbed through building). Could theoretically occur with portable systems if manufactured incorrectly, but rare.
  • Equipment damage → develops over time in machines used for years.

Crossed Gas Lines Scenario

🎯 High-Yield Exam Scenario

If you dial up 100% oxygen and the patient becomes MORE sedated → the oxygen and nitrous oxide lines are reversed (crossed gas lines).

This applies to both new installations AND old machines with damage. The professor presented this as a specific clinical vignette in class — this is MCQ-ready.

What To Do If Safety Feature Failure Suspected

Slide states: "Remove the face mask from the patient immediately."

Professor's expanded protocol:

  1. Remove the nasal hood / face mask immediately
  2. Use your portable emergency O₂ tank — put patient on face mask or nasal cannula from the separate emergency tank
  3. Do NOT just let them breathe room air — risk of diffusion hypoxia
🎯 Exam Alert — Diffusion Hypoxia

Prof specifically asked the class: "What's your concern if you just take off the nasal hood and let them breathe room air?"

Answer: Diffusion hypoxia. N₂O flooding out of the blood into the alveoli dilutes alveolar oxygen, causing transient hypoxia. It could resolve over time, but use your emergency O₂ to prevent it.

Daily Pre-Procedure Equipment Check

🔬 Professor's Personal Protocol

He described his daily routine before sedations, matching the "Preoperative Considerations" slide:

  1. Turn on evacuation/suction → confirm scavenger system working (>45 L/min)
  2. Turn on oxygen → cover nasal hood opening with hand → reservoir bag should expand (confirms no leaks)
  3. Turn on N₂O briefly (~2 L/min) → confirm it's flowing
  4. Turn OFF the oxygen → N₂O should automatically shut off (fail-safe). This confirms the fail-safe system is functional.

5. N₂O Administration Technique — Step by Step

Slides: Box 13-2 Technique / "Technique" series — Prof said: "Study this for the exam"
🎯 Prof Literally Said to Study This Sequence for the Exam

"When you go through your notes as you study for the exams, think about this because this is picking up all the technique which you're gonna follow."

Complete Technique Sequence

  1. Assemble and assess armamentarium — open tanks, turn on flowmeter. Check scavenger (45 L/min vacuum to outside), check for leaks (soapy water at connections), ensure ventilation vents to outside. Non-circulating ventilation system is best; air exchangers even better.
  2. Confirm scavenging devices functioning
  3. Health history reviewed; vital signs recorded
  4. Informed consent obtained
  5. Activate O₂ flow — fill reservoir bag approximately ⅔ full by use of O₂ flush valve
  6. Secure nasal hood — select appropriate size; snug around nose. Two styles: tubes behind head, or around ears (more comfortable)
  7. Instruct patient: breathe through nose, keep lips sealed — if patient talks, tell them they won't get the full effect (gets them to stop). Keep YOUR questions to a minimum too.
  8. Establish appropriate tidal volume in L/min by observing reservoir bag
  9. Pre-oxygenate for 2–5 minutes at 100% O₂
  10. Begin N₂O titration (see titration protocol below)
  11. Assess patient for signs and symptoms of appropriate sedation
  12. Titrate N₂O to desired level
  13. Perform operative procedures
  14. Terminate N₂O flow — try to do this with ~5 minutes left in procedure
  15. Administer 5 minutes postoperative 100% O₂ at same flow rate patient was on
  16. Assess recovery — continue O₂ if needed
  17. Ensure recovery and dismiss patient
  18. Complete sedation record
  19. Disinfect and/or sterilize sedation equipment

One size doesn't fit all — smaller patients need less, larger patients need more. Math method: 70 cc/kg × weight = tidal volume; multiply by 12 breaths/min = L/min flow.

Titration Protocol

🎯 Know This Sequence
StepAction

2Wait 90 seconds

Principle: Slow titration is key to success and safety.

Constant vs. Variable O₂ Technique

Constant O₂ Level

O₂ flow stays fixed (e.g., 6 L/min). You add N₂O on top. Simpler, but total flow increases as you add N₂O. Patients can usually tolerate increased total flow.

Variable O₂ Level

As N₂O increases, O₂ decreases — keeps total flow constant while percentage changes. Requires more manual adjustment. More complex.

Prof Tip

"Digital flowmeters where you just say 'I want 15%, 20%, 30%' — so much easier, it will calculate that for you."

✅ Correct (Suggestive)

"Your arms may feel heavy..."
"These are all common with nitrous oxide."

❌ Wrong (Directive)

"You should be feeling tingling now..."
"Your arms should feel heavy..."
→ If they don't feel it, they'll think it's not working.

Clinical Pearl — White Knuckle Syndrome

The resolution of white-knuckle gripping is due to anxiety reduction, NOT direct muscle relaxation from N₂O. N₂O doesn't directly relax skeletal muscles — the patient lets go because they're calmer.

Prof Teaching Point

"You may put them deeper and think, well, maybe that's a good thing. No, it's not good because you're putting them in a deeper level — they're NOT gonna be comfortable and they're gonna be MORE anxious in the end." Deeper ≠ better.

Oversedation Management

🎯 Management Algorithm

If patient shows oversedation signs:

  1. Stop N₂O immediately
  2. Deliver 100% O₂
  3. If nausea/vomiting → suction ready, turn patient's head to side

If patient is unconscious with snoring:

  1. Try repositioning the airway (chin lift) first — may wake them up
  2. If that wakes them → just decrease N₂O percentage
  3. If not → turn off N₂O entirely → 100% O₂ → chin lift
Clinical Pearl — Same Patient, Different Days

Prof's scenario: Patient usually runs at 40%. End of the week, tired, afternoon appointment → titrate to usual 40% → they fall asleep. The same patient can respond differently on different days. Fatigue, stress, and time of day all affect response. This is why you titrate every single time — never just dial to "their usual dose."

The Rescue Concept

🎯 Core Principle — Repeated Across Multiple Lectures

If a patient enters a deeper level of sedation than intended, you must "rescue" them back to the intended level. You must always be prepared to manage one level deeper than you're targeting.

Continuum: Minimal → Moderate → Deep → General Anesthesia. You aim for one, but may hit the next. Always be able to bring them back.

11. Pediatric N₂O — Surveys & Key Concepts

1993 Survey Data

  • 89% of pediatric dentists used N₂O
  • 87% used titration technique
  • Rarely exceeded 50%
  • N₂O alone → only 2% experienced compromised airway
  • N₂O + other medications → 33% compromised airway
🎯 Dramatic Statistic

Airway compromise jumps from 2% → 33% when you add other sedatives to N₂O. This is a 16.5× increase — and explains why N₂O + any oral sedative = moderate sedation with higher monitoring requirements.

The 50/50 Technique

Alternative for pediatrics: start at 50% N₂O / 50% O₂ rather than titrating up.

  • NOT recommended for adults — "they're gonna have a bad experience"
  • Professor still prefers titration even in pediatric patients
  • 15-year follow-up survey confirmed same patterns as 1993

What N₂O Does (and Doesn't Do) in Kids

🎯 Key Distinction

N₂O modifies uncooperative behaviors but does NOT change physiologic parameters (BP, pulse, respiratory drive all maintained).

Works for: uncooperative/anxious patients

Does NOT work for: phobic patients, combative patients running around the office

"If the patient is running around your office, nitrous oxide's probably not gonna work for them."

Silent Regurgitation & Aspiration

🎯 Safety-Critical for Pediatrics

Pediatric patients can easily reach unconsciousness → loss of pharyngeal-laryngeal reflexes → silent regurgitation → aspiration.

Prevention: Don't let the patient go to sleep. Keep them conscious = reflexes intact.

This is why the professor is "pretty tight with the guidelines for fasting" in pediatric patients.

12. AAP/AAPD Guidelines & Goals of Sedation

AAP/AAPD Guidelines — Key Principles

Developed for non-anesthesiologists doing sedation outside the OR. Apply to both pediatric and adult practice.

  1. Pre-procedure evaluation — always comes FIRST
  2. Appropriate fasting guidelines
  3. Airway evaluation (Mallampati classification) — though N₂O patients shouldn't need intubation
  4. Appropriately trained individuals to monitor
  5. Knowledge of associated risk
  6. Safety is primary concern
  7. Must be able to rescue from a deeper level than intended

Goals of Sedation

  1. Guard patient safety and welfare
  2. Minimize physical discomfort/pain
  3. Control anxiety and maximize amnestic potential
  4. Control behavior and movement
  5. Return patient to safe discharge state — this is the main goal

Principle: Lowest dose, fewest drugs, correct drug for the intended goal.

13. Indications, Contraindications & Special Conditions

Objectives of Pediatric N₂O

  • Reduce/eliminate anxiety
  • Reduce untoward movement/reaction to treatment
  • Enhance communication and cooperation
  • Raise the pain reaction threshold
  • Increase tolerance for longer appointments (but watch local anesthetic max dose — the real limiting factor)
  • Aid treatment of mentally/physically disabled or medically compromised patients
  • Reduce gagging
  • Potentiate effect of sedatives

Indications

  • Fearful, anxious patient (NOT phobic)
  • Special healthcare needs patients
  • Gag reflex interfering with dental care
  • Patient for whom profound local anesthesia cannot be obtained (e.g., abscessed tooth)
  • Cooperative patient undergoing lengthy procedure

Disadvantages

  • Lack of potency — can't put patients to sleep (but also a safety feature)
  • Dependent on psychological reassurance — "If you don't believe in it, why should your patients?"
  • Nasal hood interference with anterior maxillary injections
  • Patient must breathe through nose
  • Trace gas exposure → staff occupational hazard (patient leaves, you stay)

Contraindications

Prof Says

"I'm not gonna ask you what's an absolute contraindication, what's a moderate contraindication." — Just know the contraindications and understand WHY.

Major Contraindications:

  • Some chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases
  • Severe emotional disturbances or drug-related dependencies
  • First trimester of pregnancy
  • MTHFR deficiency (methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase)
  • Cobalamin (B12) deficiency
  • Bleomycin therapy
  • Air-filled spaces (see below)

Special Medical Conditions

  • Trait — not as much concern
  • Disease — cases of sickle cell crisis under N₂O reported. Unclear if N₂O or surgical trauma was trigger.
  • Logic: sickle cell patients must avoid hypoxia. N₂O provides supplemental O₂ (40% N₂O = 60% O₂), which should help.

Neurologic Diseases:

  • Concern with maintaining airway
  • Some neurologic patients → higher risk of malignant hyperthermia
  • BUT: N₂O is NOT a triggering agent for malignant hyperthermia — the procedure/surgical stress could be the trigger

Autistic Spectrum Disorder (ASD):

🎯 Highly Testable — ASD + N₂O Controversy

Why controversial: Some ASD individuals have abnormalities of folic acid, vitamin B12, and MTHFR deficiency. Since N₂O inactivates B12, giving it to these patients could worsen neurologic problems.

Approach: Rule out B12/MTHFR issues first. If cleared → N₂O can be used. Inform parents of the risk either way.

"If they don't have that as part of the spectrum, then you can give the nitrous oxide."

  • Dreaming and fantasizing

IV Sedation — Definition

Administration of a drug → depression of CNS. Goal: produce sedation where patient maintains protective reflexes, respiratory and cardiovascular systems minimally affected.

"If you're on a moderate sedation patient, that patient should be able to talk to you and just feeling really, really comfortable."
EXAM IV Sedation Advantages & Disadvantages
Advantages
  1. Rapid onset: arm-to-brain = 20–25 seconds
  2. Titration: Give small amounts at a time → only minimum needed → faster recovery, fewer side effects. Bell-shaped curve: hyper-responders vs. hypo-responders.
  3. Continuous IV infusion: Patent vein maintained. Give fluids to NPO patient (D5W/sugar water). Route for additional medications.
  4. N/V rare when administered correctly. Prof screens: "Do you get car sick?" → if yes, give prophylactic antiemetic IV.
  5. Salivation control: IV anticholinergics
  6. Gag reflex diminished — IV sedation better than N₂O for surgical procedures with gag issues. But for simple impressions → prefer N₂O (5 min recovery vs. longer).
  7. Diminish seizure activity (but some meds can lower threshold too)
Disadvantages
  1. Venipuncture is necessary (skill-dependent)
  2. Complications may arise at venipuncture site
  3. Monitoring must be more intensive — deeper sedation = more monitoring
  4. Recovery not complete → escort needed
  5. Most IV agents CANNOT be reversed — exceptions: opioids (naloxone) and benzodiazepines (flumazenil)

EXAM Anesthesia Continuum & General Anesthesia
🔴 "I CANNOT STRESS THIS ENOUGH" — Prof's Most Repeated Concept

Anesthesia is a CONTINUUM: Minimal sedation → Moderate → Deep → General Anesthesia

You can aim for moderate and accidentally go deep. You can aim for deep and never get there. You MUST be prepared to recover the patient to the level you're trained to treat.

General Anesthesia — Definition
  • Drug-induced loss of consciousness
  • Not arousable even by painful stimulation (vs. deep sedation where they MAY respond)
  • Ventilatory function often impaired → may need to maintain airway AND breathe for patient
  • Cardiovascular function may be impaired (usually intact; slight ↓ BP, slight ↓ HR)

GA Advantages

  1. Patient cooperation not essential
  2. Patient unconscious (can work freely)
  3. No pain response
  4. Amnesia present
  5. May be ONLY successful technique
  6. Rapid onset
  7. Titration possible
  8. Success rate ~100%

GA Disadvantages

  1. Patient unconscious (can't assess verbally)
  2. Protective reflexes depressed
  3. Vital signs depressed
  4. Advanced training required
  5. Anesthesia team required
  6. Special equipment needed
  7. Recovery area must be available
  8. More complications
  9. NPO 6 hours
  10. More extensive preop evaluation

EXAM Barbiturates — Methohexital Seizure Threshold Distinction
Barbiturates — Overview
  • CNS depressants: cerebral cortex, limbic system, reticular activating system (sleep/wakefulness)
  • Can produce: light sedation → hypnosis → GA → coma → death
  • Classified by duration: ultra-short, short, long acting
  • No effect on pain threshold until loss of consciousness → then it DROPS (patients become MORE reactive to pain, even pressure)
  • Eliminated by liver biotransformation, excreted by kidneys → liver disease = problem
  • NO REVERSAL AGENT for barbiturates
  • Hangover effect — distributes into fat, slowly leaches out
  • Typical procedures: 15–45 minutes
Contraindications
  • Allergy
  • Uncontrolled pain (pain threshold drops under sedation)
  • Addiction to sedative-hypnotics
  • Acute intermittent porphyria (classic board fact)
  • Respiratory disease with dyspnea/obstruction
Pentobarbital (short acting)
  • IV induction + seizure control
  • Effects: ↓ cerebral metabolism, modest ↓ BP, temporary respiratory depression, hangover
  • Drug of choice for elevated intracranial pressure (ICP)
  • If patient already has low BP → avoid
🔴 THE BIG METHOHEXITAL DISTINCTION

All barbiturates RAISE seizure threshold (anticonvulsant) EXCEPT:

Methohexital (Brevital) LOWERS seizure threshold

→ Can induce seizures in patients with seizure history. This is the opposite of every other barbiturate. Duration parallels propofol. Propofol has replaced it in modern practice.

"I would highly recommend propofol over the barbiturates."
EXAM IV Opioid Agonists — Fentanyl, Demerol & Serotonin Syndrome
IV Opioids — General

Same mechanism as oral opioids: bind µ receptors in CNS → inhibit ascending pain pathways → alter pain perception. Same side effects as oral EXCEPT for IV-specific effects below.

Side effects: respiratory depression, N/V, hypotension, drowsiness, histamine release

Contraindications: allergy, COPD/decreased respiratory reserve, MAO inhibitors (within 14 days), increased ICP

🔴 MEPERIDINE (DEMEROL) + MAOIs = SEROTONIN SYNDROME

MAOIs must be discontinued for at least 14 days before giving meperidine.

The three MAOIs to know: Nardil Marplan Parnate

Serotonin syndrome presentation: confusion, fever, diaphoresis (sweating), shivering, ataxia, myoclonus, hyperreflexia → coma and death

Three opioids that are weak SRIs: Meperidine Methadone Tramadol

Demerol unique niche: Alternate oral narcotic for patients allergic to codeine for moderate-severe pain. Prof says fentanyl is preferred for IV sedation.

Rapid IV infusion → skeletal muscle + chest wall rigidity → patient CANNOT ventilate → hypoxia, respiratory distress, apnea, bronchoconstriction, laryngospasm

Prevention: Don't give high doses. Inject slowly over 3–5 minutes.

🫁 Types of General Anesthesia — Overview

Three types: Inhalation Anesthetics, Neuroleptanesthesia, Dissociative Anesthesia.

Prof said he'd spend the most time on inhalation agents and dissociative (ketamine), and keep neuroleptanesthesia "to a very minimum." Prioritize ketamine and volatile agents for his exam.
💨 Inhalation Anesthetics

Most frequently used means of producing GA. Agents: N₂O, Halothane, Enflurane, Isoflurane, Desflurane, Sevoflurane.

Nitrous Oxide in GA Context

N₂O is NOT a good general anesthetic. Its MAC is 104–105% — physically impossible to deliver enough to achieve GA alone (can't give >100%). It's a weak general anesthetic. At >50% concentration you can achieve moderate sedation. A hyper-responder could drift to deep sedation — you must rescue them back.
N₂O's role in GA = Second Gas Effect / Potentiation. Running high-concentration N₂O alongside another volatile agent creates rapid uptake that concentrates the second gas in the alveoli → you need less of the more potent agent. Prof used the analogy of wind blowing over a puddle. Primary function of N₂O in GA: potentiate other agents.

Understanding MAC (Minimum Alveolar Concentration)

MAC = concentration at which 50% of patients won't respond to a surgical stimulus (without local anesthesia). Lower MAC = More Potent. If MAC is 0.75%, that's a strong anesthetic. If MAC is 6%, that's weak (need more drug).

⚠️ Introduction — Adverse Drug Reactions (ADRs)
  • ADRs continue despite prevention efforts during oral and IV drug administration
  • Dental office must: identify, document, and manage ADRs + contact primary care provider
  • Most ADRs are NOT life threatening — but some are, requiring immediate management
Three reasons ADRs occur:
  1. Dentist is at fault — but statistically the LEAST likely cause of medical emergencies
  2. Random events — unpredictable even when everything was done right
  3. Reasonable risk of treatment — e.g., syncope during injection = unavoidable complication

💊 OVERDOSE — Sedative-Hypnotics & Opioids

Definition: Toxic reaction from absolute or relative overadministration → elevated blood/plasma levels in target organs.

Core principle: Overdose manifestations are an exaggeration of the drug's normal pharmacologic effects. Sedative overdose → more sedation. Respiratory depressant overdose → more respiratory depression.
Barbiturates = greatest POTENTIAL for adverse reaction (narrow therapeutic index).
Opioids = greater NUMBER of adverse reactions (because more widely used).
Don't confuse "greatest potential" with "greatest number."

Titration & Routes

Only IV and inhalation routes permit titration. Oral, intranasal, IM = erratic absorption, no titration, no control. Once oral med is ingested → full pharmacologic effect until elimination.

Only IV allows both titration AND rapid reversal (for benzos and opioids).

  • Recent administration of sedative-hypnotic drug
  • Lack of response / extremely lethargic response to stimulation
  • Respiratory depression: RAPID rate, SHALLOW depth
  • Loss of motor coordination (ataxia)
  • Slurred speech

  • Respiratory depression: SLOW rate, NORMAL-to-DEEP depth

Management Steps — Overdose

Terminate treatment
Position patient — supine with legs slightly elevated
A-B-C — Look/Listen/Feel → Head tilt / Chin lift (simplest airway maneuver — relieves tongue, secretion, and muscle obstruction)
Definitive care: Summon help → O₂ → Monitor vitals → Establish IV → Identify drug → Give reversal if available (flumazenil for benzos, naloxone for opioids) OR BLS for barbiturates
Recovery & Discharge: Patient must stand/walk without assistance, custody of responsible adult. NEVER discharge alone or if not adequately recovered.
🚨 ALLERGY & ANAPHYLAXIS

Hypersensitive state from allergen exposure. Range: mild delayed reactions (up to 48 hrs) → immediate life-threatening reactions (seconds).

Only 2 drugs that have NEVER been shown to produce allergy: Nitrous oxide and Oxygen. (This was the fill-in-the-blank on the slide.)
"Epinephrine allergy" is almost always intravascular injection → sympathomimetic effects (↑HR, agitation). NOT a true allergy. Document what actually happened so future providers understand.

Drug-Specific Allergy Notes

  • Barbiturate allergy → skin lesions (hives, urticaria) or blood dyscrasias (agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia). More common in patients with history of asthma, urticaria, angioedema
  • Meperidine histamine release → redness along the vein path. This is NOT an allergy and requires no treatment (maybe topical gel). Classic MCQ distractor.

Allergy History Questions (5 Questions to Ask)

  1. What drug was used?
  2. What happened? (patient describes sequence)
  3. What treatment was required? (epi? histamine blocker? O₂?)
  4. Were physician/EMS required? Hospitalized?
  5. Name of doctor who treated you?

Clinical Manifestations

  • Most serious = Type I / Anaphylactic reaction — within 60 minutes. Targets: skin, respiratory, cardiovascular, GI
  • If hypotension is present → term = anaphylactic shock
  • One system only = localized anaphylaxis (e.g., asthma = respiratory only; urticaria = skin only)
Onset Rule: Faster onset = MORE intense reaction. Slower onset = less intense. If symptoms develop within seconds → expect severe/potentially fatal reaction.

Four Syndromes of Generalized Anaphylaxis

Typical anaphylaxis progresses through: (1) Skin → (2) Smooth muscle spasm → (3) Respiratory distress → (4) Cardiovascular collapse.
Fatal anaphylaxis: respiratory + CV disturbances are predominant and EARLY.

⬇️ HYPOTENSION

Definition: SBP <90 mmHg. Context matters — 90/60 in an ASA 1 young adult might be fine; same in elderly hypertensive = life-threatening.

Causes (11)

Excessive premedication, steroids/anti-HTN/tranquilizers, overdose of sedatives, reflex (pain), vascular absorption of local anesthetics, hemorrhage, positional changes, hypoxia/hypercarbia, CV abnormalities, adrenocortical insufficiency, metabolic derangement (hypo/hyperglycemia).

Signs & Symptoms

Chest pain, SBP <90, heart failure, restlessness, anxiety, disorientation, pallor, cold/clammy skin, dilated pupils, prolonged cap refill, dyspnea.

Dilated pupils = hypotension/shock. Constricted pupils (miosis) = opioid overdose. Opposite findings — classic distractor.

Management

P-A-B-C → Definitive care based on cause:

  • Inhalation anesthetic → decrease concentration
  • Opioid/benzo → give antagonist (naloxone / flumazenil)
  • Barbiturate → BLS (no reversal)
  • Fluids: Rapid IV infusion (D5W, normal saline, or Ringer's lactate)
  • Vasopressor: Dopamine — reserved for severe hypotension only
⬆️ HYPERTENSIVE EPISODES
Most common cause of intraoperative hypertension = patient is feeling PAIN (light anesthesia). Fix: give more local anesthesia. Transient elevations are usually well tolerated.

Causes (8)

Light anesthesia/sedation, pain, hypercarbia, hypoxia (these 4 → catecholamine release), emergence delirium, fluid overload, hyperthermia, pre-existing cardiac problems.

Hypertensive crisis: SBP ≥250 mmHg and/or DBP ≥130 mmHg → end-organ damage. Prof confirmed this is real — he's seen it. Most likely in patients with chronic, stable hypertension.

Goal: Avoid rapid BP changes — risk of compromising cerebral perfusion → stroke. Bring down gradually. Distinguish cardiac vs. non-cardiac cause.

Definitive Care

Monitor BP/HR q5 min + O₂ + fluids + summon EMS. Meds: Nitroprusside, IV nitroglycerin (prof said "not priority drug"), IV labetalol (beta blocker — prof added this, not on slide).

💓 CARDIAC DYSRHYTHMIAS
Dysrhythmia = most common intraoperative complication. Any deviation from normal sinus rhythm. Incidence under GA: 4–60%.

DeRango's 5 Observations (HIGH YIELD)

  1. Majority of continuously monitored anesthetized patients WILL show some dysrhythmia (this is normal)
  2. Higher incidence with history of heart disease
  3. Higher incidence in intubated patients
  4. More frequent in surgeries >3 hours
  5. Higher incidence in patients on digitalis preoperatively

Precipitating Factors (7)

Halothane (sensitizes myocardium — recurring theme), elevated CO₂, stimulation under light anesthesia (pain), vagal responses, intubation, anoxia, duration of procedure.

Management

Ensure adequate ventilation, adjust anesthesia/sedation level, provide pain control. Continuous ECG monitoring REQUIRED for deep sedation/GA.

Significance: dysrhythmias → decreased CO → cerebral/myocardial/renal ischemia, HF, pulmonary edema, MI, or V-fib.

Protective reflex preventing foreign matter entering larynx/trachea/lungs. Extraordinarily uncommon during minimal/moderate sedation.
