Master Framework: Subtractive vs Additive

Every perio surgical procedure is either subtractive (removing tissue/bone to reduce pockets) or additive (adding material to rebuild what's lost).

Subtractive Procedures
Subgingival root curettage (= SRP) · Gingival curettage (obsolete standalone) · External bevel gingivectomy · Internal bevel gingivectomy · ENAP (historical, not done) · Modified Widman flap · Osseous resective surgery · Distal wedge
Additive Procedures
Osseous graft · Guided tissue regeneration (GTR)

Key distinction (3 separate things people confuse): Open flap = access only, flap goes back where it was. Apically positioned flap (APF) = intentionally move tissue apically, usually WITH osseous surgery. Osseous surgery = modifying bone itself. Often combined but conceptually distinct.

Pre-Surgical Principles Checklist (slides)

Medical Hx (contraindications: uncontrolled HTN, uncontrolled DM) → Full tx plan BEFORE surgery → Understand anatomy of surgical area → Profound anesthesia → Aseptic technique (pre-surgical rinse reduces transient bacteria) → Atraumatic tissue management → Hemostasis → Atraumatic suturing → Obliterate dead space between flap and bone (dead space = clot accumulation = infection = failure) → Stable wound healing.

Anatomy — Know Where NOT to Cut
Maxillary Palate

Greater Palatine Foramen & Artery — ~2mm from posterior border of hard palate. Distance from alveolar crest to artery varies by vault depth: Shallow palate = ~7mm (HIGH RISK), Average = ~12mm, Deep/high = ~17mm. Same numbers from Pani's CTG harvest lecture.

Incisive Foramen — posterior to centrals, deep to incisive papilla. Nasopalatine NV bundle, supplies anterior palate canine-to-canine. In severe bone loss, may be much closer to crest than expected.

Mandible

Inferior alveolar canal (best seen on CBCT), mental foramen (visible on PA/pano), mandibular foramen, lingual nerve (runs along lingual of mandibular molars — injury risk during flap elevation).

The Subtractive Procedure Spectrum Essay 3

Listed least → most invasive. Understanding this spectrum IS the surgical decision-making framework.

1. Subgingival Root Curettage = SRP. Same thing, different name. Can be initial OR definitive therapy depending on reevaluation outcome.

2. Gingival Curettage — curette blade reversed to scrape inner pocket wall. No longer done standalone. Research shows no advantage over SRP alone. Happens inadvertently during SRP anyway. Don't bill separately.

3. External Bevel Gingivectomy — excise tissue from outside with #15 blade. TWO strict requirements: (1) sufficient KT to sacrifice and (2) no need to touch bone. If EITHER fails → wrong procedure. Maps to Coslet Type 1A (lots of KT + bone at normal distance from CEJ). Leaves raw open wound → slow healing → patients unhappy. Fleisher: "very few indications as standalone." Essay 1 Q3

4. Internal Bevel Gingivectomy — incision from gingival margin angling toward bone, thinning tissue. Same tissue reduction as external BUT as part of a FLAP. Wound covered when you close.

5. ENAP — historical only. Attempted to excise pocket lining for new attachment. Didn't work. Know the name exists.

6. Modified Widman Flap — flap for ACCESS to roots without intentionally repositioning tissue or removing bone. Clean roots under direct vision, replace flap original position. More conservative than osseous resective.

7. Osseous Resective Surgery — full combination: flap + debridement + osteoplasty/ostectomy + APF + suturing. The most comprehensive subtractive procedure.

8. Distal Wedge — tissue removal distal to last molar (tuberosity/retromolar pad area).

KEY RULE Gingival curettage and gingivectomy = suprabony defects ONLY. You do NOT gain access to bone defects with either. If infrabony defect exists → MUST raise a flap.
Full Thickness vs Split Thickness — THE Decision Essay 1 & 3
Full Thickness (Mucoperiosteal) Split Thickness (Partial)
Incision goes TO crest of bone. Peel EVERYTHING off (epithelium + CT + periosteum). Bare bone exposed. Peel outer layers only (epithelium + some CT). Periosteum stays on bone.
✓ Direct access to bone
✓ Less technique sensitive
✓ Faster		 ✓ Allows apical positioning (periosteum = bed)
✓ Maintains periosteal blood supply
✗ Technique sensitive, perforation risk
✗ Takes longer
Use when: need to SEE/MODIFY bone (osseous surgery, GTR membrane, CL assessment) Use when: need to REPOSITION flap (apically or coronally) or maintain periosteal blood supply

Why split allows repositioning: Flap is freed from bone but periosteum stays as a "carpet." Flap slides over the periosteum in any direction. Full thickness = flap includes periosteum, nothing to slide over.

The Combination (split-full): Start split coronally (thin tissue, preserve periosteum for suturing) → transition to full thickness apically (bone access). Best of both worlds. Same concept as Pani's split-full-split for CAF.

Release at MGJ: Split thickness dissection should go BEYOND the MGJ. Tissue below MGJ = alveolar mucosa = elastic/stretchy = flap mobility. Stop above MGJ = flap still tethered.

FLEISHER Palate CANNOT do APF. No mucogingival junction on palate = all keratinized tissue = no elastic mucosa to release into = no mobility. Workaround: submarginal incision to excise excess palatal tissue. Safe because palate has ABUNDANT KT.
The KT Decision Tree (Fleisher uses this EVERY case)

Q1: How much KT do I have?

Q2: Do I need to touch bone?

Plenty KT + no bone needed → External gingivectomy (simplest)

Plenty KT + need bone access → Internal bevel (submarginal), full thickness flap, osseous surgery, APF. Can sacrifice some KT.

Limited KT + need bone access → Crevicular/sulcular incision (preserve every mm of KT), split thickness flap, APF to carry KT apically.

Limited KT + no bone needed → Consider if surgery is even needed. Ortho eruption? Deep margin elevation?

Flap design rule: Vertical release incisions — base always wider than coronal (blood supply enters at base). Extend vertical releases to MGJ and beyond for mobility.

Pocket Classification

False pocket (pseudopocket): Excess soft tissue, probe does NOT extend past CEJ. No attachment loss. Just swollen tissue.

True pocket: Increased PD AND probe extends BEYOND CEJ. Attachment loss present.

Suprabony: Pocket bottom ABOVE (coronal to) bone crest. Soft tissue problem worse than bone problem.

Infrabony: Irregularity WITHIN bone. Pocket bottom extends BELOW bone crest. Bone eaten away in irregular pattern.

Why Bone Resorbs Differently by Location

Thick bone → infrabony defects (enough volume for inflammation to carve out a defect while leaving walls). Thin bone → horizontal loss (inflammation dissolves entire thin plate uniformly). Think: thick cliff → cave; thin wall → crumbles.

This is why: buccal bone (thin) → horizontal loss. Interproximal bone (thick) → craters/infrabony defects. Slides: "Most pockets are found interproximally."

Root proximity & marrow space: Where roots diverge = marrow = inflammatory pathway = infrabony defects. Where roots close together = thin cortical = horizontal loss.

Why Interproximal Pockets Are Deepest

Buccal/lingual: when attachment lost, gingival margin RECEDES (tissue shrinks back → shallow PD despite attachment loss). Interproximal: papillary tissue does NOT recede as much → deep POCKETING (tissue stays high while bone goes low).

Bone Architecture

Positive architecture (GOAL): Radicular bone APICAL to interdental septum. Natural scallop — higher between teeth, lower on buccal/lingual. More scalloping anteriorly (narrow teeth), flatter posteriorly (wider teeth).

Reverse architecture (PATHOLOGIC): Interproximal bone LOWER than buccal/palatal bone. Normal scallop inverted. Tissue follows this and creates persistent pockets regardless of hygiene.

Flattened architecture: Bone at same level everywhere. Intermediate — no scallop, no reversal.

MASTER PRINCIPLE "Soft tissue does NOT conform to underlying osseous topography." You CANNOT tell what bone looks like by looking at tissue. Tissue drapes over irregular bone like a tablecloth over a bumpy table. Must PROBE to find defects. Radiographs can miss them too (thick buccal plate masks interproximal defects on PA).

CEJ Discrepancy trap: When adjacent teeth have CEJs at different levels (tipping, supereruption), bone between them appears angled on radiograph. Looks like angular bone loss but may be normal. Check CEJ-to-crest distance on EACH tooth — if 1-2mm on both sides, it's just anatomy, not disease.

Bone Sounding

Anesthetize → push probe through tissue to bone. Horizontal probing from facial/lingual = locate crestal walls. Vertical probing = find base of defect. Maps 3D architecture before raising flap.

FLEISHER Does bone sounding with the anesthetic needle during injection — thinner than probe, easier to push through. "A little game I play to see if I can guess what it's gonna look like."

Furcation Classification
ClassDefinition
IHorizontal bone loss <1/3 of buccolingual width
IIHorizontal loss >1/3 but NOT through-and-through
IIIThrough-and-through — probe passes completely from one entrance to another

Mandibular molars: 2 entrances (buccal, lingual). Maxillary molars: 3 entrances (buccal between MB/DB roots, mesial between MB/palatal, distal between DB/palatal). If one max furcation = Class III → at LEAST one more must also be Class III (probe must enter AND exit).

Root anatomy: MB root = most oblong (furcation entrance further palatal, access from palatal side). DB root = more round (easier access). Palatal root = most conical.

Use Nabers probe (curved, designed for furcations). Not a regular straight probe.

Ostectomy vs Osteoplasty Essay 1 & 3
Ostectomy
Removal of SUPPORTING bone (radicular and interradicular). Eliminates osseous defects. Trade-off: deliberately removing attachment. Used to drop bone level for biologic width or deep infrabony defects.
Osteoplasty
Removal of NON-SUPPORTING bone for reshaping. Thinning ledges, tori, exostoses, shallow craters, blunted interdental craters, small intrabony defects on B/L, enhanced flap placement. NOT removing attachment.

Evidence (Selipsky): Very little supporting bone is removed when resective techniques properly applied. Need for splinting should be determined PRIOR to procedure.

Osseous Resective Therapy

Objectives: Create contours permitting effective plaque control → create bone contours tissue will parallel → permit primary wound closure → expose additional clinical crown for restorative.

Disadvantages: Attachment loss from removing supporting bone, unaesthetic root exposure, hypersensitive roots.

Indications: Adequate remaining attachment after physiologic morphology established, no esthetic/anatomic/sensitivity limitations.

Contraindications: Inadequate remaining attachment, esthetic limitations, anatomic limitations, sensitivity limitations, alternative more effective therapy exists.

FLEISHER NO resective surgery in ANTERIOR region unless full prosthetic reconstruction. Aesthetic result of dropping bone + exposing roots in smile zone = unacceptable. Also: don't remove so much bone that a stable tooth becomes unstable.
Double Parabolization

For multi-rooted teeth — treat each root SEPARATELY (like two premolars). Drop bone on mesial root surface, come BACK UP at furcation, drop again on distal root surface. Each root gets its own parabolic curve with a vertical groove in interdental bone extending apically from the furca. Prevents furcation exposure.

Root trunk: Often narrowest for first molars (shortest distance CEJ → furcation entrance). "The relationship of the defect to the root trunk" matters more than depth in mm for multi-rooted teeth.

Bone Instruments

Carbide bur: Efficient but too aggressive for perio. Better for OMFS with large bone removal. Diamond bur: Fleisher's preference — slower, more controlled, finer precision. Always copious water irrigation (prevent overheating → necrosis).

Hand instruments for fine-tuning: Ochsenbein chisel (widow's peaks, bony spines), back-action chisel (opposite direction access), Wedelstaedt chisel (incremental removal). Universal curette for small interproximal spicules.

Osseous Resective Surgery — Full Procedure Essay 3
Pre-op assessment (charting + rads) → Scalloped internal bevel incision → Flap elevation (full or split thickness) → Remove granulation tissue → Identify bone defects → Root debridement under direct vision → Osteoplasty (reshape non-supporting) → Ostectomy if needed (remove supporting) → Probe check (verify positive architecture) → APF repositioning + suturing → Perio dressing → Post-op instructions

Why scalloped incision: Tissue must match bone architecture after closing. Straight incision → tissue won't conform → pocketing.

Why debride granulation tissue first: Can't assess bone architecture until inflammatory mess is cleaned out.

Smooth roots = healing. Rough roots = harbor bacteria, prevent attachment.

APF advantages: Predictability, achieves minimal PDs, potential to increase zone of attached gingiva, stable base for restorative.

Target: ≤3mm probing depths → allows effective maintenance. Goal = ~2mm sulcus everywhere when possible.

Split Thickness APF — Video Walkthrough

1) Vertical release at mesiolingual line angle of premolar, extends BEYOND MGJ into elastic mucosa. 2) Inverse bevel incision mesial to distal — "an APF is really a gingival graft procedure where we move the gingival zone from coronal to apical position" = RELOCATING KT, not removing it. 3) Horizontal release in retromolar pad. 4) Papillae thinned and undermined while attached at base. 5) Split thickness elevation — periosteum stays. Internal wedge dissected and removed. 6) Elevate WELL BEYOND MGJ for mobility (most common perforation site). 7) Flap falls freely, repositioned apically.

Combination tissue reduction: Sometimes submarginal incision (reduce tissue coronally) + APF (move complex apically). Result: don't need to move flap as far. ONLY if excess KT exists to sacrifice.

Suturing Techniques Essay 1
1. Simple Interrupted

Enter outer aspect one side → pierce through → exit other side → tie knot. Surgeon's knot: 2 throws one direction (double first throw = friction hold) + 1 throw opposite direction = 3 throws minimum. Advantage: if one suture fails, others hold. "Very straightforward" but not ideal for most perio procedures.

2. Continuous Suture

Fleisher: "I don't like them. I never use them." One failure = entire suture line fails = disaster. Faster to place but all-or-nothing risk.

3. Vertical Mattress — THE Perio Suture

Fleisher: "probably the primary suture technique I'll use, particularly if I am apically positioning a flap."

Technique step-by-step: 1) Enter through buccal flap JUST apical to MGJ (not far into vestibule — prevents tissue bunching/aphthous ulcers). 2) As needle traverses, ROLL it — start perpendicular to bone, rotate to become PARALLEL with bone surface. 3) Grab periosteum (why you did split thickness). 4) Come out through periosteum. 5) Re-enter buccal tissue. 6) Go around to lingual, grab lingual tissue. 7) Come back to buccal. 8) Tie knot WHERE YOU FIRST ENTERED (most apical point, near MGJ).

WHY tie apically: Tying at first (apical) entry pulls tissue DOWN → secures flap in apical position. Tying at crest would pull coronally = defeats purpose.

Needle rolling technique: Use needle curvature — start perpendicular, roll to travel parallel along bone. Catches periosteum WITHOUT hitting bone (blunts needle tip + unnecessary). Center suture under peak of tissue for even tension.

Two Suture Types in One Surgery

Flap (keratinized tissue): Non-resorbable (silk or PTFE) — stays until removed at 1 week. KT is tough, removal painless.

Vertical releases (mucosal tissue): Resorbable (gut 3-4 days, chromic gut ~6 days) — dissolves on its own, no painful removal from sensitive mucosa.

Needle Types

Shapes: Half curve (1/2 circle) — common. 5/8 circle — tight spaces. Straight — "we don't use" in perio.

REVERSE CUTTING = the perio needle. Cutting edge on OUTER curve. When hand naturally pulls upward during suturing, sharp edge faces AWAY from tissue. Inner curve (facing tissue you're holding) is DULL. Prevents tear-through.

Conventional cutting: Cutting edge on INNER curve = BAD for perio. Natural upward pull → slices through flap.

Taper: Round body, no cutting edges. Pushes through by separating fibers. Delicate tissue.

Taper cut: Taper body + cutting TIP. Sharp tip pierces easily, round body doesn't cut passing through. "Extra sharp edge, which is great."

Periodontal Dressing

Evidence: Multiple studies — no one has shown perio pack makes healing better OR worse. Statistically no difference.

Fleisher uses it anyway — 3 reasons: 1) Protects patient FROM THEMSELVES (no looking, playing, brushing). 2) No home care needed in surgical area week 1. 3) Patient comfort/compression.

Placement: Work packing INTO interproximal spaces for retention. Hardest to keep: palate after FGG harvest (no interproximals to lock into).

Avitene trick: Place Avitene (collagen microfibrillar clotting agent) OVER graft/surgical site BEFORE pack. Creates barrier so pack doesn't stick to tissue or lock onto sutures. If pack bonds to suture and you pull → tears the flap.

If pack falls off: not a disaster. Protective, not essential for healing.

Post-Op Protocol

Instructions (WRITTEN): Rest remainder of day. Pain meds BEFORE anesthesia wears off. Antibiotics if prescribed (full course). NSAID + ICE for swelling (sends patient home WITH ice pack — day of + next day). Explain normal vs concerning bleeding. No brushing surgical area while pack in place. "Now is not the time to go on a diet" — body needs fuel to heal. Emergency contact (Fleisher gives personal number).

Suture removal: 1 week to 10 days post-op. EVERY surgical patient seen regardless of suture type. Evaluate: suture removal + pack removal + healing assessment + home care readiness.

FLEISHER Record entry: "All of your records are horrible, lousy." Records = ONLY defense in malpractice. Do FRESH assessment at reevaluation — don't copy-paste old findings. Same principle as the 16% charting stat (McFaul).
SRP Reevaluation Timing

Window: 2-8 weeks after completion of ALL quadrants. If huge gap between first and last quad → can't just evaluate based on last quad finish.

Why 3 months is hard deadline: Bacteria have repopulated, inflammation increasing again. Always evaluate at tissue's BEST, not WORST. If patient missed window → do recall cleaning FIRST → then evaluate a few weeks after that cleaning.

Fresh assessment — don't copy clinical findings from previous date. Diagnosis may have changed.

Critical Probing Depth — When Surgery Beats SRP Essay 3

LITERATURE Lindhe: 4.2mm — in sites with initial PDs above 4.2mm, more CAL gain with Widman flap surgery than SRP alone.

FLEISHER "I think that's a little on the low side." Won't recommend surgery if all PDs are 4mm. Wants ≥5mm with BOP before recommending surgery.

LITERATURE Caffesse 1986: 65% inadequate debridement at ≥5mm. SRP can't adequately clean deep pockets → need surgical access.

Crown Lengthening Video — Surgical Pearls Essay 1

1) Intrasulcular incision preserving ALL KT, extends into sulcus of 2 adjacent teeth, papillae thinned. 2) Buccal: split thickness past MGJ → extreme mobility for APF. 3) Palatal: thinned palatal flap with paramarginal incision (more accentuated on target tooth), multiple parallel incisions thin the flap, then at most apical extent → FULL THICKNESS to reach bone (split-full combination on palate). 4) Ostectomy: diamond ball bur + rodded chisel → 3-4mm from bone to future restorative margin → scalloped physiologic architecture. 5) Universal curette eliminates widow's peaks. 6) Probe check before closing. 7) ePTFE suture: vertical periosteal mattress buccal, horizontal mattress palatal. 8) Buccal flap at bone crest level. 9) 6 months healing before final prosthesis (aesthetic case).

SUTURING PEARL Surgeons in video enter suture TOO FAR apical in mucosal tissue → tissue BUNCHES UP → discomfort → aphthous ulcers. Fleisher's correction: enter JUST apical to MGJ. Small difference, significant patient comfort.
Clinical Case Pearls
19-Year-Old Molar Case

Single parabolization on distal root only (where decay was). Zero money, zero insurance, about to lose all molar occlusion on one side. Minimum effective intervention: CL to expose enough tooth for endo + temp restorations. Not ideal but functional. Teeth can last years, buying time.

Bruxer Case — Worn Dentition

Ground ALL teeth down. Wax-up + surgical template (restoratively driven). Split thickness APF. Significant bone removal BUT bruxers build up extra supporting bone — heavy forces stimulate deposition. "Rock solid" teeth could withstand bone removal. Vertical mattress suturing apically. Healing: 1-2 weeks raw → 2-3 weeks great. Significant sensitivity with this much root exposure → manage aggressively (perio pack → SuperSeal → final restoration). Not for every case — assess bone volume, crown-to-root ratio, perio status individually.

Margins on Cementum

"It doesn't have to be on the crown." Many clinical situations require finishing apical to CEJ on root surface. As long as adequate biologic width (3-4mm from margin to bone crest) and patient can maintain → margins on cementum work.

Exposed Tooth for Ortho

Limited KT in area. Instead of simple window (tooth erupts into mucosal tissue → needs graft later), Fleisher raised flap, took KT sitting apically, APF'd it so as tooth erupts, KT moves with it. Saves future graft. "Simple design, thinking ahead."

Denture Resorption Warning

Denture on ridge → pressure → continuous bone RESORPTION ("like a guarantee"). Eventually too little bone for implants. Solution: place implants early (even under removable prosthesis) → implant maintains bone. Same principle as socket preservation — preserve bone at time of extraction, not years later.

Foundational Studies — Know COLD All Essays
Löe 1965 — Experimental Gingivitis LITERATURE

Design: 12 participants, healthy gingiva. Withdrew all oral hygiene → 10-20 days to develop gingivitis → reinstituted hygiene → 4-10 days to resolve.

PROVED: Plaque accumulation causes gingivitis. 100% developed it. 100% reversed. Universal, guaranteed, reversible.

EXAM TRAP Fleisher literally says: "I don't want anyone to write about how this study showed the relationship between gingivitis and periodontitis. IT DOESN'T." Löe proved plaque → gingivitis. Did NOT prove plaque → periodontitis. Did NOT prove gingivitis → periodontitis. Most gingivitis does NOT progress to periodontitis. Different diseases.
Socransky 1984 — Episodic Breakdown LITERATURE

Design: Longitudinal, observational. 64 patients, 0/3/6 years, NO treatment during entire period.

Old paradigm destroyed: Average annual loss = 0.18mm/site → looks like slow, continuous, progressive disease. WRONG.

What he actually found: Only 12% of sites lost significant attachment (>2mm) during entire 6 years. 88% stable. Of sites losing >2mm in first 3 years, only 40% showed further loss in next 3 years (60% went quiet). ~50% of sites stable in first 3 years showed loss in second 3 years (previously stable sites suddenly active).

Paradigm shift: Periodontitis is EPISODIC, not continuous. Sites break down in BURSTS — multiple mm in short period, then quiet for months/years. Cannot predict which sites will break down next.

Two models: 1) Random burst — breakdown random among susceptible sites, prior history doesn't predict future. 2) Asynchronous multiple burst — clustering of activity in relatively short period, prolonged remission months-years, then infrequent isolated bursts.

Clinical implication: Can't predict breakdown → 3-month recall is the defense. Monitor frequently, disrupt bacteria, catch new breakdown early. Posterior interproximal sites most susceptible.

Eke Prevalence Study (2009-2010) LITERATURE

Evolution of measurement: 1999-2000: 2 sites/tooth, 2 random quads → 19.5%. 2001-2004: 3 sites/tooth, 2 quads → 27%. 2009-2010: 6 sites/tooth, full mouth → 47%. Same population, same classification — only changed HOW THOROUGHLY they looked.

The gotcha: 47% used only 4 interproximal sites (CDC-AAP definition). When ALL 6 sites measured (including mid-B/L), actual prevalence = 56%. This detail was in the article body, NOT the abstract.

Breakdown: 8.7% mild, 30% moderate, 8.5% severe.

Slide MC answer: "What is the prevalence?" — answer is E (none of the above) because actual 6-point number is 56%. But D (all of the above) is also defensible since all three numbers are "correct" for their respective protocols.

Clinical: 1 in 2 adult Americans has periodontitis. If not doing complete 6-site full-mouth assessments, you're missing disease.

2025 Bridge Longevity Study LITERATURE

Design: 94 patients, 111 long-span bridges, average follow-up 17.9 years. Stage III/IV periodontitis. 90 tooth-supported, 21 tooth-implant supported.

Results: 25 bridges: patients dropped out (bridges still working). 18: patients died (still working). 16: failed. 52: still intact — average 20.4 years, range 13-30 years. 76% success rate.

Fleisher's thesis: Good perio surgery + good prosthetics + good maintenance = decades of function, even in severe periodontitis. Don't extract prematurely. Quality of work + patient compliance determines longevity.

Study Age Principle

"A study that's 1 year old can be irrelevant and a study that's 50 years old can be just as relevant today." Quality determines relevance, not date. Löe 1965 never disproven. Gargiulo 1961 still biologic width standard.

Essay Topic Connections — Quick Reference
Essay 1: Crown Lengthening

Biologic width (Gargiulo 1961: CT ~1mm + JE ~1mm = ~2mm; need 3-4mm margin to bone). Gingivectomy alone = adequate KT + no bone modification needed (Coslet 1A). Otherwise flap + bone. Healing: posterior 6-8wks, anterior ≥3mo, movement up to 12mo. Complications: bleeding (additional sutures), pain/swelling (NSAID + ice + narcotic), sensitivity (perio pack → SuperSeal → final restoration). Full vs split thickness decision drives everything. Vertical mattress through periosteum, tied apically.

Essay 2: GTR/GBR

Defect classification determines predictability: 3-wall best, 1-wall worst. Combination defects get combination tx. PTFE suture = same material as membrane. Suture choice matters — monofilament (no bacterial wicking) for membrane procedures.

Essay 3: Non-Surgical/Surgical Therapy

SRP = initial or definitive (depends on outcome). Lindhe 4.2mm critical PD. Caffesse 1986: 65% inadequate at ≥5mm. Surgery for what SRP can't fix. The entire subtractive procedure spectrum. Fleisher's clinical threshold: ≥5mm + BOP. Nyman 1977: without maintenance, pocket depths rebound regardless of surgical technique. Socransky 1984: episodic breakdown → 3-month recall defense. The 75yo vs 30yo scenario: same probing depths, same tx principles, but consider systemic health, healing capacity, life expectancy, compliance.

Essay 4: Occlusal Trauma

Bruxer case: heavy forces cause wear BUT can stimulate bone deposition. Same patient = destroyed enamel + solid bone. Occlusal trauma affects teeth/PDL but can strengthen bone through adaptive response (until threshold exceeded). 2017 classification: "excessive occlusal force" replaced with "traumatic occlusal force."
