CAL — The Holy Grail of Periodontal Diagnosis

Why CAL Is King

CAL (Clinical Attachment Level) is the single most important measurement in periodontics. Probing depth alone can lie in both directions — pseudopockets inflate PD, recession hides severity. CAL cuts through both lies.

  • 1–3mm probing depths = healthy
  • 4mm+ = where concern starts
  • The tooth needs bone and gingiva holding it in — lose that attachment and you lose the tooth

The CAL Formula — Two Scenarios

Recession Present

Gingival margin is apical to CEJ — you can see root surface exposed

CAL = PD + Recession

You're adding because attachment has been lost in both directions

Gingival Margin Coronal to CEJ

Tissue is swollen up and over where it normally sits — pseudopocket territory

CAL = PD − Coronal Excess

You subtract because some of that PD is just swollen gingiva, not real attachment loss

Clinical Scenarios — Lock These In

ScenarioPDGM PositionCAL CalculationInterpretation
Pseudopocket 6mm 2mm coronal to CEJ 6 − 2 = 4mm Still not great, but better than 6mm suggested
Recession 4mm 3mm apical to CEJ (exposed root) 4 + 3 = 7mm Way worse than PD alone would tell you
The One-Liner to Remember
Pseudopocket = swollen gingiva makes PD look worse than reality.
Recession = exposed root makes PD look better than reality.
CAL cuts through both lies.
Mnemonic
"Can I see root? Add. Can't find CEJ? Subtract."

Pseudopocket vs True Pocket

The Midterm Trap — 75% Got This Wrong
Dr. Fleisher sent a department-wide email with bold red text because 75% of the class got the pseudopocket question wrong. The answer was D: none of the above — not radiographs, not probing alone, not even the combination. The question had a good point biserial (discriminated high from low performers), meaning there was a fundamental teaching gap, not just a tricky question.

The ONLY Way to Determine a Pseudopocket

"You cannot accurately, reliably, or predictably assess attachment loss from a radiograph."
— Dr. Fleisher

The ONLY way to determine if a pseudopocket exists is by identifying the location of the gingival margin relative to the CEJ and recording it as RECESSION. That's it. That's the whole thing.

Positive Recession

Gingival margin is apical to CEJ

True recession — you can see root surface

→ True pocket territory

Negative Recession

Gingival margin is coronal to CEJ

Tissue is covering/above the CEJ — swollen over it

→ Pseudopocket territory

Radiograph Limitations — The 50% Problem

The Three-Panel Case That Proves the Point

Fleisher showed three panels, left to right:

Left: Clinical Photo

Probe going into a deep pocket on the facial. Tissue looks relatively normal from the outside.

Center: PA Radiograph

Interproximal bone looks fine — maybe even normal.

Right: Flap Raised

Massive bone destruction on the buccal/facial aspect — completely hidden on the radiograph.

Why Radiographs Only Show ~50% of the Bone

When you shoot a PA, the x-ray beam comes from one direction and flattens a 3D structure into a 2D image.

Visible (~50%)

Interproximal bone — mesial and distal. The x-ray beam passes through these areas without much overlapping structure.

Invisible (~50%)

Buccal and lingual/palatal bone — superimposed directly OVER the tooth root on the image. Even if buccal bone is completely destroyed, the intact lingual bone behind it makes everything look fine.

Mnemonic
"X-rays see between teeth, not around them."
Or: "Radiographs are HALF the story — Mesial and Distal only, Buccal and Lingual are BLIND SPOTS."

Why We Need BOTH Probing AND Radiographs

  • Probing can lie — pseudopockets inflate PD, recession hides severity
  • Radiographs can lie — they only show interproximal bone well, can't see buccal/lingual bone loss easily
  • Together they give you the real picture — but even together, they still can't show you everything (hence why we sometimes need to raise a flap)

Biologic Width — Same Radiograph Limitation

  • You cannot determine biologic width invasion from a radiograph either
  • Gargiulo study gave AVERAGES (2.07mm) with huge variability (less than 0.5mm to over 3mm)
  • The only way to assess biologic width invasion: place the temp restoration, have the patient come back in about a week, remove it, and look for inflammation
  • No inflammation = no invasion, regardless of what the radiograph shows

Socransky's Burst Theory (1984)

Periodontitis Does NOT Progress Continuously

Periodontitis progresses in asynchronous bursts — periods of active destruction followed by periods of complete remission. A site can be quiet for years, then suddenly lose attachment, then go quiet again.

Critical Implication
When you probe and find attachment loss, you're measuring a historical record of past destruction. You have NO idea if the site is actively breaking down RIGHT NOW. There are currently no reliable tests to determine if a site is in the active process of losing attachment.

The "Chronic Disease" Nuance

Definition
Chronic disease: "A disease which has a prolonged course, does not resolve spontaneously, and for which a complete cure is rarely achieved."
  • For the exam: call periodontitis chronic — answer YES it's chronic
  • But know the nuance: Socransky would argue this doesn't fit — disease goes into full remission at sites, sometimes permanently
  • If Fleisher asks a nuanced question about Socransky's perspective, know that Socransky would disagree with the "chronic" label

Critical Concept
It's the HOST RESPONSE that does the damage, not the bacteria directly. The bacteria trigger your immune system, and your immune system's inflammatory response is what actually destroys the tissue and bone. Two patients can have similar bacteria but totally different disease because their host responses are different.
Analogy
Bacteria are the match, but your immune system is the gasoline. Some people have a little gasoline (mild response, minimal destruction). Some people are walking around with a full tank (aggressive response, rapid destruction). Same match, very different outcomes.

This explains why risk factors matter so much. Smoking doesn't directly cause periodontitis — it modifies the host response. Diabetes doesn't directly cause it either — it alters the inflammatory and healing pathways. They're pouring MORE gasoline on the fire, not lighting additional matches.
07

Risk Factors vs Causation

The Critical Distinction

Cause

Bacteria / microbial biofilm = the suspected precipitating etiologic agent

Even this isn't 100% definitively proven beyond all doubt — but for the exam, bacteria = cause

Risk Factors

Smoking, diabetes, genetics — these increase the likelihood of disease. They predispose you. But they are NOT causal.

Plenty of smokers never get periodontitis. Plenty of diabetics don't either.

Exam Logic
What CAUSES periodontitis? → Bacteria / microbial biofilm
What INCREASES RISK? → Smoking, diabetes, genetics, etc.

Don't mix up causation and association. Association ≠ causation.

Extraction vs Conservation Philosophy

Fleisher's Core Teaching Points

  • Don't make the extraction decision based on the radiograph alone when decay is involved. Radiographs don't accurately represent decay 100% of the time — sometimes there's MORE decay than the radiograph shows, sometimes there's LESS
  • If you're on the fence, you MUST remove the decay first and then assess. Only after you've cleaned out the caries and can see how much sound tooth structure remains should you make the call
  • A lot of teeth that get condemned in clinic could actually be saved. 90% of faculty would look at some cases and say "pull it" without thinking twice, but with proper perio surgery (crown lengthening) and good restorative work, those teeth can have excellent long-term prognosis
  • Your initial visual assessment of restorability is not sufficient — you need to go through a methodical checklist (Avila 2009 factors) before condemning a tooth
"If you don't remove the decay, you won't know for sure what you have left."

The Root Amputation Case — A Detective Story

  • Persistent bleeding around a maxillary molar. Radiograph shown — someone shouts "biologic width violation!"
  • The clue everyone missed: there weren't 3 canals visible. A maxillary molar should have at least 3 canals (MB, DB, palatal). The missing canal was the clue — the mesiobuccal root had been amputated
  • The previous dentist removed the MB root but kept the existing crown to save the patient money. Where the root used to be = massive overhang. Crown extends over empty space. Plaque and bacteria get trapped. Patient cannot physically clean it.
  • Result: chronic persistent bleeding and inflammation
Two Teaching Points
1. Be detail oriented. "You have chosen a profession that deals with minutia." Missing a canal on a radiograph, not noticing something looks off — that's how you miss diagnoses.

2. If doing root amputation → MUST redo the crown. Need to reshape the prep to account for the missing root. Root amputations are rarely done now — implants offer more predictable outcomes.

Why Flaps Exist — Three Levels of Treatment

The Escalation Ladder

LevelWhat It IsHow It WorksLimitation
1 OHI (Oral Hygiene Instructions) Patient brushes and flosses better. Handles superficial stuff — plaque on accessible surfaces. Works great for gingivitis. But if the pocket is deep, the patient physically CANNOT clean it. An 8mm pocket is impossible for the patient to keep clean.
2 Non-Surgical (SRP) Go in with instruments subgingivally, blindly, through the pocket opening. Clean the root surface of calculus and bacteria by touch. Works really well for pockets up to about 5mm. As pockets get deeper (6, 7, 8mm), you're reaching further in blind. Can't see if you got everything. Can't see the shape of the bony defect.
3 Surgical (Raise the Flap) Cut the tissue, peel it back. NOW you can see the root surface directly, see the bone defect, see exactly where the calculus is. Clean everything under direct vision. Then decide: access flap? Osseous surgery? GTR/bone grafts?
Analogy
Imagine you dropped your keys down a narrow storm drain.
Level 1 = reaching in with your hand — maybe you get them if they're right at the top
Level 2 = using a long stick to fish around blindly — you might get lucky
Level 3 = popping the grate off so you can actually SEE the keys and grab them directly

That's what raising a flap does — it pops the grate off.
Bottom Line
Non-surgical therapy = cleaning blind through the pocket.
Surgical therapy = opening it up so you can see and treat directly.
Everything else in surgical lectures — flap types, incision designs, suturing — is just the details of HOW you open it up and close it back up. The WHY is what's here.

Key Cases & Clinical Pearls

The 30-Year-Old Premolar Case

  • 30-year-old patient, only TWO sites in the entire mouth probing 8mm+, both on facial of premolars (contralateral teeth)
  • Healthy looking mouth, not a ton of calculus, not a lot of bleeding everywhere
  • Fleisher tested endo multiple times (teeth were vital), asked about ortho (patient never had it), and eventually had to accept it was periodontal
Teaching Point
Very localized, very severe attachment loss at isolated sites should make you SUSPICIOUS that it's NOT periodontitis. Look for other explanations first — endo issues, orthodontic history, trauma. The instinct to question isolated severe defects is what Fleisher wants you to have.

The Late-20s Aggressive Perio Case

  • Woman in her late 20s, Stage 3 progressing to Stage 4 (number of teeth already missing)
  • Class shouts "dental implants!" and "take out all the teeth!"
  • Fleisher pushes back — a woman in her late 20s needs her teeth for another 50-60 years
  • Full extraction and implants in a late-20s patient with aggressive perio is a HUGE decision with massive long-term implications
Teaching Point
Don't jump to extraction without exhausting other options. Treat what's treatable, extract only what's truly hopeless, consider regenerative procedures on salvageable teeth, and manage this as a long-term case.

Crown Lengthening Is a Goal, Not a Technique

First thing Fleisher says — first thing you write on the essay: Crown lengthening is NOT "the crown lengthening procedure." It's a GOAL. The goal is: expose more clinical crown.

Multiple Techniques Can Achieve the Goal

  • Osseous surgery (primary method Fleisher teaches — raising a flap and removing bone)
  • Gingivectomy
  • Orthodontic eruption
  • Combination of any of the above

Restoratively Driven

Your slide: "CROWN LENGTHENING — a periodontal procedure, restoratively driven."

  • The periodontist isn't deciding independently — something happened with the TOOTH (decay, fracture, wear, short crown) that puts the restorative margin in a danger zone relative to bone
  • The restorative need drives the perio procedure
  • Goes beyond crowns — even a simple composite or amalgam that ends up subgingival can invade biologic width
  • Every single time a restorative margin goes near or below the gingival margin = potential biologic width issue
02

The Live Surgical Case — 11 Steps

Step 1: Assess the Situation

Prepped teeth with temps removed. Where are the prep margins relative to the sulcus? Relative to bone? Some areas clearly subgingival — you can't tell everything from just looking. Crown lengthening is needed.

Step 2: Evaluate Keratinized Tissue FIRST

The single most important assessment before you pick up a blade. How much KT do you have? It determines your ENTIRE incision design:

KT StatusIncision DesignRationale
Enough or extra KTSubmarginal incision (internal bevel, slightly away from margin)You can sacrifice a little KT because you have plenty to spare
Not enough KTCrevicular/sulcular incision to preserve every mmYou're not giving up anything — cutting KT away creates a mucogingival problem on top of the restorative problem
From Surgical Principles slides: "Amount of keratinized gingiva will have an impact on how you design your incision." THIS is where that principle gets applied. Connects to Pani's lecture too — if you don't have enough KT and cut some away, bad day.

Step 3: Raise the Flap

  • Fleisher does a split thickness elevation — leaves the periosteum on the bone
  • Reason 1: He can position the flap apically later (periosteum stays put while flap slides down)
  • Reason 2: The periosteum gives him something to SUTURE THROUGH when closing — critical for Step 9
  • Reflects down to approximately the mucogingival junction to release the flap
  • Makes a vertical incision for apical-coronal mobility ("base is always wider than the gingival margin")

Step 4: Distal Wedge + Debridement

Simultaneously does a distal wedge to release the distal area and cleans out granulation tissue. Already combining techniques in one surgery.

Step 5: Refine Tissue Contours — The Gingivectomy Move

Looks at the transition between flap tissue and adjacent papilla — doesn't like how they'll meet. Excises some interproximal gingival tissue (gingivectomy) to make the transition seamless.

Essay-Critical Concept: In his words: "I'm doing a distal wedge, I'm doing an apically positioned flap, and I'm doing a gingivectomy — all different techniques during the same procedure."

Crown lengthening isn't ONE technique. It's whatever COMBINATION of techniques you need to achieve the goal. Write this on the essay and you'll stand out from everyone who just describes "the crown lengthening procedure" as one thing.

Step 6: Osseous Reduction

The bone comes off. Goal: get the prep margin a specific distance from the osseous crest (3-4mm). Uses diamond burs, other bur types, and hand instruments.

Step 7: The Widow's Peak — DON'T SKIP THIS

During bone removal, he spots a little peak of bone sitting in the concavity of the premolar — the widow's peak from the Osseous Surgery slides.

  • "Bony spines called 'widow's peaks' are removed from the transitional line angles by hand instruments such as Ochsenbein chisels."
  • Why MUST you remove it? Tissue follows bone. If you leave the peak, soft tissue heals following bone contour — flat along radicular bone, then BOUNCES UP coronally at the interproximal where the peak is, creating pocketing.
  • "Gingival tissues do not conform to abrupt architectural changes in bone produced by disease or resulting from surgical procedures."
  • In this case he had an edentulous area behind the tooth so he could SEE the peak. Imagine how much harder it is with another tooth right next to it.

Step 8: WHY the Tooth MUST Be Prepped and Temped BEFORE Crown Lengthening

Dentists constantly ask: "Why can't I just send the patient before I prep?"

ReasonExplanation
Visualization & accessWith temp off, Fleisher sees 360 degrees around the tooth. Without a prep, he's blind to where margins will end up.
Precision of bone removalFleisher bases bone removal on where YOUR prep margin ends. Without a prep, he's totally guessing. Too much = unnecessary attachment loss. Too little = biologic width violation.

Yes, the restorative dentist has to re-prep after healing. That's extra work. But imprecise bone removal is worse.

Step 9: Suturing — The Apical Direction Trick

This maps to the "Suturing / Periosteum" slide. Here's exactly what he does:

  1. Suture enters through keratinized tissue just coronal to the MGJ
  2. Passes through the periosteum still attached to bone (he left it there during split-thickness elevation — THIS is why)
  3. Comes out, rotates around, goes through buccal papilla area, then wraps to lingual
Critical Detail — Knot Placement: He ties the knot where he initially penetrated — on the buccal, in the more apical position — and pushes DOWN in an apical direction when tying.

Why? If you tie from above, you pull tissue CORONALLY — covering back up the tooth you just exposed. Defeats the entire purpose. By tying apically and pushing down, flap stays apically positioned.

This is the vertical mattress suture concept: "Needle penetrates through the flap and through periosteum still attached to bone. Knot is tied apically, allowing flap to be secured in an apical position."

Step 10: Post-Op Assessment — What You See Is NOT What You Get

  • Temps back in, gingival margin visible relative to prep margin
  • But this is NOT where tissue will end up. Expect approximately 2mm of coronal migration during healing

Biologic Width — The Foundation

Gargiulo et al., 1961 J Periodont 42:8

Picture a healthy tooth in cross section. From the crest of bone going coronally, three layers stacked:

Layer 1 (at bone)

~0.69mm

Biologic Width = CT Attachment + JE = ~2mm
This is the distance from bone crest to base of sulcus. The sulcus depth sits ON TOP of the biologic width.

The 3-4mm Rule

The math: biologic width (~2mm) + sulcus (~0.5-1mm) + safety margin = 3-4mm between the prep margin and the crest of bone.

Your final slide confirms: "How much distance from the restorative margin to the crest of bone? ±4mm"

Fleisher's Warning: "If we place the restorative margin too close to the osseous crest and we invade biologic width, then we will have a chronic inflammatory response with unpredictable, irregular loss of bone."

The body WILL re-establish its biologic width. Either you give it 3-4mm surgically (controlled), or it takes it from you with bone loss (disaster).

Critical Caveats

  • The Gargiulo numbers are AVERAGES with HUGE variability — some teeth had <0.5mm, others had >3mm
  • The actual biologic width on the specific tooth you're working on could be more or less than 2mm
  • You can't use other teeth in the same mouth to predict it
  • You're working with averages and hoping for the best
  • Connects to the Diagnosis lecture: "You cannot accurately, reliably, or predictably assess attachment loss from a radiograph. Or an invasion of biologic width, for that matter."
Mnemonic: "1-1-1 = 3, but give it 4 to be free."
1mm CT + 1mm JE + 1mm sulcus = 3mm minimum. But Fleisher wants 3-4mm for safety.

Or: "The body demands its 2mm of biologic width and it WILL take it — either you give it surgically, or it takes it from you with bone loss."

Indications for Crown Lengthening

From the Slides

  1. Tooth decay at or below the gingival margin
  2. Tooth fracture below the gingival margin — with adequate remaining periodontal support and attachment
  3. Teeth with excessive wear
  4. Teeth with insufficient interocclusal space
  5. Delayed passive eruption

Fleisher confirms the first two are the primary ones: "usually some sort of decay or tooth fracture that creates a margin that's going to be too close to the osseous crest."

Nuance on #2: Fracture below the margin with adequate remaining support. If the tooth has advanced bone loss AND a subgingival fracture, crown lengthening might leave you with nothing to restore. That's a contraindication.

Nuance on #5: Delayed passive eruption connects directly to Pani's Coslet 1977 classification from Lecture 7. That's where you decide gingivectomy alone (Type 1A) vs gingivectomy + osseous (Type 1B). The Coslet classification IS the answer framework for Essay Question 3.

Wednesday Case Resolution — Extraction + Socket Preservation

The Decision

  • Crown-to-root ratio won't be favorable after bone removal
  • Defect extends several mm subgingivally with irregular margins
  • Anterior teeth: root gets NARROWER as you go apical — even if you save it, the cervical area will be much narrower than adjacent tooth. Aesthetically unacceptable.
  • Conclusion: cost-risk-benefit doesn't work out. The tooth is essentially hopeless.

Patient Communication Walkthrough

You can't just say "we're pulling it." You explain the uncertainty:

  • "We think there's a good chance it needs to come out, but we won't know for sure until we open it up."
  • "We want to have a flipper ready in case we extract, but there's a possibility we might save it and then you've paid for a flipper you didn't need."
  • Alternative: two separate procedures (explore first, close up, make flipper, come back). More visits, more cost, more surgical trauma. Neither option is perfect.
  • The patient needs to understand options and financial implications BEFORE surgery, not after.

CBCT Callback

If he's raising a flap anyway (whether to save or extract), he'll see EVERYTHING directly. CBCT would give the same info he'll get intraoperatively. For THIS case, not necessary.

If planning an immediate implant, different story — but he's NOT doing immediate implant here because of too much facial bone loss. He'd have massive exposed implant on a maxillary central. "I won't sleep for months worrying about whether I grew bone in that area." He'd rather graft first, grow bone, come back later and place the implant into solid bone.

The Extraction + Socket Preservation — Same GBR Principles

When he opened the flap, the defect was massive. No way to restore. He extracted, then immediately:

  1. Bone graft in the socket
  2. Membrane over it
  3. Coronally positioned flap with two vertical incisions for primary closure
  4. Sutured onto the palate

MGJ got pulled into a V-shape from coronal advancement — he'll relax it back later. If frenum is a problem, he'll deal with it then.

Fleisher's Sermon: "If you're not prepared to do all of this at the time of extraction — the graft, the coronal positioning, the membrane, the suturing for primary closure — then you shouldn't be the person taking the tooth out."

Don't rationalize "we can go back later for ridge augmentation." Going back later means tissue has already collapsed, making reconstruction much harder. IDENTICAL to his GBR lecture on socket preservation.

Orthodontic Super-Eruption — The Alternative

The Concept

Instead of removing bone to expose more tooth, PULL THE TOOTH OUT of the bone (coronally, with orthodontic force). The tooth erupts further, bringing more clinical crown above the gingival margin.

The Critical Nuance: Does Bone Come With It?

The Radiograph Bomb — "This Will Be on Your Exam"

Fleisher's Rant — Against Standard Clinic Practice

Clinic faculty take an X-ray, measure prep margin to bone crest, and if it's <2mm, say "crown lengthening needed." Fleisher says this is WRONG.

The Scenario

X-ray shows prep margin is <2mm from bone. But when you take the temp off, the tissue looks PERFECT. Pink, no bleeding, no inflammation. Beautiful Michelangelo sulcus.

Fleisher asks: "Why do I need to do a crown lengthening?"

The Logic

  • The tissue is telling you biologic width has NOT been violated
  • The 2mm from Gargiulo is an AVERAGE
  • This particular tooth might only need 1mm of biologic width
  • If the body is happy — no inflammation, no bleeding — then biologic width for THIS tooth has been respected, regardless of what the radiograph shows

The Flip Side

If the prep is literally AT the osseous crest — bone and margin at the same level — then yes, you know for certain you need CL. That's beyond any variability. But short of that extreme, the CLINICAL picture is your answer, not the radiograph.

Mnemonic: "The tissue tells the truth, the X-ray tells a number."
Inflamed after eliminating all variables → biologic width invasion → CL needed.
Healthy tissue → biologic width is fine for THIS tooth, regardless of X-ray measurement.
Same principle across the entire course: You can't determine attachment status from radiographs alone (Diagnosis lecture). You can't determine biologic width invasion from radiographs alone (this lecture). You can't see buccal dehiscences on a PA (Pani's lecture). The radiograph is a tool, not the answer. Clinical assessment is king.

Temp Quality & Essay Answer Framework

The Temp Quality Rant

Students spend 98% of session time prepping and 30 seconds making the temp. The temps Fleisher sees in clinic are terrible.

A bad temp with overextended margins or poor fit creates EXACTLY the inflammation that gets misdiagnosed as biologic width violation, leading to unnecessary crown lengthening.

Spend as much time making a beautiful temp as you spend making the prep. A good temp protects the tissue, maintains biologic width, and gives you accurate diagnostic information at re-evaluation. A bad temp poisons every subsequent clinical decision.

Complete Essay Q2 Answer Framework

Q: "How do you determine if crown lengthening is necessary, or another problem exists?"

Other Problems Causing Bleeding/Ulceration

  1. Excess subgingival cement
  2. Overextended or ill-fitting temp margins
  3. Poor patient hygiene
  4. Rough/irregular temp surface causing plaque retention

How to Distinguish

Process of elimination. Remove all cement. Evaluate and remake temp with proper margins. Reinforce hygiene. Have patient return. Remove temp, reassess. If ALL variables eliminated and inflammation persists → biologic width violation confirmed.

The Young Patient Molar Case — Double Parabolization

The Setup

19-20 year old, massive distal decay on molars, no insurance, no money, recommended for extraction of both molars. Fleisher refused to eliminate posterior occlusion on an entire side for a 20-year-old.

The Furcation Problem & Solution

If you drop bone to expose the distal decay, won't you expose the furcation?

Fleisher's Solution — Double Parabolization: Treat it as two premolars. Don't touch the bone at the furcation. Drop the bone on the DISTAL only, then bring it back UP at the furcation.

From the Osseous Surgery slides: "The development of a parabolic curve over each root toward the furcation, with a vertical groove in the interdental bone extending apically from the furca."

Furcation stays intact and covered, distal decay is now supragingival and accessible, kid keeps his molars. Not ideal, but infinitely better than bilateral extraction at age 20.

Important: The mesial didn't need CL — decay was only on the distal. Don't automatically do both sides. Treat what needs treating and leave alone what doesn't.

Deep Margin Elevation — Know It, Be Skeptical

The Concept

Instead of extending your crown prep margin past an existing subgingival restoration (traditional approach), you LEAVE the existing restoration in place and finish your crown prep ON the restorative material. Then use composite to build up the deep margin to a more coronal, accessible position. Final crown seats on this elevated margin rather than going all the way subgingival.

Fleisher's Take

"On some levels it makes sense, but on a lot of levels it doesn't."

  • Shows a dental student's own mouth where DME was done — crown prep ends on restorative material, BUT it's not closed (there's a gap) AND it's overhanging
  • Because the margin is so far subgingival, you can't get a good finish on it
Key Point: "Crown lengthening is not just for crowns. Crown lengthening is for any restorative material that impinges on biologic width." If your DME composite is itself invading biologic width, you've traded one problem for another. You still need CL — you've just delayed it and added a layer of restorative material.

The Narrow Scenario Where DME Works

The existing restoration is JUST far enough from bone that it doesn't violate biologic width, but extending a crown prep apical to it WOULD violate it. Stopping your prep on the restorative material saves the patient from CL. But Fleisher says "most situations are not like that — they're more clear cut" and biologic width is already violated.

The Principles Checklist — Before You Cut

  1. Medical History — contraindications: uncontrolled hypertension, uncontrolled diabetes
  2. Develop a full treatment plan before surgery — charting and radiographic assessment before touching the patient
  3. Understand the anatomy of the surgical area — bone levels, furcation involvement, calculus location
  4. Provide profound anesthesia
  5. Follow aseptic surgical technique — "use of a pre-surgical aseptic rinse has been shown to be beneficial in reducing transient bacteria"
  6. Atraumatic tissue management — handle tissue gently, don't crush or tear flaps
  7. Attain hemostasis — control bleeding before closing
  8. Atraumatic suturing — suture without further tissue damage
  9. Obliterate dead space between flap and bone — flap AGAINST bone with no gap (dead space = blood clot = infection = failure)
  10. Promote stable wound healing — everything above serves this goal
03

The Surgical Video — Pre-Op & Incision

Step 1: Pre-Operative Assessment

  • Deep probing depths on molars/premolars (5-6mm interproximally), shallower buccally (~3mm)
  • Radiographs: horizontal bone loss, furcation involvement, interproximal bone loss
  • Ideal osseous levels should be 2-3mm from adjacent CEJs
  • INTERPROXIMAL pockets are deepest — that's where craters and bone defects form
  • Buccal bone relatively intact — the problem is between the teeth
Deep interproximal pockets make root instrumentation "much more difficult, if not impossible." This is Level 2 → Level 3 escalation — SRP can't adequately clean these depths (Caffesse 1986: 65% inadequate debridement at ≥5mm), so you need to open it up.

Step 2: The Primary Incision — Scalloped Internal Bevel

  • Scalloped incision — follows the natural parabolic contour of underlying root form. If you make a straight incision, tissue won't conform to curved bone → pocketing or irregular healing.
  • Internal bevel incision (also: primary incision, reverse bevel) — designed to:
    • Reduce diseased soft tissue (excising inner pocket wall)
    • Allow flap to be repositioned apically
    • Create soft tissue contours ideal for post-op maintenance
  • Wider scallop on the distal (tuberosity area) — accounts for thicker tissue there
  • Internal bevel thins tissue and removes diseased inner pocket lining in one move. Compare to external bevel gingivectomy (cuts from outside, like slicing bread) — internal bevel preserves the outer surface for a better flap.

Debridement & Bone Defect Identification

Step 4: Remove Granulation Tissue

All angry, inflamed granulation tissue lining the defect comes out. "Once this granulation tissue is removed, inspection of osseous contours is performed." Can't assess bone architecture until you've cleaned out the inflammatory mess. Same step in EVERY procedure — CL, GTR, osseous surgery.

Step 5: Identify the Bone Defects

With clean bone exposed, the narrator identifies three things:

  1. Intraosseous (infrabony) defect — bone loss extending below the crest, between roots
  2. Ledges of bone on the buccal — thick shelves preventing flap adaptation
  3. Furcation involvement — bone loss into the furcation area

Step 6: Root Debridement Under Direct Vision

  • Visible calculus (dark areas on roots) — end-cutting bur, then chisels, hand instruments, ultrasonics
  • This is the same thing as SRP but under DIRECT VISION
  • Storm drain analogy: SRP was fishing blindly. Surgery popped the grate off. Now you can SEE.
  • "When root surfaces are smooth, the clinician can be confident that the roots are fully debrided and biologically healthy for stable periodontal tissue attachment."

Osteoplasty vs Ostectomy

Why Remove Buccal Ledges?

Tissue follows bone. If thick ledges of bone remain on the buccal, the flap can't adapt at a more apical position — held out by the ledge, creating dead space and pocketing. Thinning the ledge (osteoplasty) lets the flap lay flat.

Osteoplasty Indications (From Slides)

  • Pocket elimination
  • Tori reduction
  • Reduction of thick heavy bony ledges/exostoses
  • Shallow osseous craters
  • Blunted interdental craters
  • Small intrabony defects on buccal/lingual
  • Enhanced flap placement with improved alveolar contours

Water irrigation always during bone removal — bone is living tissue. Overheat with bur = necrosis. Dead bone won't support healing.

Full vs Split Thickness — The Decision Tree

Fleisher's Detailed Breakdown

Full Thickness (Mucoperiosteal)Split Thickness (Partial)
IncisionGoes right down to crest of boneFirst incision removes tissue (internal gingivectomy), then redirect blade to SPLIT — peel outer layers but leave periosteum
What stays on boneNothing — bone completely exposedPeriosteum — stays as a "carpet" on bone
TechniqueLess technique sensitive, fasterMore sensitive, thinner flap, easy to perforate
Use whenNeed to SEE and MODIFY boneNeed to REPOSITION the flap (apically or coronally)
Why split thickness allows repositioning: The flap is freed from the bone (periosteum stays behind as a "carpet"). The flap can now SLIDE in any direction. With full thickness, the flap includes the periosteum — it's all one unit stuck together, nothing to slide over.

The Combination Approach

You can start with full thickness (to see the bone crest) and then transition to split thickness beyond that (to gain mobility). This is EXACTLY the "split-full-split" concept from Pani's CAF technique: split at papilla, full at crestal bone, split apically for mobility. Same principle, different application.

Release at the MGJ

Split thickness dissection should go down to the MGJ and beyond. Why? Tissue below the MGJ is alveolar mucosa — elastic and stretchy. Releasing into this zone gives much more freedom of movement. Stop above the MGJ and the flap is still tethered by inelastic keratinized tissue.

Flap Design Principles & The Exposed Tooth Case

Vertical Release Incisions

From slides: "With vertical incisions, always the base is wider than the gingival margin."

Why: BLOOD SUPPLY. The base is where blood vessels enter. Narrower base = restricted blood flow = flap necrosis. Wider base ensures adequate vascular supply. Extend to the MGJ and beyond for elastic mucosal tissue mobility.

Plenty of KTYesInternal bevel incision (submarginal) + full thickness flap + osseous + APF. Can sacrifice some KT.

The Exposed Tooth Case — Thinking Ahead

Tooth needs surgical exposure for orthodontic bracketing. Easy approach: window in the tissue, expose crown, bond bracket, pull into position.

But he looks at the KT: not much. Mostly alveolar mucosa. If he just windows it, tooth erupts into a zone of mucosal tissue with no KT at the gingival margin → patient needs a gingival graft later.

Instead: Raise a flap, take the KT sitting apically, APICALLY POSITION it so as the tooth erupts and tissue moves coronally with it, there will be a band of KT at the CEJ level. Simple design, thinking ahead, saves the patient a future graft.

"It's just simple design, looking at it, identifying it, and taking the time to do the right thing for the patient."

SRP Reevaluation Timing

The Biologic Rationale

  • Reevaluation window: 2-8 weeks after completion of ALL quadrants of SRP
  • If huge gap between first and last quad (first done 4 months ago, last 3 weeks ago) — can't just evaluate based on last quad
  • Why 3 months is the hard deadline: bacteria have repopulated and inflammation is increasing again
  • Always evaluate at the tissue's BEST, not its WORST. If patient missed the window → recall cleaning FIRST → then evaluate a few weeks later

Connects to the SPT lecture — 3-month recalls disrupt bacterial repopulation. Same biological clock.

"Don't Copy-Paste Notes": When you reevaluate, do a FRESH assessment. Don't copy clinical findings from a previous date. The diagnosis may have changed. Copying an old diagnosis that's now wrong = falsifying a record.

Suturing — Single Interrupted & Continuous

Armamentarium

  • Castroviejo needle holder — from ophthalmic surgery, precision instrument with locking mechanism. Common among periodontists, uncommon among oral surgeons.
  • Scissors, tissue pickups (1×2), tissue pliers

Single Interrupted Suture

  • Enter from OUTER aspect of one side → pierce through → come through other side → exit → tie a knot
  • Surgeon's Knot: 2 throws in one direction (first throw is doubled = surgeon's knot, creates friction to hold position) + 1 throw in opposite direction (locks it). Total: 2 + 1 = 3 throws minimum.
  • Advantage: If one comes loose, only that spot is affected. Rest still hold.
  • Fleisher notes: "not necessarily appropriate for a lot of perio procedures" — perio often requires more sophisticated tissue positioning than just pulling two edges together

Continuous Suture

Fleisher: "I don't like them at all. I never use them."

Why? "If it comes loose, the whole thing comes loose." One failure point takes down the entire suture line → flap is open → disaster. With 4-5 individual interrupted sutures, if one comes loose, the other 4 still hold.

Faster to place but the all-or-nothing risk makes Fleisher avoid it entirely.

Vertical Mattress — THE Perio Suture

Fleisher: "Probably the primary suture technique that I'll use during surgery, particularly if I am apically positioning a flap."

Step-by-Step Technique

  1. Enter through buccal flap — pierce through KT just ABOVE the mucogingival junction
  2. Roll the needle — start perpendicular to bone, but as you get deeper ROTATE the needle to become PARALLEL with bone surface
  3. Grab the periosteum — as you become parallel, needle catches periosteum still attached to bone. This is WHY you did a split thickness flap.
  4. Come all the way out through the periosteum
  5. Come back through to the buccal — re-enter tissue
  6. Go around to the lingual — grab lingual tissue
  7. Come back to buccal side
  8. Tie the knot WHERE YOU FIRST ENTERED — this is at the most apical position, near the MGJ. Tying HERE and pulling DOWN secures tissue in an apical position.

Why Tying Location Matters

If you lift up and tie at the crest → pull all tissue CORONALLY → defeats the purpose of apical positioning. By tying at the initial entry point (which is apical) → pulling tissue DOWN toward that point → flap stays where you want it.

The Needle Rolling Technique — Clinical Gold

NOT on any slide but essential for performing this suture. The curved needle (half circle or 5/8 circle) is used strategically:

  • Start perpendicular to bone surface (pointing straight down through flap)
  • As you get close to bone, ROLL the needle → curves and travels PARALLEL to bone surface
  • Catches periosteum WITHOUT hitting bone (needle tip becomes blunt on bone contact, and you don't need to go through bone — just need periosteum for anchorage)

Centering principle: Suture should be centered underneath the peak of tissue for even tension and proper flap adaptation.

Periodontal Dressing & Post-Op Protocol

Does Perio Dressing Help?

Evidence: No study shows it makes healing better OR worse statistically.

Why Fleisher uses it anyway — THREE practical reasons:

  1. Patient protection FROM THEMSELVES. "I don't want the patient looking at it, playing with it, brushing it."
  2. No home care needed in that area during week 1. Simplifies post-op.
  3. Patient comfort. Slight compression, reduces sensation of exposed tissue.

Practical Tips

  • How to keep it in place: work packing INTO the interproximal spaces so it locks between teeth. Like snapping puzzle pieces.
  • Hardest to keep: the palate after FGG harvest — no interproximals to lock into. May need a stent.
  • The Avitene Trick: Before placing the pack, put Avitene (collagen microfibrillar clotting agent) over the graft/surgical site. Creates a barrier so the pack doesn't stick to the tissue or lock onto sutures. If pack bonds to a suture and you pull it off → you're pulling the suture → tearing the flap.
  • If pack falls off: Tell patients not to worry. Protective, not essential for healing.

Follow-Up: 1 Week to 10 Days (EVERY Patient)

  1. Suture removal (if non-resorbable) or assessment (if resorbable)
  2. Pack removal (if placed)
  3. Evaluate healing — is tissue adapting? Complications?
  4. Assess home care — ready for gentle hygiene in surgical area?

Even with resorbable sutures, you want to SEE the patient — you're evaluating the entire healing response.

Post-Op Instructions & Record Entry

What You Tell (and WRITE DOWN for) the Patient

  1. Take it easy — rest for remainder of day
  2. Pain medication — take BEFORE anesthesia wears off
  3. Antibiotics if prescribed — complete full course
  4. Control swelling — CRITICAL — NSAID + ICE. Fleisher sends patients home WITH an ice pack. On and off for surgery day AND next day.
  5. Bleeding possibility — explain normal oozing vs what requires a call

  8. Emergency contact — patient MUST have a way to reach you. Fleisher gives his personal number. "You don't leave patients high and dry."

Definitive vs Initial Therapy — Lindhe’s 4.2mm

SRP: Same Procedure, Two Classifications

SRP can be either initial therapy or definitive therapy — depends on the outcome at reevaluation:

Re-eval FindingSRP Was…Next Step
Pockets resolved, no further tx neededDefinitive therapySPT maintenance
Surgery determined to be neededInitial therapySurgical phase

Same procedure → different classification based purely on outcome.

Lindhe’s Critical Probing Depth: 4.2mm

In sites with initial probing depths above 4.2mm, more clinical attachment gain occurred following a Widman flap surgery than following SRP alone.

4.2mm = Lindhe’s cutoff for when surgery becomes superior to SRP.

Fleisher’s Clinical Threshold
“I think that’s a little on the low side.”
He won’t go for surgery if all probing depths are 4mm. He wants to see at least 5mm WITH bleeding upon probing before recommending surgery.

For the exam: Know Lindhe’s number (4.2mm). Know Fleisher’s clinical threshold (5mm + BOP).

The CEJ Discrepancy — Don’t Be Fooled

The Radiographic Interpretation Trap

When adjacent teeth have CEJs at different levels (tipping, super-eruption, natural variation), the bone between them appears ANGLED on the radiograph. Looks like angular bone loss — but might just be normal bone following different CEJ positions.

How to Tell the Difference

Measure CEJ-to-crest distance on EACH tooth:

FindingDiagnosis
Distance within normal limits (1-2mm) on BOTH sidesCEJ discrepancy — bone healthy, just following anatomy
Distance increased on one or both sidesActual bone loss — true pathology
Common Scenario
Tipped second molar after first molar extraction. Tipping creates huge CEJ discrepancy with adjacent premolar. Radiograph shows what looks like severe angular bone loss, but bone-to-CEJ distance may be normal. Don’t automatically diagnose periodontitis based on radiographic angle alone.

Fleisher’s Clinical Commentary — Flap Nuances

Perforation Risk

The biggest danger with split thickness = perforating the flap. If blade angle is wrong — even slightly — you go through the thin tissue, especially near the MGJ. Periosteal elevator can perforate too. This is why split thickness is “technique sensitive” and “takes longer.”

Suturing Options with Remaining Periosteum

Because you left periosteum on bone, you have multiple strategies:

  • Suture flap directly TO the periosteum (vertical mattress technique)
  • Suture to connective tissue that wasn’t elevated
  • Both keep flap at a more apical level

The Combination Approach for Tissue Reduction

Sometimes combine submarginal incision (removing some KT) WITH apical positioning:

  • Submarginal incision reduces tissue height from above
  • Apical positioning moves remaining complex downward
  • Result: don’t have to move flap as far apically — already reduced some tissue coronally

BUT: Only if you have EXCESS keratinized tissue to sacrifice. If KT is limited → crevicular incision + move entire complex apically without sacrificing anything.

The Setup

Mandibular molar with a shallow 2-wall interdental crater (buccal plate + lingual plate intact, bone lost interproximally).

Buccal2-3mmBone is fine here

The Treatment

Flatten bone to depth of crater. Take some supporting bone off the buccal (ostectomy) to create positive architecture — radicular bone apical to interdental bone. Then APF. Result: minimal probing depths everywhere, patient CAN clean it.

With ONE wall → he probably wouldn’t try.
At the time this case was done, GTR barely existed as an option.

The Palate Limitation — No APF on Palate

Why You Can’t Apically Position on the Palate

What makes apical positioning possible? Releasing tissue at the MGJ, where elastic alveolar mucosa allows free flap movement.

But the palate has NO mucogingival junction. It’s ALL keratinized tissue from gingival margin to midline. No elastic mucosal tissue to release into. You physically cannot gain the mobility needed for apical repositioning.

The Workaround

Achieve the EFFECT of apical positioning by doing a submarginal (paramarginal) incision — excising some excess palatal tissue.

You CAN do this because the palate has ABUNDANT keratinized tissue (the opposite of the buccal concern).

Palatal approach: submarginal incision to thin and reduce tissue — NOT a mobile flap that gets repositioned.

Same principle from KT decision tree: palate = unlimited KT → submarginal always safe → but no MGJ → no apical repositioning.

2mm Sulcus Depths & The Humbling Reality of SRP

The Thesis of the Entire Surgical Curriculum

Goal: approximately 2mm sulcus depth everywhere when possible.

Why? Because ≤3mm probing depths allow:

  • Complete debridement during maintenance
  • Effective daily home care
  • Predictable brushing/flossing

Anything deeper = ticking time bomb — patient can’t clean, hygienist barely reaches, bacteria thrive in deep anaerobic environment.

The Exception: Anterior Maxillary Teeth

Almost always compromise here — aesthetic consequence of APF would be devastating. Long exposed roots in smile zone = unacceptable unless teeth going under full prosthetic coverage.

The Humbling Reality of SRP
Fleisher on reflecting a flap after SRP: “You see how much calculus you have left behind. No matter how experienced you are, you will leave much more behind than you ever imagined.”

This is Caffesse 1986 come to life — 65% inadequate debridement at ≥5mm — and it’s why surgery exists.

Same storm drain analogy from Day 1: SRP is fishing blind. Surgery pops the grate off. The sequential treatment approach: SRP first (get what you can blind) → surgery for what SRP couldn’t reach.

“Is This Tooth Restorable?” & The Denture Warning

Margins on Cementum

Student objects: margins are on root, not crown. Fleisher: “It doesn’t have to be on the crown. There’s lots of situations where we end up on cementum.”

Ideally finish above CEJ, but many clinical situations require finishing apical to CEJ on root surface. As long as you have adequate biologic width (3-4mm from margin to bone) and patient can maintain the area → margins on cementum can work.

Connects to Diagnosis lecture: the question isn’t “is the margin on enamel?” — it’s “can this tooth function long-term with this restoration in this patient?”

The Denture Warning — Think Ahead

When a patient gets a removable denture, it sits ON the ridge. Pressure from denture causes continuous bone RESORPTION — “like a guarantee.” Ridge gets flatter and flatter. Eventually so little bone left that you can’t place implants even if patient later wants them.

Placing implants early (even under removable prosthesis) PREVENTS the bone loss that makes future treatment impossible. Implant maintains bone. Without it, bone disappears.

Same principle from GBR lecture on socket preservation: best time to preserve bone = at time of extraction, not years later after resorption. Whether socket graft or implant placement — goal is maintaining bone volume before it’s lost.

Too many dentists say “we’ll just make a denture” without thinking about the ridge in 10 years. Think ahead. Plan for the future. Give patients options down the road even if they can’t afford ideal treatment today.

Laser Physics – The Three Key Concepts

Laser functioning is based on three concepts: Absorption, Spontaneous Emission, and Stimulated Emission.

1. Absorption

Basic unit of matter = Atom (nucleus with protons + neutrons, plus electrons).

A photon strikes an atom → the electron jumps from ground state (E1) to excited state (E2).

2. Spontaneous Emission

Electron naturally falls from E2 → E1, releasing a photon.

Occurs naturally, no control over it.

Produces ordinary incoherent light — photons do NOT flow in the exact same direction.

3. Stimulated Emission

An incident photon hits an already excited electron → releases 2 photons (1 from incident photon + 1 from the energy release).

Faster than spontaneous emission.

The emitted photons have same energy, same frequency, are coherent, and travel in the same direction.

Why It Matters
Stimulated emission is the core mechanism behind laser light — it's how a single photon gets amplified into a powerful, directional beam of identical photons.
04

Fundamentals of Laser Light & Radiation

Wave Properties of Photons

Photon waves travel at the speed of light and are defined by two basic properties:

Amplitude

Vertical height of the wave from the zero axis to its peak. Relates to intensity/energy.

Wavelength

Horizontal distance between any two corresponding points on the wave. Measured in nanometers (nm).

Frequency

Number of wave oscillations per second.

Velocity

Frequency × Wavelength = Speed of light

High-Yield Relationship
Frequency is inversely proportional to wavelength — the shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency.

Radiation & Electromagnetic Spectrum

Lasers produce light as a specific form of electromagnetic energy.

  • All dental laser devices emit wavelengths approximately 500 nm to 10,600 nm
  • They fall within the visible or invisible infrared portion of the spectrum and emit thermal radiation
Critical Rule – Wavelength vs. Penetration
Shorter wavelength = MORE penetration into tissue and bone
  Example: Nd:YAG at 1064 nm

Longer wavelength = LESS penetration (only 2–4 cell layers at surface)
  Example: Er:YAG at 2940 nm

Laser–Tissue Interactions

When a laser beam contacts tissue, four things can happen:

Absorption ✓

The most desired interaction.

  • Specific tissue molecules absorb photons: chromophores, pigments (hemoglobin/melanin), water content
  • Light energy → thermal (heat) energy → performs work
  • Used for excision and coagulation
Short wavelength (500–1000 nm) → absorbed by pigmented tissue & blood → Argon, Diode, Nd:YAG

Long wavelengths → interact with water & hydroxyapatite → Er:YAG, CO₂

Reflection ✗

Beam bounces off surface with no interaction.

Undesired & dangerous — beam can hit unintentional targets, especially eyes (major safety concern).

Example: Erbium laser reflecting off titanium implant.

Transmission

Beam passes through tissue without affecting the target, allowing interaction with deeper areas.

Effect depends on wavelength.

Examples: Nd:YAG and Diode lasers

Scattering

Laser beam diverts into low-energy radiation in various directions.

Produces unwanted heat transfers to adjacent tissues.

However, scattering facilitates composite resin curing.

Effects of Laser on Tissues

These are the biological effects caused when laser beam photons enter and are absorbed by tissues:

💡 Photochemical (Biostimulation / Fluorescence)

Light interacts with chromophores and triggers biological cascades:

  • Mitochondrial stimulation → increased ATP production
  • Increased cell metabolism → faster/better tissue repair
  • Improved blood circulation & vasodilation
  • Analgesic effect — relieves acute/chronic pain
  • Anti-inflammatory & anti-edematous
  • Used in non-contact mode at low power
Clinical result: Increased post-op comfort, minimal edema, more rapid healing than traditional techniques.

Fluorescence: A second wavelength of light is emitted from a tissue molecule after laser exposure. Useful for diagnosing carious lesions at 655 nm visible light (Diode).

🔥 Photothermal (Coagulation)

Conversion of laser beam energy into heat.

Used to perform excision and coagulation.

Effects are reversible or irreversible depending on the temperature produced.

TemperatureTissue Effect
37–60°CWarming, welding → Reversible
60–65°CCoagulation / protein denaturation
65–90°CDesiccation (drying out)

💥 Photomechanical (Photoacoustic)

Step 1
High-power laser beam interacts with water
→
Step 2

→

  • Example: Er:YAG

A two-stage treatment combining light energy with a photosensitizer:

Stage 1

Production of electrons when light is shone onto a material. Studied in electronic physics, quantum chemistry, and electrochemistry.

Applications in Periodontal Therapy

Non-Surgical Periodontal Therapy

Lasers have been suggested as adjunctive or alternative tools to conventional mechanical periodontal therapy.

Removal of Subgingival Calculus

  • Laser not only removes calculus — it also alters cementum to make it favorable for fibroblast attachment
  • CO₂ CANNOT be used for calculus removal (damages soft tissue)
  • Nd:YAG is INEFFECTIVE for calculus removal
  • Er:YAG is CAPABLE of removing subgingival calculus without major thermal change to root surface (Schwarz 2001)

Bacterial Reduction

  • Simple, nonsurgical procedure to eliminate/reduce viable bacteria subgingivally
  • Only two soft tissue wavelengths that meet criteria for effective delivery into periodontal pockets: Nd:YAG and Diode

  • Emits short-wavelength red/infrared light with low water absorption, reaching depth of 3 mm to 15 mm

LANAP Protocol (Nd:YAG)

Background
Conventional mechanical tools are not effective for complete curettage of soft tissue. The Nd:YAG laser in a Laser Assisted New Attachment Procedure (LANAP) removes diseased soft tissue on the inner gingival surface of the periodontal pocket and underlying connective tissue.

What LANAP Does

  • Destroys quantity and quality of bacteria
  • De-epithelializes pocket → prevents epithelial downgrowth and its attachment to root surface
  • Penetrates into gingiva (minimizes damage to healthy tissue) and cementum → kills bacteria & removes endotoxin
  • Removes calculus and smear layer for root detoxification
  • Stimulates regeneration (cementum, PDL, bone) for wound healing

LANAP: Nd:YAG Superiority Data

  • Kills P. gingivalis 16× greater with Nd:YAG vs. Diode to complete depth of 2 mm from laser tip — Diode has surface effect only
  • Complete removal of epithelium
  • Decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines: IL-1β, MMP-8, LPS
  • Increased anti-inflammatory proteins: IL-20, IL-8
Surgical Pocket Therapy with Lasers

For optimal regeneration, root surface and bone defect must be completely debrided and decontaminated.

Lasers used: CO₂ and Erbium family

  • Calculus removal
  • Osseous surgery
  • Detoxification of root surface and bone
  • Removal of granulation tissue
Advantages
Better access in furcation areas, hemostasis, less post-operative discomfort, and faster healing.

Osseous Surgery & Lasers

  • Erbium lasers are becoming popular for bone surgery
  • More precision, better access
  • Reduces risk of collateral damage
  • Reduces noise, eliminates vibration of mechanical cutting → increases surgical precision
  • Er:YAG is currently safe and useful for periodontal bone surgery (osseous removal or recontouring) when used with saline irrigation

Post-Therapy Reevaluation

⚠ Important Protocol
  • Do NOT re-probe before 3 months — healing in sulcus begins at the bottom of the pocket and can be damaged by the probe
  • Probe with light touch at 3 months
  • Reevaluate every 3 months for 1 year
  • At 1 year → decide on surgical intervention, bone grafts, or other surgical therapy

Wound Healing

Types of Wound Healing

Primary Intention

Clean edges brought together (e.g., sutured incision)

Secondary Intention

Wound left open to heal from the bottom up

Tertiary Intention

Delayed primary closure

Two Distinct Healing Processes

Regeneration

Natural renewal of structure by growth and differentiation of new cells and intercellular substances that function the same as original.

Growth from the same type of tissue destroyed or its precursors.

However, periodontal tissues are limited in regenerative capacity.

Manifested by:

  • Mitotic activity in epithelium of gingiva & CT of PDL
  • Bone remodeling
  • Continuous deposition of cementum

Repair — "Healing by Scar"

Replacement of one tissue with another (e.g., fibrous connective tissue) that may NOT function the same as original.

Two processes in repair:

  • Granulation tissue formation
  • Contraction of wounds

* Both processes can take place simultaneously

Three Phases of Granulation Tissue Formation

PhaseTimingWhat Happens
1. Inflammation 0–24 hrs Blood clots at site of injury. Acute inflammatory response with exudation of plasma, neutrophils, and some monocytes.
2. Clearance 1–2 to 3 days Proteolytic enzymes (from neutrophils) + autolytic enzymes (from dead cells) + phagocytic activity of macrophages clear necrotic tissue, debris, and RBCs.
3. Ingrowth of Granulation Tissue 2–3 days to 2 weeks Two processes: (a) Angiogenesis and (b) Formation of fibrous tissue

Contraction of Wounds

  • Starts after 2–3 days, completed by day 14
  • Wound reduced by approximately 80% of original size
  • Contracted wound → rapid healing since less surface area needs replacement

Problem with Conventional Instruments
Steel curettes and ultrasonics are not completely suitable — they damage the titanium implant surface and interfere with bone healing.
Er:YAG = Best Choice
Er:YAG possesses the best property for both degranulation and implant surface decontamination because of its dual action on both soft and hard tissue ablation without thermal damage to adjacent tissue (Schwarz 2006).

Laser Safety & Advantages / Disadvantages

⚠ Dental Laser Safety Rules

  • Patient, assistant, and doctor must ALWAYS wear safety goggles
  • Laser warning poster must be displayed where patients can see it
  • Record that eyewear was used in the chart
  • Use wavelength-specific glasses — labeled with associated wavelength and optical density
  • Smoking plume: Potentially hazardous/infectious material emitted during tissue ablation
  • No OSHA standard on surgical plumes — use high-speed evacuation
  • Laser is a possible ignition source — care with inflammable materials (e.g., alcohol)

✓ Advantages

  • Less pain, less need for anesthetic (advantage for medically compromised patients)
  • No risk of bacteremia
  • Excellent wound healing
  • Bleeding control
  • No need for sutures
  • Ability to remove both hard and/or soft tissues
  • Can be used in combination with scalpels

✗ Disadvantages

  • Laser light interacts in both contact and non-contact modes → safety glasses required for eye protection
  • Extreme temperatures → tissue necrosis
  • Relatively high cost of devices
Bottom Line (Conclusion from Lecture)
Laser irradiation in combination with conventional mechanical treatment OR alone has potential to improve periodontal pocket condition more than mechanical therapy alone.

Based on research: Er:YAG holds promise to debride both root surface and soft tissue wall of pocket. Nd:YAG, Diode, and Argon have potential for soft tissue curettage.

Fundamentals of Periodontal Diagnosis

"...if all periodontal conditions had the same prognosis and required the same treatment, there would be no reason for diagnosis beyond simply establishing the presence of periodontal disease."

Why Diagnosis Matters

  • Periodontal diseases are the result of complex interactions between the host and periodontal microflora
  • These give rise to a variety of clinical entities
  • The goal of diagnosis: identify and classify the problem in some biologically meaningful way that will aid in selecting the most optimum therapeutic modality
Key Lecture Point
"They may all probe the same amount, but for different reasons."
Same probing depths can have completely different etiologies, prognoses, and treatment plans. This is why diagnosis must go beyond just numbers.

Two Basic Categories

Gingivitis

Color changes + Bleeding upon probing

Inflammation confined to gingiva — no attachment loss

Periodontitis

Diagnosed mechanically by measuring the historical record of disease activity that has already occurred in the form of attachment loss

Disease Activity

Definition & Limitations

"In the context of periodontology, disease activity is defined as the loss of clinical attachment (looking back in time)."

What We CAN Determine

Is this site actively breaking down?

We measure past disease activity through clinical attachment loss.

What We CANNOT Determine

Is this site likely to break down in the future?

Currently no reliable tests or criteria exist to determine if a site is about to lose attachment, or is in the active process of doing so.

Key Bridge
However, we have identified risk factors known to increase the likelihood of attachment loss in the future. This is where risk assessment becomes essential for prognosis.

Classification Evolution

1999 AAP Classification

Separated periodontal disease into distinct categories including Chronic Periodontitis and Aggressive Periodontitis as separate diseases.

Moved to a staging and grading system. Aggressive and chronic periodontitis were unified under "Periodontitis" with Stages (I–IV) for severity/complexity and Grades (A–C) for rate of progression and risk.

Prognosis — Core Concepts

Definition of Prognosis

Prognosis — from Greek prognostikos: "of knowledge beforehand" — foreseeing, predicting the outcome.

"Prognosis is an art based on a science."
— John Prichard

Prognosis Is Based on Two Things

1. Causality

Identifying the agents responsible for causing the disorder (e.g., smoking? bacteria?)

2. Risk Assessment

Weighing the relative strengths of all risk factors present to quantify the risk for developing disease

Periodontitis as a Chronic Disease

Definition
Chronic disease: "A disease which has a prolonged course, does not resolve spontaneously, and for which a complete cure is rarely achieved."
  • Periodontitis is chronic and multifactorial
  • Both the precipitating etiologic agent (bacteria) AND predisposing factors that increase risk are present
  • Although bacteria are the cause, there are numerous predisposing factors that contribute to and shape the disease process for each individual

Risk Factor vs. Causal Agent

Key Distinction
A risk factor is associated with increased risk for disease but is not necessarily causal in nature. Such factors tend to predispose someone to disease.

Since not all risk factors are established or fully known, it is impossible to completely assess risk for any individual. This impacts our ability to accurately establish a prognosis.

Deep Margin Elevation

Concept — Deep Margin Elevation (DME)

Deep Margin Elevation was referenced at the end of this lecture. Source article by Andy Janiga, 2021 (speareducation.com). Earliest published work: Dietschi & Speafico, 1998. Concept gained traction in 2012.

Definition
DME involves placing a restorative material (composite) to raise a deep subgingival margin to an equigingival or supragingival location, making it accessible for impression-taking and cementation without the need for crown lengthening surgery.
  • Also called: "open sandwich technique," "proximal box elevation," and "margin elevation technique"
  • A conservative technique for restoring teeth with subgingival margins
  • Can be useful for both direct and indirect restorations
  • Avoids the removal of bone and surgical treatment often necessary to access subgingival margins
What the Lecture Images Show (p124–125)
  • Pre-op radiograph: Tooth with deep subgingival margin visible radiographically
  • Radiograph of ceramic crown with deep margin: Shows the problematic deep margin location
  • Crown preparation utilizing DME: Clinical photo showing composite placed to elevate the margin
  • Final image: Line showing the area of deep margin elevation — the subgingival margin has been relocated supragingivally
  • Final PA radiograph (p125): Shows the completed crown restoration with the elevated margin
Clinical Relevance
DME can avoid the need for a full crown lengthening procedure in cases where the deep margin is the main issue. However, it is not a substitute when there is true biologic width violation requiring osseous recontouring. It is a restorative solution, not a periodontal one — know the difference for the exam.

SRP — What the Midterm Covered (Quick Review)

  • Scientific rationale: disrupting subgingival microflora, delaying repopulation of pathogenic microbes

  • Limitation: in pockets 5mm or greater, clinicians inadequately debride roots 65% of the time (Caffesse 1986)
  • "Attachment gains" after SRP may just be reduced inflammation and recession making the numbers look better — not true regeneration
  • Shallow pockets actually tend to LOSE attachment after SRP (mechanical trauma from instrumentation)
  • Deeper pockets show gains
  • Greenstein 1992: despite limitations, SRP remains the definitive approach for mild to moderate periodontitis and the starting point before surgery

The "Obvious" Questions That Aren't Obvious

Fleisher's Trap Questions — Students Have Gotten These Wrong on Summative Exams

QuestionAnswerWhy
"Do you probe only sites that probed >3mm last time?" NO. Probe EVERY SINGLE SITE, EVERY SINGLE TIME. Connects to Socransky — disease is episodic and asynchronous. A site that was 2mm last visit could be 6mm now. You record only depths >3, but you PROBE everything. Some students confused "only record depths >3" with "only probe sites that were >3 last time." Completely different things.
"Do you only clean supragingivally?" NO. Clean BOTH supra and sub. Fleisher is furious about this one — he's had students in summative exams who literally didn't go subgingival during a recall. Subgingival is MORE important — the bacteria at the most apical extent of the pocket in that anaerobic environment causes the most damage.
"Do you sharpen your curettes?" YES. Before you start AND during the procedure. Nobody in sterilization sharpens instruments. You get back dull curettes from the last person. Re-sharpen at least once during recall, multiple times during SRP. Kitchen knife analogy: keep it sharp and it takes 2 seconds to restore the edge. Let it go 3-4 patients and you're spending real time. Sharp = efficient = better results = less time.
"Do you instrument past the attachment?" NO. Instrument TO the base of the pocket (attachment level), not past it. Going past the attachment = traumatizing healthy connective tissue fibers = iatrogenic damage.

Recall Frequency — The Evidence (or Lack of It)

The 6-Month Recall: A Marketing Slogan, Not Science

Exam Bomb — 100% Will Be on Your Final
6-month recall for healthy patients: THERE IS NO SCIENTIFIC EVIDENCE FOR THIS.

The origin? An 1890 women's magazine (like Good Housekeeping) that recommended seeing your dentist twice a year. Then Colgate picked it up in the early 1900s as a marketing slogan. That's it. No science. It's convention.

Fleisher's not saying don't do it — it's logical, a year is too long, patients need monitoring, and it's easy to remember. But if an exam question asks "what is the evidence basis for 6-month recall in healthy patients?" the answer is there isn't one.

The 3-Month Recall: Overwhelming Evidence

Four major long-term studies from:

University of Michigan

University of Gothenburg

University of Minnesota

Loma Linda University

All showed the same thing: patients receiving routine maintenance care maintained attachment levels better than those who didn't, even when home care was mediocre.

Mnemonic
"Six is a MYTH, Three is a MUST." No evidence for 6 months (healthy). Overwhelming evidence for 3 months (perio history). Axelsson & Lindhe 1981 is the study to cite.

  • The point is NOT "send everyone to the periodontist." The point is that a closely monitored, effective recall program is what matters.   • "If a patient isn't willing to commit to 3-month recalls after active therapy, they shouldn't bother doing the active therapy in the first place." Without maintenance, surgical or non-surgical treatment results WILL relapse. It becomes a complete waste of time and money.
  • He preempts the "this study is old" objection — the reason there aren't newer studies is because this is settled science. Nobody's repeating these studies because the results were so conclusive in the 80s and early 90s that there's nothing left to prove.

Instrumentation Deep Dive

The Whiteout-on-a-Root Demonstration

One single curette stroke covers a tiny strip of the root surface. Now imagine: you're doing this blindfolded (subgingival), on 4 surfaces per tooth, on 28 teeth. That's hundreds of strokes. And offices are giving hygienists 30 minutes to do all of this PLUS medical history review, charting, radiographs, and cleanup. That's insane. Fleisher gives his hygienists 90 minutes if radiographs are needed.

Why Smaller Instruments Matter

  • Even on a relatively HEALTHY patient during a routine cleaning, a standard Gracey curette will lacerate the tissue with the slightest misangulation. When you nick tissue → bleeds like crazy → 10 minutes packing gauze
  • Smaller blades (mini, micro mini) let you work in tight areas — especially furcations — without causing damage
  • For years, the furcation was essentially uncleanable with standard instruments because the blade was too thick to fit between the roots
  • Hygienists used to save their old, repeatedly-sharpened curettes (which got thinner over time) specifically for furcation access
  • Now the micro mini is purpose-built for this, and less likely to break mid-procedure than a worn-down old curette

Explorers, Scalers & Power Scaling

After Five Explorer — Testable Distinction

After Five Explorer

For detecting CALCULUS

Pigtail Explorer

For detecting CARIES

Do NOT use them interchangeably.

Technique
Hold with a VERY light grasp, finger resting directly on the shank as close to the tip as practical. You're trying to feel vibrations transmitted from the tip when it catches on calculus. The closer your finger is to the source of vibration, the more likely you'll detect it. If you're gripping tightly or holding way up high, you won't feel anything. Hold it so loosely someone could almost grab it out of your hand.

Critical Distinction

Curette = ONE cutting edge → can go subgingival

Because both sides of a scaler are active, you can go just barely to the gingival margin, maybe a touch subgingival, but NOT deep — you'll lacerate tissue with that second cutting edge.

Peri-Implant Maintenance

Implant-Safe Instruments

Titanium is a soft metal. Standard steel curettes are harder than titanium and will scratch the implant surface.

The Evidence
Unreinforced resin curettes produce the LEAST alteration of the titanium implant surface following instrumentation. Gold-plated metal and reinforced resin curettes significantly alter the titanium surface.
— Cross-Poline, Shaklee, Stach. American Journal of Dentistry, Vol. 10, No. 1, 1997
  • Resin instruments are honestly pretty lousy — thick, bulky, hard to maneuver
  • The newer option: titanium-tipped instruments — same material = no scratching, way better handling
  • For probing: use a plastic-tipped probe around implants, not a standard metal probe

Should You Probe Around Implants? — The Debate

AspectDetails
The Concern (FACT) There is NO connective tissue insertion into titanium. Unlike a natural tooth where CT fibers insert into cementum, around an implant the fibers just run parallel to the surface and tighten around it. There's no true attachment.
The Theory (NOT PROVEN) When you push a probe through that loosely-adapted tissue, you create a space. When you pull it out, that space is like a "10-lane superhighway" for bacteria to dive down.
The Reality Studies have NOT shown that probed sites break down more than non-probed sites. Probing is critical for detecting problems early.
Bottom Line
YES, probe implants. Be gentle, be judicious, but always probe at recalls. By the time the radiograph shows catastrophic bone loss, it's too late — same principle as every other radiograph limitation we've discussed.

The Cement Problem — A Growing Crisis

The History

  • Early implants: all screw-retained. Had an external hex (~0.6mm height) and the screws would loosen or break because of the mechanical design
  • Field shifted to cement-retained crowns, which seemed great — except now subgingival cement is causing peri-implant mucositis and periimplantitis everywhere
  • Fleisher shows a case: reflected a flap around an implant, found a GLOB of cement sitting subgingivally. This is not rare.
  • Gets referrals for patients 3 years post-restoration with bone loss — all because of undetected subgingival cement

Fleisher's Clinical Pearls on Cement

#Pearl
1You probably don't need a strong cement for implant crowns. Even temporary cement is often sufficient and nearly impossible to remove the crown from.
2Do NOT fill the crown with cement and smash it on like a regular crown — you'll push excess subgingival and leave it there.
3The newer composite cements are almost impossible to remove once set.
4Tie-base system: cement the crown to the abutment OUTSIDE the mouth, then screw the whole assembly in — avoids subgingival cement entirely.
5The field is moving back toward screw-retained with better internal connection designs.

Plastic-tipped Probe

Can You Extend Beyond 3-Month Recalls?

Fleisher's Answer: Technically Yes, Practically Almost Never

  • You have a system that's WORKING (3-month recalls keeping patient stable). Why would you change a working system?
  • The Axelsson & Lindhe study showed the 3-month group held stable for 6 years. The moment you stretch to 4 or 6 months, you're removing the very thing that was keeping them controlled
  • The balance between bacterial insult and host response CHANGES over time. Stress increases (spouse dies, kid gets sick, financial problems), immune function fluctuates, aging changes everything
  • A patient who was stable at 3-month intervals during a calm period of their life might relapse during a stressful period if you've already stretched them to 6 months
  • Clinical experience: patients who get extended almost always relapse. The older people get, the more problems occur
For the Exam
3 months is the standard for perio patients. Extending carries real risk. If a question asks "can you extend recall intervals for a stable perio patient?" — the nuanced answer is: theoretically yes, but the evidence supports maintaining 3-month intervals because extending removes the factor that created the stability.

Reattachment vs Repair vs New Attachment vs Regeneration

The Most Testable Conceptual Distinction in the Entire GTR Lecture

Your slide shows these side by side. Fleisher defines each one:

TermDefinitionWhat Actually Happens
Reattachment Reconnecting what was surgically separated in a HEALTHY area When you do surgery, your flap extends beyond the diseased area into healthy tissue. You surgically separate connective tissue from root surface in a HEALTHY area, do your procedure, then put the flap back. In that healthy area, the CT will reattach — it was connected before, you temporarily separated it, and it reconnects. This is expected and routine. Not new attachment because there was never a loss of attachment in that zone.
Repair
(Long Junctional Epithelium)		 What happens in MOST surgical procedures in areas of actual attachment loss The epithelial cells migrate down the inner aspect of the flap along the root surface. It heals, looks healthy, probing depth decreases, no bleeding. BUT — you do NOT have new connective tissue fibers inserting into previously diseased cementum. The epithelium essentially forms a barrier that prevents connective tissue from reaching the root. This is a long junctional epithelium. It's functional healing, but it's NOT true new attachment.
New Attachment CT fibers inserting into previously diseased cementum The actual connective tissue fibers inserting into previously exposed, diseased cementum. This is the real deal — the tissue that was lost to periodontitis is actually being rebuilt at the cellular level.
Regeneration New attachment + complete restoration of ALL lost structures Not just connective tissue fibers reinserting, but the COMPLETE restoration of architecture and function of ALL lost tissues — new cementum, new PDL fibers, AND new bone. First described by Melcher (1969). Regeneration is the ultimate form of new attachment.

Mnemonics to Keep These Straight

Mnemonic — "RE-RE-NE-RE"
  • REattachment = reconnecting what was surgically separated (healthy tissue reconnecting)
  • REpair = long junctional epithelium (looks healed but no new fiber insertion)
  • NEw Attachment = connective tissue fibers inserting into previously diseased cementum
  • REgeneration = new attachment + complete restoration of all lost structures (bone, PDL, cementum)
The Clinical Framing
"Repair is the body's default. Regeneration is the dream. The whole point of GTR is to override the default and achieve the dream."

What They Observed at One Week

Healing was NOT uniform across the exposed bone surface. The MOST activity — the most robust, fastest healing — was happening at a specific location: right at the level of the periodontal ligament.

Where are those cells coming from? Someone says epithelium, someone says bone. So you have PDL cells migrating out and healing the wound FASTER and DIFFERENTLY than the epithelial cells migrating from the edges or the bone cells coming from below. A few weeks later, everything fills in, but you can still see that the PDL-origin healing is leading the charge.

The Birth of GTR
This observation — that different cell types produce different healing outcomes at different speeds — is literally the birth of the GTR concept. Goldman and others started questioning: if PDL cells heal differently than epithelial cells and bone cells, what happens if we CONTROL which cells get to participate?

The Four Cell Types — "Winning the Race"

The Slide Question

"What are the different cell types which have the potential to be involved in the healing process after surgery?" and "Allowing certain cell types to participate while excluding others… is known as Guided Tissue Regeneration."

Ranked by Speed — Fastest to Slowest

#Cell TypeSpeedOutcome if They WinVerdict
1 Epithelial Cells FASTEST — the sprinters Migrate down inner aspect of flap along root surface → form long junctional epithelium REPAIR — body's DEFAULT healing mode
2 Gingival CT Cells FAST — from the flap tissue Contact root surface → ROOT RESORPTION — body starts eating the root BAD — ironically, the body's default (epithelial migration) is PROTECTIVE against this
3 PDL Cells MODERATE — different embryologic origin than gingival CT Connective tissue fibers inserting into previously diseased cementum NEW ATTACHMENT — the goal!
4 Bone Cells SLOWEST — the tortoises Bone fuses directly to root, no PDL space → ANKYLOSIS Tooth becomes part of jawbone — no ligament, no shock absorption, no proprioception
Fleisher's Emphasis
PDL cells and gingival connective tissue cells migrate at roughly the same rate, but they are FUNDAMENTALLY DIFFERENT cells that produce completely different outcomes. Same speed, completely different biology.

The Critical Insight — Why GTR Makes Sense

You need to handicap the race. The fastest cells (epithelium) produce the least desirable outcome (just repair). The cells you WANT to win (PDL cells) are slower. So you need to physically BLOCK the fast cells from reaching the root surface, giving the PDL cells time to get there first.

Mnemonic — "Every Girl Plays Ball"
  • Epithelium = fastest = long junctional epithelium (REPAIR)
  • Gingival connective tissue = fast = ROOT RESORPTION (bad!)
  • PDL cells = moderate = NEW ATTACHMENT (the goal!)
  • Bone cells = slowest = ANKYLOSIS (tooth fuses to bone)
The Simplest Way to Remember
"The fastest runner gives the worst prize." Epithelium wins the race naturally but gives you the least desirable healing. GTR is about rigging the race so the RIGHT cells win.

Why Gore-Tex?

After the millipore filter proved the concept, they needed something that could actually be used clinically. Enter Gore-Tex — polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), a stretched Teflon. Already used in medicine for vascular surgery patches and artificial ligaments, so it had a track record of biocompatibility.

The First Gore-Tex Animal Experiment

Created defects in dogs, wrapped Gore-Tex around the tooth to block epithelial and gingival CT migration, compared to control sites with no membrane.

Control Site at Re-entry (56 days)

Not much happened. Minimal healing of the defect.

Gore-Tex Site at Re-entry (56 days)

Tissue filling the defect — BUT Fleisher makes a critical point: looking at the tissue clinically tells you NOTHING. You can't tell by looking whether it's repair or new attachment. You MUST look histologically.

Gore-Tex membrane sitting between the flap and the root surface. The membrane blocks epithelium and gingival CT. The ONLY cells with access to the root surface are bone cells and PDL cells. PDL cells are faster than bone → PDL cells win → new attachment.

Gore-Tex Membrane Anatomy — Two Distinct Zones

Two Structurally Different Portions

Top: OPEN MICROSTRUCTURE (Coronal)

  • Gore-Tex stretched MORE here, creating larger pores
  • Purpose: allow connective tissue fibers from the overlying flap to grow INTO the membrane — anchoring the flap so it doesn't slide apically and cause recession
  • Fleisher's honest clinical take: great idea in theory, didn't work that well in practice. He and his colleagues still got a lot of recession. The tissue didn't consistently grow into the open microstructure the way they hoped.
  • Histologic slides show this at 100x — you can see the stretched, porous architecture

Bottom: OCCLUSIVE MEMBRANE (Body)

  • Tightly structured, no cell penetration
  • This is the part that does the actual work of GTR — physically blocking epithelial cells and gingival CT from reaching the root surface
  • Nothing gets through this portion
  • Histologic slides show this at 25x — dense, no penetration
Trimming Principle
When you cut the membrane to fit, you trim from the SIDES and BOTTOM (the occlusive portion). You try to preserve as much of the open microstructure (top portion) as possible because that's what interfaces with the flap.

Gore-Tex: Past Tense

Fleisher explicitly says he talks about Gore-Tex in the PAST TENSE because Gore doesn't manufacture it anymore — there isn't enough demand. A company still makes the PTFE membrane under the name Cytoplast. But the field has largely moved to resorbable membranes.

Why Gore-Tex Is STILL the Gold Standard Despite Being Replaced
Resorbable membranes have one major uncertainty: you don't know exactly when they'll resorb. They might absorb too quickly before healing is complete. Gore-Tex stays until YOU take it out — total control over timing.

But the trade-off is a second surgery for removal, which is inconvenient, costs more, and adds morbidity.
For the Exam
The ONLY advantage of resorbable over non-resorbable membranes is: no second surgery needed for removal. That's it. Everything else favors Gore-Tex.

The Full Surgical Technique — Step by Step

Complete Clinical Walkthrough

Step 1 — Initial Incisions
  • Intrasulcular incisions — NOT an internal bevel. You want to preserve MAXIMUM tissue, especially all keratinized tissue, because you need to replace the flap as coronally as possible to cover the membrane.
  • Vertical releasing incisions generously spaced mesial to the defect so there's no tension on the flap.
  • Preserve the interdental papilla.
  • Excise the pocket epithelium from the inner flap surface.
↓
Step 2 — Flap Reflection
  • Full thickness mucoperiosteal flap — NOT partial thickness. You need to get down to bone and down to the root.
  • Reflect PAST the defect so you have bone exposed beyond the margins of the bony defect.
  • Why? Because the membrane needs to extend 2-3mm beyond the defect onto healthy bone. If you don't reflect far enough, you can't seat the membrane properly.
↓
Step 3 — Defect Preparation (Debridement)
  • Remove ALL granulation tissue.
  • Thoroughly plane all root surfaces — Fleisher says he goes for the "smooth, glassy" look on the root.
  • You can use thin tapered diamond burs carefully to remove surface cementum where hand instruments can't reach, but be very careful not to remove too much root structure.
  • Use hand instruments aggressively, ultrasonics as supplemental.
↓
Step 4 — Membrane Shaping
  • Cut the membrane to size by trimming the apical and lateral borders ONLY (preserve the coronal open microstructure).
  • The membrane must cover the ENTIRE defect and extend 2-3mm apically beyond it.
  • This is critical — the membrane needs to tent over the bone margin so there's SPACE underneath for PDL cells to migrate coronally. If the membrane is flush against the root with no space, there's nowhere for the cells to go.
  • For complex defects (like a wraparound — facial + interproximal + palatal), trimming the membrane to fit takes a LONG time. It has to wrap around the tooth through the interproximal and onto the palate. Tedious, precise work.
↓
Step 5 — Suturing the Membrane
  • TWO rounds of suturing.
  • First: suture the membrane directly around the tooth — go through the membrane, around the tooth, back through the membrane, tie it down.
  • The tension should pull the Gore-Tex SNUGLY against the tooth but NOT cause the edges to curl. If it curls, those curled edges will poke through the flap and cause membrane exposure/perforation.
  • Gore-Tex suture or Dexon suture is recommended.
↓
Step 6 — Flap Closure
  • Second round of suturing — replace the flap over the membrane.
  • The membrane should be COMPLETELY covered, sitting 2-3mm below the coronal aspect of the flap margin.
  • This often requires coronally advancing (or "hiking") the flap.
  • Close interproximal sutures FIRST, vertical releasing incisions LAST.
Fleisher's Honest Clinical Admission
The company photos show beautiful complete coverage with no membrane visible. That was the rare exception. In his experience, membrane exposure was very common. The flap would recede and you'd see the open microstructure poking through. Initially this was very concerning. Over time, they learned that underneath the exposed membrane, something amazing was happening — PDL cells had migrated CORONAL to the outer flap. Tissue was "bubbling up" underneath.
↓
Step 7 — Post-Operative Care
  • Follow-up at 2 weeks and 6 weeks.
  • Membrane stays in place 4-6 weeks typically (up to 3 months if fully covered, but exposure usually limits it to 6-8 weeks).
  • Patient uses chlorhexidine rinse daily to keep the area clean and prevent infection.
↓
Step 8 — Membrane Removal
  • If not covered by tissue, a gentle tug often removes it.
  • If the open microstructure has tissue ingrowth, you need a small incision to dissect it free.
  • The occlusive body portion usually comes out easily.
  • Anesthetize first, snip the suture, pull it out.

Complications — Know All Four

#ComplicationDetails
1 Infection If active infection develops with pus, try antibiotics first, but you may have to remove the membrane prematurely. Prevention = chlorhexidine rinse.
2 Perforation Membrane pokes through flap. Usually from tying membrane sutures too tightly, causing edges to curl.
3 Recession Fairly common. The flap recedes, exposing membrane. Not ideal but the healing underneath can still be excellent.
4 Gingival Irregularities Disparity between the outer flap level and the tissue that grew underneath the membrane creates irregular contours.

Clinical Cases — Three That Tell the Whole Story

Case 1: The Wraparound Defect (Before and After)

  • Presentation: Premolar with massive attachment loss on the facial, interproximal, AND palatal.
  • Alternative considered: even if you considered implants, you'd have to extract both premolars, deal with a huge bone level disparity, do ridge augmentations — a nightmare of work.
  • Treatment: bone graft + resorbable membrane.
  • One year later: tremendous result. Saved the patient enormous aggravation.
The Critical Point — Why Bone Graft Under a Membrane?
The Swedish studies (Karring, Nyman, Lindhe) showed that bone contacting root surface produces resorption and ankylosis. So why are we putting bone graft under the membrane right against the root?

Fleisher's answer: the bone graft material they use is freeze-dried bone — it's NOT active living bone. It serves primarily as a scaffold. It has some osteoinductive potential but it takes a LONG time to actually become vital bone. Meanwhile, PDL cells are migrating at a much faster rate. So the PDL cells reach the root surface and establish new attachment BEFORE the bone graft material can cause any resorption problems. The graft fills space and provides a framework; the PDL cells win the race on the root surface.
American Approach vs Swedish Approach

Swedish (Purists)

Their studies showed bone against root = bad, so they didn't graft.

American (Pragmatists)

"We've been bone grafting since the 70s, we're not giving it up" — combined grafts with membranes.

Clinically, the resorption the Swedes demonstrated in animal studies doesn't seem to happen when using processed freeze-dried bone grafts. Both approaches work, but the combination (graft + membrane) became the dominant clinical practice.

Case 2: The Molar Wraparound with Membrane Removal Photos

  • Presentation: Big defect on both buccal and palatal. Membrane wrapping around the whole tooth.
  • At time of Gore-Tex removal: flap has receded, membrane is exposed, BUT underneath — tissue has grown CORONAL to the outer flap. It's "bubbling up."
Fleisher's Key Point — The PDL Proof
That tissue bubbling up coronal to the flap can ONLY be from PDL cells. Why?

  • The membrane blocked epithelium and gingival CT.
  • The only cells in that space were bone and PDL.
  • There's NO WAY bone cells moved that far in 4-6 weeks — bone is the slowest cell type.
  • So it HAS to be PDL-origin connective tissue.
  • And this connective tissue behaves differently than the connective tissue from the flap — you would NOT get this result from just doing a flap without a membrane.

Case 3: The 20-Year-Old Premolar Case — "I Didn't Go For It"

Same premolar case from the Diagnosis lecture.

DetailFinding

Complete loss of facial bone from CEJ almost to the apex. Calculus all the way down the root. The defect was enormous — 10mm+.

The Compromise

He doesn't think he'll get a good result, so he compromises:

  • Instead of placing the membrane all the way to the CEJ (which is what GTR is designed to do), he positions it lower
  • Apically positions the flap to minimize pocket depth mechanically rather than relying on complete regeneration
  • Uses Gore-Tex with titanium struts and TACKS to hold the membrane in place (can't tie it around the tooth because it would need to be way up high)
  • Also places bone graft behind the membrane

The Result

He gets bone and PDL fill. The tacks kept the membrane completely covered, so it stayed in place longer. Great outcome.

Fleisher's Regret
Fleisher is COMPLETELY UNHAPPY — because he didn't go all the way. He's "100% confident" that if he had placed the membrane up to the CEJ and coronally positioned the flap to cover it, he would have gotten full regeneration all the way up.

"I didn't go for it... and I would have gotten it 100%."

He tried to convince the patient to do a second procedure to get the remaining attachment, but the patient declined — he was happy enough with the result as it was.

Teaching Points from This Case

#PointExplanation
1 Flap design Because there was no interproximal bone loss, he kept the flap to just that one tooth, angling the vertical releases to allow access for the tacks. Didn't need to extend to adjacent teeth.
2 Reattachment zone Where the flap DID extend beyond the diseased area onto healthy tissue, that area heals by REATTACHMENT (not new attachment). The CT was surgically separated from healthy root surface and will simply reconnect.
3 Root planing rationale There are no PDL cells on the exposed, diseased root surface (they were destroyed by disease). It's covered in epithelium, endotoxins, and calculus. You need to clean it to a smooth, glassy finish so the PDL cells migrating coronally from below have a clean surface to attach to. Some research says you don't need to be as aggressive, some says you do — Fleisher goes aggressive.
4 Epithelium blocking A student asks "how do you keep epithelium out if you didn't suture the membrane all the way up?" Fleisher's answer: the flap is positioned just coronal to the membrane, and the epithelial cells would have to migrate THROUGH the bone cells to reach the root surface below the membrane. They can't do that. So even without the membrane extending all the way up, the bone itself acts as a barrier in the apically positioned scenario.

QuestionAnswer

01

GTR Part 1 — Speed Review

The Core Concept in One Paragraph

Fleisher's Speed Round
GTR has been shown HISTOLOGICALLY to produce true new attachment — connective tissue inserting into cementum that was previously diseased. The essential component is a membrane that excludes gingival epithelial cells and gingival connective tissue from healing against the root surface. This leaves only TWO cell types: PDL cells and bone cells. PDL cells migrate faster than bone, so they reach the root first and create new attachment.

Why GTR Exists

You can treat periodontitis, resolve inflammation, reduce pocket depths — but you almost never get back what was lost. A patient with 7mm of attachment loss might end up with 3mm probing depths after treatment. Looks great on paper, but they still have less bone, less PDL, less cementum. They're "healthy but compromised." The dream is "healthy and uncompromised" — and that requires new attachment.

Four Terms — Know Cold

TermOne-Line Definition
ReattachmentReconnecting what was surgically separated in a healthy area. Routine, expected, not exciting.
RepairEpithelial cells race down the root → long junctional epithelium. Looks healthy but no CT fiber insertion. The body's default.
New AttachmentCT fibers physically inserting into previously diseased cementum. The real thing.
RegenerationNew attachment + complete restoration of ALL lost structures (cementum + PDL + bone). First described by Melcher 1969.

The Race — Four Cell Types

Cell TypeSpeedOutcome
EpithelialFastestLong junctional epithelium (REPAIR) — body's default
Gingival CTFastRoot resorption — epithelium's default is actually PROTECTIVE against this
PDLModerateNew attachment — the goal
BoneSlowestRoot resorption AND ankylosis — both bad
Update from Part 1
In this review, Fleisher says bone cells can cause BOTH resorption AND ankylosis. In Part 1 he mainly emphasized ankylosis. For the exam, know that bone alone contacting the root can produce either outcome — both are bad.

The Four Landmark Studies

StudyProved
Karring 1980 (beagles)Bone alone → ankylosis + resorption. No new attachment.
Nyman 1980 (monkeys)Gingival CT → root resorption.
Nyman 1982 (monkeys, millipore filter)PDL cells regenerate → new attachment confirmed histologically.
Nyman 1986 (monkeys, elastics)Coronal PDL migration required for new attachment.

Key Clinical Pearls

  • Three Paradigm Shifts: SRP era (1890s–1950s), Flap era (1957–1978), Osseous Graft era (1965–1981) — all claimed new attachment, none could prove it with probes/radiographs alone. Only histology is definitive.
  • Dry Ice Case (Goldman): 18-year-old, exposed vital bone from dry ice. Healing concentrated at PDL level → sparked the GTR concept.
  • American vs Swedish: Americans combine bone graft + membrane. Swedes used membrane alone. Both work — freeze-dried bone is scaffold, PDL cells outpace it.
  • Gore-Tex: PTFE, non-resorbable, gold standard. Replaced by resorbable membranes (only advantage = no second surgery). Cytoplast is current brand.
  • 8-Step Technique: Intrasulcular incisions → full thickness flap past defect → debridement to "smooth, glassy" → trim membrane (sides/bottom only) 2-3mm beyond defect → suture membrane (snug, no curling) → flap closure 2-3mm coronal to membrane → CHX rinse, 4-6 weeks → membrane removal.
  • Tissue "bubbling up" at membrane removal = PDL cells (only possible source given membrane blocked epithelium/CT, bone too slow).
  • Under-promise, over-deliver. Always manage patient expectations before GTR.
  • Pseudopocket callback: find the CEJ, measure gingival margin, calculate CAL. Same answer from day one.

GTR vs GBR — The Critical Distinction

The Most Commonly Confused Concepts

Directly from the slide: "What is the major conceptual difference between GTR and GBR?" Fleisher hammers this because students confuse them constantly.

GTR — Guided Tissue Regeneration

Around TEETH.

Any procedure where you're trying to regenerate attachment around a natural tooth — even if you use bone graft AND a membrane — is GTR.

The goal is new connective tissue attachment to the root surface.

GBR — Guided Bone Regeneration

In EDENTULOUS areas.

Building bone where there are NO teeth — extraction sockets, ridge augmentation for implants, etc.

The goal is new bone formation, NOT attachment to a root (because there's no root).

Fleisher's Exact Words
"Don't be confused about this. If you're trying to build back bone and attachment around a tooth, that is NOT guided bone regeneration. Guided bone regeneration is bone growth in an edentulous area."
Student Clarification
A student asks about a case where bone graft was placed around a tooth WITH a membrane. Fleisher confirms: that's GTR, not GBR, even though bone grafting material was used. The defining factor isn't WHAT materials you use — it's WHERE you're doing it.

Around a tooth = GTR. Edentulous area = GBR.
Mnemonic
"GTR = Got Teeth Remaining. GBR = Got Bone to Rebuild (no teeth)."

Bony Defect Classification — The Combination Defect

Case Presentation

Fleisher shows a clinical case with a defect and asks "what kind of defect is this?" The answer: it's a combination defect. The defect changes character as you move from apical to coronal:

PositionDefect TypeWalls PresentPredictability
Most Apical 3-Wall Defect Bone on mesial, distal, AND lingual (root = 4th wall) Most predictable — bone walls contain graft, blood supply from multiple directions
Middle 2-Wall Defect One bony wall lost; still have lingual + hemiseptum on adjacent tooth Moderate predictability
Most Coronal 1-Wall Defect Only the hemiseptum on the adjacent tooth remains Least predictable — minimal containment, blood supply from one direction only
The Principle
More walls = more predictable regeneration. 3-wall > 2-wall > 1-wall.
Most real-world defects are combination defects like this one.

Clinical Application

  • Traditionally, a tooth with this kind of extensive, multi-wall defect would be deemed hopeless and extracted.
  • With GTR + bone grafting, you can treat it. Fleisher shows the case treated with particulate bone graft + resorbable membrane — healed beautifully.
  • He also mentions a liquid resorbable membrane that you can mold to any shape while liquid, and body heat causes it to solidify. Easier to adapt to complex defect shapes than trying to trim a sheet of material.

Osteoconductive vs Osteoinductive

The Critical Distinction — ABSOLUTELY Testable

Osteoconductive

The material acts as a SCAFFOLD. It facilitates new bone formation from EXISTING bone.

Analogy: Think of it like a trellis for a vine — the trellis doesn't grow, but it provides a structure for the vine to grow along. The bone cells from surrounding bone crawl along the scaffold and lay down new bone.

Most graft materials are at least osteoconductive.

Osteoinductive

The material can actually INDUCE new bone formation from undifferentiated cells or cells that are NOT osteocytes.

It's recruiting and transforming cells that weren't going to become bone into bone-forming cells. This is way more powerful.

Not all materials can do this.

The Key Slide Question: "Is DFDBA Osteoinductive?"

Study 1 — Urist 1965 (Published in Science)
Discovered that demineralized, lyophilized (freeze-dried) segments of bone induced new bone formation when implanted in muscle pouches in rabbits. This was the foundational discovery — demineralization exposes Bone Morphogenic Proteins (BMPs) which are the actual osteoinductive factor.

Urist coined the term "Bone Morphogenetic Protein" in 1971 in the Journal of Dental Research.

So the THEORY is: DFDBA (which is demineralized) should expose BMPs and therefore be osteoinductive. FDBA (which is mineralized, NOT demineralized) would mainly be osteoconductive — a scaffold without exposed BMPs.

Study 2 — Schwartz et al. 1996
Tested 14 batches of commercially available DFDBA from 6 different bone banks.

Bone from 3 of the 6 banks showed NO signs of osteoinductive potential. Even different lots from the SAME bank had different levels of osteoinductivity.
The Real Answer for the Exam
DFDBA is THEORETICALLY osteoinductive because demineralization exposes BMPs, but in PRACTICE it's highly variable and unreliable. Different bone banks, different lots, different donors all produce inconsistent results.

You can't count on any given batch of DFDBA to be osteoinductive. It might be, or it might just be a scaffold (osteoconductive only).

Vital Bone Grafts — The Specifics

Extraoral Autograft (Iliac Crest)

  • Schallhorn showed effectiveness
  • Complications: second surgical site to harvest AND root resorption
  • Freezing the harvested bone controls the resorption problem
  • This is rarely done anymore for perio — too invasive for the benefit

Intraoral Autograft (Tuberosity, Extraction Sites, etc.)

  • Varying degrees of success in controlled studies
  • Quality of the graft varies
  • Intra-oral vital sites do NOT cause the same resorptive problems as iliac crest bone

Cancellous vs Cortical — "Which Is Better?"

TypeOsteogenic PotentialWhy
Cancellous (Marrow) Better Contains hematopoietic marrow with better osteogenic potential
Cortical Less LESS osteogenic potential than cancellous

Cancellous > cortical for osteoinductivity. Vital cancellous bone from a tuberosity or recent extraction site provides better osteogenic potential because of the hematopoietic marrow.

Important Detail from the Slide
Vital cancellous bone also contains PDL cells (shown on the slide image). If harvested from a recent extraction site, there may be PDL remnants in the bone, which adds regenerative potential beyond just osteogenesis.

The 1980s Bone Grafting Case & The Muscle Pouch Test

Before GTR Existed — What Perio Used to Do

A defect you can't identify from the buccal or lingual clinically, but radiographically there's bone loss interproximally. When you open the flap: a mixed/circumferential defect wrapping from buccal to lingual.

Step1980s Approach (Pre-GTR)
1Opened the flap
2Debrided the defect
3Packed bone graft material into the defect
4Placed tetracycline INTO the bone graft — considered helpful then, now considered unnecessary or potentially harmful. Fleisher: "these days we think that's not necessarily a good thing"
5No membrane on top — just bone graft, sutured closed
6Allowed to heal

The Radiograph Trap — One More Time

Before/after radiographs show what LOOKS like new bone growth — bone level is more coronal after treatment.

Fleisher's Exact Words
"Is there new bone? When you look at the radiograph, it certainly appears that there's been new bone growth. Is there new attachment? There is NO WAY to know by looking at these radiographs."

A new nuance: when they've looked histologically, they've SOMETIMES seen epithelial cells migrating down between the new bone and the root surface. So you can have new BONE formation (graft worked as scaffold) but still have a long junctional epithelium against the root — meaning REPAIR, not true new attachment. The bone filled in, but CT fibers never inserted into cementum.

Other times, histology HAS shown true new attachment. So bone grafting alone (without a membrane) CAN sometimes produce new attachment, but it's unpredictable. You're "making assumptions and guesses based on what you see the clinical outcome to be."

Why Membranes Were Developed
Bone grafting alone gives you bone fill but unreliable attachment. Adding a membrane controls WHICH cells reach the root surface, making new attachment predictable rather than a coin flip. This is exactly WHY the American approach combined graft + membrane — the Swedes were right that you can't prove new attachment from a radiograph.

The Muscle Pouch Test — How You Test Osteoinductivity

The experiment: Take bone graft material. Place it in the middle of a MUSCLE (not near any bone). Wait a couple months. Look under a microscope.

Key reasoning: a muscle contains ZERO osteocytes. There are no bone cells anywhere near this graft material. Every cell in muscle tissue is non-mineralized.

If BONE Forms in the Muscle

The material is OSTEOINDUCTIVE. It recruited undifferentiated cells (mesenchymal stem cells in the muscle tissue) and transformed them into bone-forming cells. It INDUCED bone formation where bone had no business being.

If NO Bone Forms

The material is NOT osteoinductive. It's just a scaffold. Might still be osteoconductive (helps bone grow along it when bone cells are already present), but it can't create bone on its own.

Why This Test Is Elegant
If you test the material by placing it in a bone defect, you can't tell whether new bone formed because the material induced it (osteoinductive) or because surrounding bone cells simply grew along it (osteoconductive). Both mechanisms could be happening simultaneously. The muscle pouch eliminates that variable by removing ALL bone cells from the equation.

Fleisher's clinical bottom line: "Our preference is to always use osteoinductive materials when possible" — osteoinductive = can actively recruit and transform cells. Osteoconductive = just a passive scaffold needing existing bone cells.

DFDBA Controversy & Vital Bone Details

Extra Autograft Details

  • Vital allograft (cryopreserved bone, transplant iliac): Fleisher explicitly says "It's not something that's used in dentistry at all. It's used for other applications." Know the definition for the exam but know it's not clinically relevant in perio.
  • Iliac crest root resorption: The bone was so bioactive (packed with living cells and growth factors) that it actually caused root resorption — it was essentially TOO active and attacked the root surface. Freezing the harvested bone reduced the bioactivity enough to control this.
  • Intraoral vital bone does NOT cause the same resorption — likely due to much lower volume and concentration of bioactive cells vs a massive iliac crest graft.

The Osteoid Bone Harvest Technique

Clinical Pearl
Extract a tooth, let the socket partially heal for a couple of weeks, then go BACK into that extraction site and harvest the osteoid bone (newly forming bone) from the socket. That fresh osteoid bone has excellent osteogenic potential because it's loaded with active bone-forming cells in the early stages of healing.

So sometimes you strategically plan the extraction timing to create a harvest site for a future grafting procedure.

The DFDBA Controversy — Fleisher's Clinical Translation

The theory (Urist): Demineralization of cortical bone exposes BMPs → BMPs are osteoinductive → DFDBA should be osteoinductive → DFDBA should be better than FDBA (which is mineralized, doesn't expose BMPs).

This made complete logical sense and for years the field operated under this assumption.

The reality (Schwartz et al. 1996): Three of six banks showed NO osteoinductive potential. Even different lots from the SAME bank varied.

Fleisher's Clinical Translation
"There's no guarantee that the vial of bone you're utilizing for your patient that day is truly osteogenic just because it's demineralized freeze-dried." In the 90s, it was a "hit or miss proposition."

Some bone banks NOW claim to test every lot specifically for osteoinductivity before selling it — "at least that's what they tell us." You can hear his skepticism.
Essay Framing
Advantage of DFDBA: potential osteoinductivity through exposed BMPs (Urist 1965).
Disadvantage: inconsistent osteoinductive potential (Schwartz 1996), batch-to-batch variability, dependence on bone bank quality.

Bio-Oss, Sinus Lifts & The Blood Vessel

Bio-Oss Processing

Bio-Oss = anorganic bovine bone (xenograft). Processing: treated at high temperatures for more than 15 hours, followed by several hours of cleaning with strong alkaline solutions. The result is a "highly purified anorganic natural bone mineral." All organic material is removed — what remains is the mineral scaffold.

Prion Concern
Bone is classified by experts as prion-free tissue (unlike brain, intestine, or spinal cord from cattle). The extensive processing further eliminates any risk.

The Sinus Lift Procedure — Step by Step

Step 1
Initial incision with vertical release on the lateral wall of the maxilla
↓
Step 2
Reflect flap to expose the lateral sinus wall
↓
Step 3
Create a window in the lateral wall using a handpiece initially, then switch to piezo (safer, less risk of perforating the membrane)
↓
Step 4
Remove the remaining bone carefully
↓
Step 5
Push the Schneiderian membrane (sinus lining) inward and upward, creating space
↓
Step 6
Check for tears: have patient hold their nose and breathe. The membrane will move like it's breathing. If there's a perforation, you'll see air bubbles or the membrane won't move properly. If torn: repair with a membrane.
↓
Steps 7-8
Pack xenograft material (Bio-Oss) into the space. Place a membrane over the window.
↓
Steps 9-10
Tack it in place. Suture closed.

The bone that forms in the sinus with Bio-Oss is incredibly dense and histologically confirmed as vital bone. The xenograft scaffold gets incorporated and replaced with living bone over time.

Fleisher's Sinus Lift Horror Story

Clinical Gold
His first few sinus lifts went great. Then he had a big perforation. He describes looking through the perforation and being able to see toward the base of the skull — "a very unsettling thing."

He packed it with bone, sent the patient home, and didn't sleep that night waiting for the ER call. Never got a call. The patient came back a week later completely fine.

His point: "You can do the biggest procedure and the patient has no symptoms, or the smallest procedure and get 10 phone calls." You never know.

The Blood Vessel Complication — Posterior Superior Alveolar Artery

The posterior superior alveolar artery is a branch of the maxillary artery. It can travel right through the area where you're cutting your sinus window.

Position on CBCTDescription
IntrasinusalBelow the membrane
IntraosseousWithin the bone
SuperficialOn the outer cortex
  • Before CT scans, nobody knew about this — sinus lifts done blind to vascular anatomy.
  • With CBCT you can now see the foramen and plan window placement to avoid it.
  • If severed: significant bleeding. If cut at edges of bur, you can't clamp the ends — have to crush the bone and hope it coagulates. "A very unnerving thing."
  • However, significant hemorrhage during sinus lifts is RARE overall because the blood vessels are terminal branches of peripheral vessels.

Non-Osseous Materials — Emdogain & PRP

Enamel Matrix Derivative (Emdogain)

From the slide: "Enamel Matrix — harvested from porcine tooth buds, contains a mixture of low molecular weight proteins including amelogenins, ameloblastin, and enamelin."

Fleisher's Honest Take
"I was never impressed with this." It didn't make sense mechanically — you're squirting a gel derived from pig tooth buds into a bone defect and expecting new bone formation.

But he concedes: "The research that they publish is pretty impressive... the fact of the matter is the research shows that it works pretty well and so at this point I accept it."
  • Mechanism: enamel matrix proteins bind with blood and surrounding tissues in the defect, promote new bone formation. PDL cells show enhanced protein production and proliferation in response to EMD.
  • Theory: mimicking natural tooth development — enamel matrix proteins play a role in cementogenesis (cementum formation), so applying them to a diseased root may help re-initiate that developmental process.
For the Exam
EMD (Emdogain) = from porcine tooth buds, contains amelogenins/ameloblastin/enamelin, enhances PDL cell activity, evidence supports it works for regeneration despite mechanistic skepticism.

PRP (Platelet Rich Plasma/Protein)

From the slide: "Platelets: a major source of growth factors at wound sites" — with specific growth factors: PDGF, TGF-B, EGF/TGF-ALPHA, IGF-1. And: "The use of PRP concentrates platelets in a wound site by 330-900%."

How PRP Works

  • Take the patient's own blood, spin it in a centrifuge, separating and concentrating platelets at the bottom of the test tube.
  • Pipette out the platelet concentrate.
  • Platelets are packed with growth factors (PDGF, TGF-beta, etc.), so by concentrating them 300-900%, you're massively amplifying the growth factor signal at the wound site.
  • Combine PRP with bone graft material. Bio-Oss mixed with PRP gel using thrombin to activate, creating a moldable mixture packed into the defect.
The Combo Logic
The bone graft provides the SCAFFOLD (physical framework for cells to grow on). The PRP provides the SIGNALING (concentrated growth factors that recruit and stimulate cells to form bone).

Scaffold + Signal = Enhanced Regeneration.

PRP — The Setup and the Letdown

The Logistics

  • In the early days, Fleisher had to bring a phlebotomist into the office with a centrifuge. Draw patient's blood, spin it down, concentrate platelets. Extra cost (passed to patient), extra operatory occupied, extra coordination.
  • His first PRP case? The patient had an undisclosed needle phobia and passed out in the chair during the blood draw. Classic dental office chaos.

PRP for Wound Healing — Even Without Bone Graft

Clinical Pearl
PRP matrix is excellent for WOUND HEALING even without bone graft underneath. He shows a case where PRP material was placed between buccal and palatal flaps that couldn't be fully approximated (no primary closure). Normally a gap like that would open up and fail. But the PRP matrix maintained closure, tissue filled in over it, healed beautifully.

Even if PRP doesn't grow more bone, it's a fantastic wound healing agent.

The Bombshell — PRP Didn't Live Up to the Hype

Fleisher's Words
"The research didn't bear it out. We didn't get any better results from using PRP in terms of bone growth. We didn't get MORE bone growth. We got it a little faster in the studies, so it would speed up the process a little bit, but it didn't enhance the amount of bone that you grow."

His honest assessment: "Was it worth it to go and do all of that just for that purpose? I'm not sure."
PRP ClaimReality
More bone growthNO. No significant increase in total bone formation.
Faster healingYES. Speeds up the process somewhat.
Better soft tissueYES. Excellent wound management and closure.

PRF (Platelet Rich Fibrin) — a newer variation that's "supposedly a little more bioactive than PRP." He shows a sinus lift case using PRF with demineralized bone allograft and "sticky bone" (bone graft mixed with PRF creating a moldable, cohesive mass). Results were good. Different surgeons use different approaches — there's no single right way.

Alloplasts & GEM 21 (PDGF) — Fleisher's Favorite

Alloplasts — The Honest Truth

From the Slide
"Alloplast — inert biologic fillers. Calcium Sulfate (plaster of Paris), Calcium ceramics, Bioactive glass polymers. Unlikely to result in true regeneration. Hydroxyapatite, Tricalcium Phosphate."

Fleisher is even more blunt: Bioactive glass "has been shown to not be bioactive in any way. Forget about the fact that it calls itself a bioactive glass." Calcium sulfate is literally medical-grade plaster of Paris. These are FILLERS. Osteoconductive scaffolds at best, unlikely to produce any true regeneration.

GEM 21 (rhPDGF-BB) — The Game Changer

A completely synthetic grafting system combining:

  • rhPDGF-BB (recombinant human platelet-derived growth factor) — the bioactive protein
  • Beta-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) — the osteoconductive scaffold/carrier

Mechanism — Step by Step (From the Slides)

Step 1
After implantation, PDGF is released from the β-TCP matrix into the surrounding environment.
→
Step 2
PDGF binds to specific cell surface receptors on target cells, initiating intracellular signaling cascades.
→
Step 3
PDGF-induced intracellular events lead to chemotaxis (directed cell migration) and mitogenesis (cell proliferation) of osteoblasts, PDL fibroblasts, and cementoblasts.
→
Step 4
Proliferation leads to increased matrix synthesis → formation of new alveolar bone, new PDL, AND new cementum. Angiogenesis (blood vessel formation) continues.
Key Distinction from PRP
PRP just concentrates whatever growth factors happen to be in the patient's platelets. GEM 21 delivers a SPECIFIC, purified, genetically engineered growth factor (PDGF) at a known concentration. It's targeted, consistent, and reliable.

Fleisher's Clinical Results with GEM 21

The Reveal
ALL those jaw-dropping cases from GTR Part 1 — the central incisor that looked hopeless, the lateral lower incisor with bone growth nobody would anticipate — all used GEM 21 in addition to the membrane and bone graft. That's the secret sauce he didn't mention last time.
  • Case 1: Interproximal defect with lost buccal and palatal plates → fantastic bone regeneration at 1 year
  • Case 2: Significant bone growth at 1 year
  • Case 3: More bone growth
  • Case 4: Calculus present, not endo → new bone formation at 1 year

He describes it as getting "really nice bone growth" and "fantastic results" consistently.

Success Criteria & Going Back

QuestionAnswer
What counts as success? Minimal probing depths in the area — 2 to 4mm is acceptable. If he doesn't get that, he doesn't consider it a success regardless of radiograph appearance.
Can you go back a second time around a TOOTH? Yes, but opening a flap again destroys some new attachment you just gained. Usually satisfied with what he gets. Doesn't re-enter unless absolutely necessary.
Can you go back a second time in an EDENTULOUS area? Going back costs you nothing — no attachment to lose. He'll readily do a second GBR procedure if more bone is needed for implant placement.

BMP (Infuse/rhBMP-2) — The Cautionary Tale

What BMP Is

From the slide: "Bone Morphogenetic Proteins — a group of regulatory glycoproteins which stimulate differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts." Research focused on BMP-2, BMP-3 (osteogenin), and BMP-7 (OP1).

The results look INCREDIBLE — cases going from minimal bone to massive ridge, enough for implant placement. On paper, BMP is the most powerful regenerative material available.

Fleisher's Story — One of the Best Teaching Moments

  • He'd been reading about Infuse (commercially available rhBMP-2) for years. Used in spinal surgery and orthopedics but wasn't available for dental.
  • When it became available for dental use, he immediately contacted the company. They gave him two free samples (expensive stuff — $300-400 per vial).
  • He was saving them for the PERFECT case. And like a great bottle of wine, the perfect occasion never came.
  • Then he went to a lecture. The speaker mentioned ONE sentence in passing: "You shouldn't use too much of it because it causes a very exuberant inflammatory response."
  • Fleisher went up afterward. The speaker said: "Yeah, you gotta be really careful. The patient will blow up like a balloon."
  • Fleisher realized his instinct would have been to use A LOT of material (he's a "more is better" guy, and you can't save the vial anyway). He would have potentially caused serious complications.
  • He already had GEM 21 working fantastically with no complications.
  • He sent the Infuse samples back to the company without ever using them.

The Bigger Lesson — Fleisher's Clinical Philosophy

On New Products
"When that rep comes in and talks about all the great things about this new product, they sometimes don't mention the potential problems and pitfalls. They're trying to sell you a product. If you're already doing something that works really well, there's not much incentive to jump to a new product."
#Framework
1If what you're using works well → stick with it
2New products need LONG-TERM data (10-15 years, not 5-6 months) before switching
3The marginal gain (98% → 99%) may not be worth the unknown risks
4Don't be the first to adopt something new just because it sounds better on paper
5Always consider: what's the WORST thing that can happen, and is the potential benefit worth that risk?
BMP for the Essay
Most potent osteoinductive material available. Stimulates mesenchymal stem cell differentiation into osteoblasts. Dramatic results for ridge augmentation. BUT: risk of exuberant inflammatory response ("blow up like a balloon"), expensive, dose-sensitive (too much = complications). Research focused on BMP-2, BMP-3, BMP-7. Available as Infuse (rhBMP-2).

Fleisher's preference: GEM 21 (PDGF) over BMP because comparable results with fewer complications.

GBR Case — Membrane Exposure Teaches a Lesson

  • Extracted teeth, placed non-resorbable membrane with titanium struts and apical tacks.
  • Planning point: apical tacks mean at re-entry you need a BIG flap to get everything out. Anticipate during initial surgery — don't place tacks where you can't retrieve them.
  • Sutured up but didn't have primary closure — opening over the membrane. If he'd used PRP (wound healing benefit), he could have avoided that gap.
The Teaching Pearl
At re-entry: beautiful solid bone formation everywhere EXCEPT the one spot where the membrane had been exposed. That area had no bone.

Fleisher: "I don't think it's coincidental. I think it made a difference." Membrane exposure in GBR allows soft tissue ingrowth into the regenerating area, which compromises bone formation at that specific site.

Primary closure is CRITICAL for GBR — anywhere the membrane is exposed, you're likely to get incomplete bone formation.

Final result: massive ridge dimension increase. Implants placed successfully. But that one spot of exposure = one spot without bone. The lesson is clear.

Socket Preservation — Philosophy & Procedure

The Fractured Central Incisor — Fleisher's Sermon

A central incisor probing 15mm+ on an isolated site. First thought = fracture (isolated deep probing = always suspect fracture, just like the premolar case from Part 1). He confirms the fracture after exposure.

The Critical Point
"If you don't have the materials you need to regenerate this area, then you have NO BUSINESS taking this tooth out."

Why? Without grafting, the tissue collapses into the extraction site. You end up with a massive V-shaped soft tissue collapse that creates an aesthetic disaster — whether you're planning an implant (won't have enough bone) or a bridge (massive black triangle, long pontic). Completely avoidable if you graft at the time of extraction.
The Collagen Plug Rant
"You go to a Yankee Dental conference and somebody shows you 'I take the tooth out, put a little collagen plug in it, and everything is great.' It's not. It doesn't work. It won't work. It can't work. No matter what they say, they're making it up. 1 in 100 will form new bone."

If you've lost the buccal plate, you need an extensive regenerative procedure, not a collagen plug and a prayer.

Socket Preservation Procedure — Step by Step

Step 1
Extract the tooth. You're left with a massive defect — buccal plate missing, socket exposed. Without intervention, this will collapse.
↓
Step 2
Rebuild the buccal plate temporarily. Place a membrane on the facial to create a wall where bone used to be. Like building a retaining wall — prevents soft tissue ingrowth and gives the graft material something to stay behind.
↓
Step 3
Tack the membrane in place. Suturing alone won't hold it reliably — needs tacks against the facial aspect.
↓
Step 4
Fill with bone graft material. Pack the socket and defect area behind the membrane wall.
↓
Step 5 (MOST CRITICAL)
Coronally position the flap for primary closure. Make vertical incisions all the way into the mucosal tissue (past the mucogingival junction) so you can advance the flap coronally enough to completely cover everything. You can see the discrepancy in the mucogingival junction on the slide — that's how far Fleisher had to advance.
↓
Step 6
Ensure tension-free closure. If there's tension, it'll dehisce, membrane exposed = no bone formation at that site.

When to Extract and When NOT To

Option A: Go Ahead

You have the right materials (bone graft, membrane, tacks), you know how to coronally position a flap, you can achieve primary closure. Extract AND graft in the same appointment.

Option B: DO NOT EXTRACT

You're NOT comfortable with the regenerative procedure. Refer the patient to someone who will extract AND graft in the same appointment.

The Common Mistake
A GP says "easy extraction, I'll pull it and send them to the specialist to fix it." But by then the tissue has collapsed, the ridge has resorbed, and the specialist has a much bigger problem to solve.

The extraction was easy — the reconstruction is a nightmare.

What He Found When He Opened

Massive defect. His concern shifted immediately — stopped worrying about the implant and started worrying about whether the CANINE was saveable. The defect extended onto the distal of the canine with significant bone loss.

Clinical Thinking Moment
Your priorities can change mid-surgery. He went in planning for implant placement. Within minutes, his primary concern became saving the adjacent tooth. Flexibility and real-time assessment are everything.

The Immediate Implant Debate

Some clinicians will place an implant immediately as long as they can achieve primary stability (implant doesn't spin — mechanically locked in). They'll graft around it and bury it.

Fleisher's Position
The research shows immediate placement in compromised bone doesn't succeed at as high a rate as placement into adequate existing bone. The difference isn't enormous but it's real — not just statistical variance.

His preference: stage it. Graft first, let it heal, come back and place the implant into solid bone. More predictable.

The Treatment — GTR AND GBR in One Surgery

A student asks: is this GTR or GBR? Fleisher's answer: it's BOTH.

Distal of the Canine = GTR

Regenerating attachment around a tooth. PDL cells participating.

Premolar Socket = GBR

Regenerating bone in a toothless area. Only osteoblasts contributing.

Same surgery, same materials, same flap — but two different biological processes happening simultaneously based on whether there's a tooth present or not. This is the most elegant demonstration of the GTR vs GBR distinction in the entire lecture.

Healed nicely. Implant placed at a later date without major problems. Dr. Pani's Framework — MGD Beyond Recession

Setting Up the Lecture

MGD is way broader than just "recession" — when you write "MGD with recession" on your perio chart, that's scratching the surface. There's a whole taxonomy of conditions under this umbrella, and understanding what's actually going on determines whether you refer, what you can achieve surgically, and what expectations to set.

Key Clinical Framing
Know what you can and can't fix BEFORE you refer. Pani gives an example of being referred a patient "for root coverage" where there was no root exposed — just gingival enlargement from inflammation or medication. She says "what am I gonna cover here? There's no root exposed."

Knowing the difference between recession (root exposed, needs coverage) and enlargement (tissue overgrown, needs reduction) saves everyone time and money.
02

Phenotype & Subgingival Margins

Why Phenotype Matters — Same Insult, Different Response

Thin Phenotype + Subgingival Margins + Inflammation

→ RECESSION. The thin tissue can't handle the inflammatory insult so it retreats apically, exposing root surface.

Thick Phenotype + Subgingival Margins + Inflammation

→ POCKET FORMATION. The thick tissue doesn't recede — instead it swells and forms pockets. Real clinic patient: crown margins 3mm subgingivally, thick biotype, tons of bleeding and pain, no recession — just pocketing and swelling.

Student Misconception She Shuts Down
Students sometimes think "if I put a crown margin subgingivally, the inflammation will do my crown lengthening for me."

NO. Chronic inflammation does NOT equal crown lengthening. In thick biotype you get pocketing, not tissue reduction. In thin biotype you get uncontrolled recession, not a nice predictable margin. Crown lengthening is a controlled surgical procedure — you can't outsource it to inflammation.
Terminology Note
We now say phenotype (what we observe clinically) rather than biotype (which implies genetic determination). Same concept, updated term. She uses both interchangeably, and your slides list both.

Clinical Cases — Three Teaching Moments

Case 1: "Referred for Root Coverage but It's Actually Enlargement"

  • Patient sent to Pani for root coverage on #24 and #26.
  • She looks and there's no recession — the tissue is enlarged from inflammation/medication.
  • The referral was based on a misdiagnosis.
Exam Scenario
Don't confuse gingival enlargement (tissue growing over the tooth) with recession (tissue pulling away from the tooth). They look superficially similar if you're not careful, but the treatment is completely opposite.

Case 2: The Aggressive Brushing Case

  • Generalized recession. Students immediately guess malocclusion/traumatic occlusion as the cause.
  • Pani pushes back hard: "Evidence from several observational studies shows that occlusal forces do NOT cause gingival recession."
  • In this case, the patient was brushing with the hardest toothbrush using an extremely aggressive technique. Zero plaque everywhere — "negative plaque" she jokes. But the mechanical trauma from brushing was destroying the tissue.
Testable Point
If an exam question asks "what caused this generalized recession?" and gives options including traumatic occlusion and aggressive brushing — the answer is brushing. Occlusal forces don't cause recession — that's a myth Pani specifically debunks with research evidence.

Case 3: The Flossing Cuts Case

  • A thin cleft of recession, typically interproximal or on the facial near the papilla.
  • When Pani sees this pattern, she always asks the patient to demonstrate their flossing technique.
  • Invariably: jamming the floss straight down through the contact, snapping it into the tissue.
  • Correcting the technique can improve the area and make it more favorable for grafting later.

Clinical Case — Recession + Altered Passive Eruption

The Anterior Case

  • Students identify recession on #11 (canine area). But Pani pushes further — what about #9?
  • The gingival margins are at different heights. Centrals and canines should be at roughly the same level, with laterals about 1mm more coronal.
  • Margins being off = COMBINATION of slight recession AND altered passive eruption.
  • The crowns were made based on enamel that was visible clinically — but there was more tooth structure hidden under the gingiva.
Key Point
If someone refers this patient for a soft tissue graft, it won't work. A soft tissue graft can't "stick" to enamel surface — it needs root surface (cementum) or bone. The correct treatment is crown lengthening to expose the hidden tooth structure, not grafting to cover already-visible tissue.

  • Having a dehiscence does NOT guarantee recession. It INCREASES THE RISK, especially in combination with other factors (thin tissue, inflammation, brushing trauma).
  • A patient can have a significant dehiscence with no recession if the tissue is thick and there's no inflammatory or mechanical insult.
  • But add inflammation to a dehiscence with thin tissue? Recipe for recession.
  • She shows a case: only 2mm of clinical recession but the actual dehiscence underneath is MASSIVE — extending way further apically. The visible recession is just the tip of the iceberg.
  • Bone is normally 1-2mm apical to the CEJ. If the crest is way below that = dehiscence invisible until you probe deeply or raise a flap.
  • Connects directly to Fleisher's radiograph lesson — you can't see buccal dehiscences on a PA radiograph.

Occlusion Does NOT Cause Recession

Pani's Evidence-Based Stance
"Evidence from several observational studies shows that occlusal forces do NOT cause gingival recession."

The notion that bad occlusion causes recession is wrong (unless teeth are literally biting on soft tissue). This connects to Essay Topic #4 (Occlusal Trauma) — occlusal forces can affect the attachment apparatus through different mechanisms (widened PDL, bone changes), but they do NOT cause gingival recession.

Root Sensitivity — The Promise You Should NEVER Make

The Co-Resident Horror Story

Pani watched a co-resident get burned by this. Patient comes in with recession and sensitivity. Beautiful root coverage procedure — root is covered. But the patient STILL has sensitivity. They're furious: "Whatever you did didn't work, I went through all that pain for nothing."

Why Sensitivity Persists After Coverage

  • Your sulcus still has depth, and that sulcular depth may still be on the root surface (below the CEJ)
  • Even with the gingival margin at or above the CEJ, the probe still reaches root surface within the sulcus
  • Thermal stimuli (especially cold) can still reach exposed dentin tubules through the thin sulcular epithelium
Pani's Rule: NEVER promise that root coverage will eliminate sensitivity. Inform the patient that desensitizing agents (Gluma, SuperSeal — connecting back to the SPT lecture) can help, but sensitivity resolution is a bonus, not a guarantee. If it goes away, great. If it doesn't, the patient was warned.

The Post-Treatment Recession Paradox

Patient comes in with periodontitis — deep pockets, attachment loss, inflammation, but maybe not much visible recession. You do SRP, maybe surgery. Treatment is successful — pockets reduce, inflammation resolves. And then the patient has RECESSION they didn't have before and they're unhappy.

Why It Happens — Two Simultaneous Changes

DirectionWhat Happens
ApicallyLong junctional epithelium forms, reattachment/repair occurs at a more apical level
CoronallyInflammation resolves → swollen, puffy tissue SHRINKS → gingival margin recedes to its true position relative to the now-lower bone level
The recession was always "there" — it was just hidden by swollen inflammatory tissue. Successful treatment reveals the true tissue level. These cases are typically Miller III/IV or Cairo RT2/RT3 (interproximal attachment loss) — meaning root coverage to restore the original gingival margin is usually NOT predictable.

Clinical lesson: Tell patients BEFORE treatment that recession is a likely outcome of successful periodontal therapy. Connects to Fleisher's "healthy but reduced periodontium" — the patient is now healthy, but with less tissue, less bone, and visible recession. That's the expected outcome, not a complication.

Pedicle Grafts — Rotational Flaps

Key Principle: Pedicle grafts use tissue ADJACENT to the recession site — rotated over the defect. The tissue stays connected to its blood supply (the pedicle), which is a major advantage over free grafts.

Laterally Repositioned Flap Grupe & Warren 1956, De Waal 1988

  • Split-thickness flap from the papilla mesial or distal to the recession
  • Slide laterally over the exposed root
  • Donor site re-epithelializes on its own (split-thickness = periosteum stays = bone protected)
  • Flap gets nutrition from the wide pedicle base AND from periosteum of the prepared recipient bed

Requirements

  • Adequate keratinized tissue (thickness AND quantity) adjacent to the recession
  • Works for single isolated teeth, NOT multiple recession sites
Pani's Key Detail: You must prepare a recipient bed with partial-thickness dissection on the OTHER side of the recession too — the rotated flap needs to land on periosteum/connective tissue, not on epithelium. Connective tissue to connective tissue = blood supply = graft survival.

Aesthetic concern: The donor site may have a different mucosal appearance after epithelialization, creating a visible inconsistency in the mucogingival junction.

Double Papilla Flap Cohen & Ross 1968

  • Both papillae (mesial and distal to the recession tooth) mobilized with split-thickness dissection
  • Rotated over the recession from both sides — meets in the middle
  • DUAL blood supply from both pedicles
Pani's Clinical Pearl: She did one in residency and jumped at the opportunity because these cases are rare. You need the perfect setup — adequate papilla on both sides of a single recession.

Technique detail: Suture the MOVABLE flap to the NON-MOVABLE one FIRST. If you cut both papillae free simultaneously and then try to suture two floppy mobile pieces together, it's a nightmare. Do one side, stabilize it, then do the other.

Usually combined with a CTG for additional bulk and predictability. Position tissue 2-2.5mm CORONAL to CEJ to account for post-op shrinkage.

Advanced Flaps — Coronally Advanced FGG & Semilunar

Coronally Advanced Free Gingival Graft Bernimoulin 1975

A two-stage approach:

  1. Stage 1: Place a free gingival graft to increase keratinized tissue thickness and width. Wait for maturation.
  2. Stage 2: Coronally advance that grafted tissue to cover the recession.
Pani's Clinical Take: This has the MOST long-term stability of any technique. She's found this through personal experience AND conversations with experienced clinicians. The Zucchelli Technique & Tunnel Debate

Zucchelli Technique — CAF for Multiple Recession

The key innovation: the incision design is mathematically calculated based on how much recession you need to cover.

The Logic: Say you have 3mm of recession on a canine. Your new surgical papilla has its tip positioned AT LEAST 3mm apical to the existing papilla tip. When you advance everything coronally, that new surgical papilla slides up, and the free gingival margin moves coronally by that same distance — covering the 3mm of exposed root.

Before you ever pick up a blade, you MEASURE the recession at each tooth, and that measurement determines exactly where you place your incision lines. No guessing.

Critical Steps

  • Follows the same split-full-split concept (split at papilla → full at bone → split apically)
  • De-epithelialize the papilla — the new surgical papilla's inner surface must contact connective tissue, NOT epithelium. CT-to-CT = adhesion = survival
  • Release all internal muscular attachments — if you leave them, (1) you can't physically advance the flap, and (2) muscle pull shrinks tissue back during healing
  • CTG enhancement usually placed underneath for thickness → less post-op shrinkage → more predictable long-term coverage

The Flap vs Tunnel Debate — "Popcorn-Worthy"

Pani attended a debate between the Italian group (CAF advocates, Zucchelli et al.) and a group advocating the tunneling technique:

  • The CAF group had to release tissue underneath papilla without formally raising a flap. The tunnel group called them out: "I see some tunneling happening over there!"
  • The tunnel group accidentally ripped a papilla or had to elevate tissue. The CAF group fired back: "I think you needed to elevate a flap here!"
The Takeaway: These techniques CONVERGE in practice. A pure CAF sometimes needs tunneling elements, and a pure tunnel approach sometimes needs flap elements. The best clinicians use BOTH as needed. It's not dogmatic — it's about reading the case and adapting.

Free Gingival Grafts — The Full Deep Dive

Pani's Joke

She stopped calling them "free gingival grafts" because patients thought the procedure was FREE of charge. A resident once had a full argument with a patient about payment because "you said it was free!"

Why Pani Likes FGGs

If done correctly, they're very predictable. She can identify them clinically on patients who had them 20+ years ago — they don't disappear over time. That's long-term stability.

The Technique — Two Surgical Sites

Recipient Site Preparation (the "bed")

  • Split-thickness incision at the MGJ
  • May include vertical incisions mesial/distal (or semilunar depending on anatomy)
  • Creating a bed of periosteum and connective tissue — the graft sits on this
  • May suture mucosal tissue to depth of vestibule, or trim excess flabby mucosa
  • Can eliminate the frenum at the same time

Donor Site (Palate)

  • Pani uses a template — takes the internal paper from a blade package, traces exact size needed. Ensures precise graft dimensions
  • Harvest 2mm apically from palatal tooth margins (avoid creating palatal recession)
  • Graft thickness: at least 1mm ("at least 1 millimeter or so, otherwise it might shrink and you might not have the outcome you want"). Slide says 0.75-1.25mm.
  • Be careful of the greater palatine artery — know your anatomy to avoid cutting it

Placement

  • Graft placed at recipient site with NO dead space (dead space = no blood supply = graft necrosis)
  • Secured with mattress sutures, simple interrupted, or sling sutures depending on the case

Healing of Free Gingival Grafts

Key Difference from Pedicle Grafts: A pedicle graft never loses its blood supply — it stays connected via the pedicle base. An FGG is completely severed and must establish an entirely new vascular network from the recipient bed. This is why bed preparation is so critical — the periosteum and CT of the recipient site ARE the blood supply. If the bed isn't prepared properly (epithelium left, dead space present), the graft starves and fails.

Three Phases Gargiulo & Arocha 1967, Oliver 1968, Nobuto 1988

PhaseTimelineWhat Happens
1. Initial0-3 daysPlasmatic circulation sustains the graft — like a sponge soaking up fluid from the wound bed. The EPITHELIUM of the FGG actually DEGENERATES — the original epithelium dies and will be replaced later.
2. Revascularization2-11 daysCritical phase. Blood vessels from recipient bed grow INTO the graft and anastomose (connect) with graft vessels. Blood circulation re-established. Capillaries proliferate. Fibrous union forms. Graft re-epithelializes with new epithelium.
3. Maturation11-42 daysNormal vascular system established by day 14. Epithelium matures and keratinizes.

Subepithelial CTG — The Gold Standard

What CTG Is

Instead of taking the full-thickness graft from the palate (epithelium + CT = FGG), you take ONLY the connective tissue layer and place it UNDERNEATH your flap. The overlying flap covers the graft completely.

Advantages & Disadvantages

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Excellent color blend — patient's own facial tissue covers graft (no color mismatch like FGG)Two surgical sites (palate donor + recipient)
Increases zone of keratinized tissuePost-operative discomfort

Critical Distinction: CTG gives you LESS keratinized tissue gain than FGG. With FGG, you're directly transplanting keratinized epithelium. With CTG, the amount of KT depends largely on what you started with at the recipient site. If the primary goal is INCREASING KT width (not root coverage), FGG may be the better choice.

Three Harvesting Techniques

TechniqueDescription
1. Double incisionTwo parallel incisions on the palate, dissect the CT layer between them. Gets an elongated, longer piece.

Pani describes witnessing a horror scene: "I have never seen so much blood coming out of a patient's mouth." Hitting that artery during harvest is a real emergency.

Hemostasis options: Collagen sponge, collagen tape, surgical glues, suction/suck-down devices. Multiple approaches work — the key is controlling bleeding before closing.

Placement

  • CTG secured at the CEJ with sling sutures
  • Flap advanced coronally over it (2-2.5mm coronal to CEJ for shrinkage)

Alloderm & Alternatives

What It Is

Acellular human dermal matrix — processed human dermis made of collagen, elastin, and vascular channels for revascularization. Used INSTEAD of harvesting a CTG from the palate.

When to Use It

Patients who refuse a second surgical site ("I don't want a wound on the roof of my mouth"). Pani always proposes it as an option but tells patients: "Your gold standard is your own tissue, but this is a good alternative." Studies show comparable outcomes.

CRITICAL Technique Requirement: The Alloderm MUST be completely covered by the flap. If Alloderm gets exposed:
  1. It becomes necrotic
  2. It smells terrible — "as if you have a dead animal in your mouth"
This is different from CTG, which can survive partial exposure. Alloderm cannot. Flap elevation and coronal advancement must be PERFECT.

Special warning: Be especially careful with mandibular anteriors — tissue is very thin and perforation is easy.

Practical Details

  • Hydrate for 15-20 minutes before placement (soak while prepping recipient site)
  • Secure with sling sutures
  • Recipient site technique doesn't change from CTG
  • Other alternatives: specific collagen membranes, tissue-engineered grafts — same tunneling or CAF techniques, just different graft material underneath

Orthodontic Correction & Pinhole Technique

Orthodontic Correction of Recession

If a tooth is positioned too far facially (outside the alveolar housing) → dehiscence, thin tissue, recession. If orthodontics moves that tooth LINGUALLY (back INTO the housing):

  • Bone forms on the facial (tooth now inside housing)
  • Tissue thickness increases
  • Gingival height increases
  • Recession decreases or resolves
Pani's Case: Tooth #25 appeared to need a graft, but proper orthodontic alignment alone achieved gingival tissue improvement WITHOUT any surgical procedure. No graft needed.

Lesson: Before jumping to surgery, consider whether orthodontic repositioning could solve the problem. If recession is caused by tooth position, moving the tooth may be more effective than grafting.

The Pinhole Technique — The Mystery

Concept: instead of raising a formal flap, make a small "pinhole" incision in the vestibular area, tunnel underneath, advance tissue coronally, place materials to maintain new position.

Pani's Take: "It's being advertised as magic." But the developers require clinicians to sign contracts preventing them from sharing details, instruments, or techniques. This means the broader community cannot study it or produce evidence supporting or refuting it.

"It might be the best technique to use, but if you're not showing us what you're using and what's happening, we cannot have evidence to prove it's actually better than what we know so far."

Bottom line: Know it exists as a concept, but there's no independent evidence base.
