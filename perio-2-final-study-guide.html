Overview & Tissue Fundamentals

I. Gingival Tissue Fundamentals

The document opens by defining every tissue type you need to know cold. Here's the hierarchy:

KEY DEFINITION
Gingiva = fibrous investing tissue covered by keratinized epithelium that immediately surrounds a tooth. It's contiguous with the PDL and mucosal tissues. It naturally maintains a scalloped/wave-like configuration — interdental papillae are always coronal to mid-buccal/lingual marginal gingiva, regardless of underlying bone contours.

The four tissue zones (coronal → apical):

TissueDefinitionKey Features
Free GingivaNot directly attached to tooth surface; separated from tooth by the gingival sulcusMarginal gingiva is the most coronal part and is ALWAYS free gingiva
Marginal GingivaMost coronal portion of gingivaInvariably free gingiva
Attached GingivaFirm, stippled, tightly bound to periosteum/toothExtends from base of sulcus/pocket to MGJ
Alveolar MucosaLocated just apical to MGJLoosely attached and MOVABLE; critical for flap management
EXAM ALERT
The MGJ stays at a CONSTANT position. When a perio pocket forms, the epithelial attachment migrates apically along the root — the amount of attached gingiva decreases by an EQUAL amount. The MGJ doesn't move. This is a classic exam concept.

Pseudopocket vs. True Periodontal Pocket:

This is the CENTRAL concept of the entire module. Everything — every procedure choice, every limitation — flows from this distinction:

FeaturePseudopocketTrue Periodontal Pocket
Probing depthIncreasedIncreased
Epithelial attachmentNO apical migrationYES — migrated apically
Bone lossNONEYES — must have occurred
CauseCoronal proliferation/swelling of gingivaDestruction of attachment apparatus
TreatmentGingival surgery (EBG, IBG, curettage)Flap surgery with osseous access

Causes of gingival enlargement (pseudopockets):

Plaque-induced inflammation is the most common cause. But the module specifically lists Drug-Induced Gingival Overgrowth (DIGO) with three drug categories you must know:

Drug CategoryExampleUsed For
AnticonvulsantDiphenylhydantoin (Dilantin)Epilepsy — creates 'Dilantin hyperplasia'
ImmunosuppressantCyclosporineOrgan transplant patients
Calcium channel blockerNifedipine, AmlodipineHypertension / cardiac conditions

II. Historical Perspective (Condensed)

The module walks through 25+ centuries of gum surgery. Here's what's actually testable:

Susruta (6th century BC, India) — first to mention gum surgery as treatment.
Pierre Fauchard (1746) — removed excess gum tissue with scissors; others used hot wire.
J.M. Riggs (1860s) — brought perio surgery to the US. G.V. Black described his procedure: 'cut away all diseased tissue to the line of attachment of the periodontal membrane.'
1920s (Robicsec, Zentler, Newman, Ward) — modifications including removal of marginal bone, mucoperiosteal flaps, preoperative pocket drug packing.
Dr. J.W. Hisey (1893) — first reported 'excess hypertrophy of the gums'; considered it a tumor and removed entire alveolar process including gums, bone, and teeth.
Until 1930s-40s — diseased gums = 'oral focus of infection' theory (arthritis, gastric problems attributed to it).

KEY DEFINITION
Goldman (1950) introduced the term 'gingivoplasty' — a procedure to reshape gingival deformities to produce 'physiologic gingival contours.' His concept: healthy gingiva has knife-edged margins, interdental grooving, facial/lingual gingiva more apical than interproximal, and conical papillae. This form-follows-function concept has been the basis for gingival and osseous resective surgery since.
Four Types of Gingival Surgery

III. The Four Types of Gingival Surgery

Gingival surgery involves epithelium + underlying CT. Two purposes: (1) correct gingival changes when there are NO bony aberrations, and (2) as part of flap reflection to access osseous/dental defects. The primary purpose = eliminate pseudopockets.

A. Gingival Curettage

The module distinguishes two things that people confuse:

ProcedureWhat It DoesKey Points
Subgingival Root Curettage
(= SRP / debridement)		Removes calculus, necrotic cementum, bacterial products from root surfaceSharp instrument to root → coincidental scraping of inner pocket wall
Gingival (Soft Tissue) CurettageFocuses specifically on inner surface of gingival pocket wallIntentional removal of sulcular and junctional epithelium after SRP. Complement to SRP, not replacement

The theory: converting a 'pathologic wound' into a 'surgical wound' → swollen tissue shrinks down faster and more. PD is reduced. Regardless of which approach removes gingival tissue, a gingivectomy by definition has been performed.

B. External Bevel Gingivectomy (EBG)

KEY DEFINITION
The classic gingivectomy. The entire soft tissue wall of the pseudopocket — including sulcular, junctional, AND oral epithelia — is removed by an external bevel incision at ~45 degrees.

What gets removed: ALL three epithelial layers (oral, sulcular, junctional) plus underlying CT. The excised tissue is thrown away. Healed result at ~1 month shows reduced sulcus depth.

C. Internal Bevel Gingivectomy (IBG)

KEY DEFINITION
Sulcular and junctional epithelium + subjacent CT removed by an internal bevel incision to bone. If the thinned flap is reflected past the MGJ → can be positioned apically to reduce PD.

Modified Widman Flap = similar but the flap is NOT reflected past the MGJ → cannot be apically positioned. Can only be replaced to original position. Advocates argue PD reduction comes from regeneration of lost attachment after careful debridement.

D. Gingivoplasty

Surface reshaping of gingival deformities to create more easily cleaned physiologic form. KEY DISTINCTION: it does NOT remove junctional or sulcular epithelium and does NOT reduce probing depth. Used alone (e.g., to remove soft tissue craters after NUP/ANUG or flap surgery) or more commonly as part of the EBG to refine contours.

VI. Healing of Excisional Surgery (EBG & Gingivoplasty)

This is a detailed histological timeline. Know the sequence and the key events at each stage:

TimeEpitheliumConnective TissueOther
0-5 hours—Acute inflammation; PMN migration startsBlood clot forms; fibrin meshwork covers raw wound surface; clot begins to organize
5 hrs - 1 dayEpithelial cells migrate from wound margins; basal cells begin to proliferateNew blood vessels budding (angiogenesis); fibroblasts begin collagen productionPROTECT the blood clot; surface is extremely fragile at this stage
2-3 daysCovering wound at 0.5mm/day. ANOTHER source (sulcular epithelium) = FASTER coverageGranulation tissue organizing; PMN count decreasing; new collagen fibers formingEpithelial cells proliferating and migrating across wound surface
4-10 daysCovers wound in 4-7 days avg. Complete epithelial coverageGRANULATION TISSUE fully established; collagen fibers INCREASE density and supportGingival contour improving; tissue remodeling begins
10-28 daysAt 21 days: clinically normal, similar to pre-surgical appearanceGranulation tissue remodels to mature connective tissue; progressive organizationKeratinization of epithelial surface resumes; complete maturation by ~28 days
EXAM ALERT
Clinical Implications of Excisional Surgery Healing:
1) Longer bevel = greater wound surface = longer healing (cells migrate further)
2) More accurate 45-degree bevel = less height in regenerated gingival margin (esp. thick tissue — a 90-degree cut creates a thick ledge that epithelializes into ANOTHER pseudopocket!)
3) Smooth wound bed (no tags) = rapid epithelialization + better lamina propria
4) Inflammation delays healing → meticulous OHI critical
5) The forming sulcus is inflamed from the start → potential for apical migration of attachment
6) Leftover calculus → excessive local granulation tissue → slow healing → remove at first post-op
7) Polish teeth immediately after dressing removal; resume plaque control

Healing Timelines

VII. Healing of Incisional Surgery (IBG / Flaps)

TimeWhat's HappeningClinical Significance
0-4 daysBlood clot forms between flap and bone (helps hold flap in position)If flap is NOT HELD = THINNER, less stable clot; GREATEST risk of failure is in this initial interposed blood clot period
4-10 daysFibrin clot resorbed, replaced by young CT from underlying boneBy 1 week flap is held; PDL cells begin to proliferate; bone resorption at crestal peak may occur
10-21 daysInflammation less marked. Crestal resorption with CT repairConnective tissue repair; periosteum reattachment complete; PDs 1-1.5mm (avoid probing in this period)
21+ daysMaturation/repair of all tissues. Bone formation replacing resorbed boneBy 1 MONTH flap is firmly attached; slight decrease in gingival crest height may occur
2-3 monthsComplete healing and maturation.Proper flap positioning critical in first weeks. Loose sutures/muscle action can displace flap
EXAM ALERT
If epithelial attachment is NOT removed via IBG, it acts as a focus for epithelial proliferation and migration → causes MORE APICAL position of attachment than normally seen post-op. This is why IBG removes junctional epithelium.
CLINICAL PEARL
OHI timing: Light brushing + flossing can start almost immediately. Prophy at weeks 1 and 3. Don't probe sulcus until at least 4 weeks (let epithelial attachment seal). Restorative with subgingival margins → no sooner than 6-8 weeks. Max anterior → wait 6 months.
