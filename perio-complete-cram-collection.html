1. Crown Lengthening — Core Concept

CL is a goal (gain clinical crown height), not a single technique. It is a restoratively-driven surgical procedure altering the periodontium (gum + bone) to enable a successful, healthy restoration. Primary method = surgical CL via osseous (bone) resection.

Primary indication: Any restorative margin that will impinge upon or invade the biologic width.

Crown Lengthening: The problem → osteotomy procedure → biologic width re-established

2. Biologic Width (BW) — THE Foundation

ESSAY Q1 Essay Topic 1, Q1: "Explain the concept of Biologic Width in depth. Include measurements and the study from which they were derived."

LITERATURE Gargiulo, Wentz & Orban (1961) — J Periodontol 42:8, 1961. Landmark study defining dimensions of the "dentogingival junction" (now = supracrestal attachment). Confirmed directly on your slides.

Definition: BW = the dimension of soft tissue attachment from the base of the sulcus to the alveolar bone crest.

Components (Gargiulo 1961 measurements)

ComponentAverage
Sulcus Depth0.69 mm
Junctional Epithelium (JE)0.97 mm
Connective Tissue (CT) Attachment1.07 mm
Biologic Width (JE + CT)~2.04 mm
Total Dimension (Sulcus + JE + CT)~2.73 mm
EXAM The 3–4 mm Clinical Rule: Must have 3–4 mm of sound tooth structure between planned restorative margin and alveolar bone crest. This accounts for ~2 mm BW + ~1–2 mm sulcus + safety margin. Professor's ideal goal = ±4 mm (confirmed on slides). He has "settled for less" in difficult cases and "it's been fine," but 4 mm is always what you aim for.
WARNING 2.04 mm is an AVERAGE, not a rule. Professor heavily emphasized: "A small amount can make a big difference!" You cannot look at adjacent teeth to determine BW of your specific tooth. Relying on 2 mm is just a "guess." That's why we use the 3–4 mm buffer.

Consequences of BW Invasion

Placing margin within this zone → body tries to recreate the space → chronic inflammatory response → chronic gingival inflammation (red, puffy, bleeding), gingival recession, periodontal pocket formation, alveolar bone loss. The body "attacks itself" trying to re-establish that ~2.04 mm.

2017 UPDATE In the 2017/2018 classification update, "biologic width" is replaced with "supracrestal supporting tissues." Know both terms.

3. Bleeding Under a Temp — The Ddx

ESSAY Q2 Essay Topic 1, Q2: "When removing a temp, you see bleeding and ulceration. How do you determine if CL is necessary, or another problem exists?"

Bleeding + ulceration under a temp does NOT automatically = BW violation. You must differentiate:

Possible CauseHow to Distinguish
BW InvasionBone sounding: margin-to-bone distance <3 mm. Radiograph may help but is NOT definitive (2D limitation). Must probe under anesthesia.
Overcontoured / ill-fitting tempExcess cement, open margins, bulky contours trapping plaque. Remove temp → inspect margin fit + contour. Inflammation resolves with proper temp.

Key diagnostic step: Bone sounding (probe under anesthesia) to determine exact margin-to-bone crest distance. If <3 mm → CL indicated. If ≥3 mm → investigate other causes above.

4. Pre-Surgical Assessment — The Roadmap
1
Bone Sounding: Probe under anesthesia to find exact margin-to-bone-crest relationship.
2
Evaluate Restorative Needs: Where will the final ideal restorative margin be?
3
Assess Keratinized Tissue (KT): Amount of KT dictates incision design (one of the most important factors).
ESSAY Q3 Essay Topic 1, Q3: "When is gingivectomy alone appropriate vs. flap + bone removal?"

KT-Based Incision Decision

Sufficient KT (>3–4 mm)

Incision: Internal Bevel (submarginal)

Rationale: Removes diseased sulcular lining. Discards excess tissue while preserving healthy KT.

Insufficient KT (<2–3 mm)

Incision: Intracrevicular (sulcular)

Rationale: Preserves 100% of minimal KT. No tissue discarded. Flap moved apically.

Gingivectomy Alone vs. Flap + Bone

Gingivectomy alone is appropriate ONLY when: (1) the problem is purely excess soft tissue (pseudopocket / delayed passive eruption / gingival hyperplasia), (2) there is adequate KT remaining after excision, AND (3) no osseous resection is needed (margin is already ≥3–4 mm from bone). Essentially: if the bone is fine and you just have too much gum, gingivectomy works.
Flap + bone removal is required when: the restorative margin is within the 3–4 mm zone to bone. You MUST raise a flap to access and resect bone to re-establish proper BW distance. Gingivectomy alone would fail here because the bone violation would persist and the tissue would rebound or continue to be inflamed.
5. The Surgical Procedure — Step-by-Step

Phase 1: Flap Design & Elevation

Initial incision = internal bevel (if sufficient KT). Releasing incisions = vertical, cutting through MGJ to create an apically positioned flap that moves down without tension. Elevation = split-thickness (sharp dissection) leaving periosteum attached to bone (critical for suturing later). Flap must be "freely movable." Adjacent areas: distal wedge removes bulky tissue hump distal to last tooth.

Phase 2: Soft Tissue Management

Remove ALL chronic granulation tissue. Gingivectomy (blending) may be performed to create seamless transition to adjacent papilla.

Phase 3: Osseous Resection (Main Event)

Goal: Remove bone to re-establish 3–4 mm from planned margin to new bone crest.

Instruments: High-speed burs (#6 or #8 round) with copious saline for bulk removal → Ochsenbein chisels (interproximal) + Sugarman files (smoothing) for positive, physiologic architecture.

EXAM The "Widow's Peak": A sharp peak of bone left in a root concavity/furcation. Why it's deadly: Gingival architecture follows bone architecture → tissue binds upward → creates an isolated, deep, non-cleansable pocket → chronic inflammation. MUST be removed and contoured. Can only be spotted if the tooth is prepped and the temp is removed for 360° visualization.

Phase 4: Closure — Periosteal Anchor Suture

EXAM Why this suture? It anchors the mobile split-thickness flap down to the immobile periosteum at the new apical level.
1
Needle enters KT of mobile flap from outside (buccal)
2
Passes through immobile periosteum at desired apical level
3
Crosses to lingual to grab lingual flap
4
Returns to buccal, knot tied on top of mobile buccal flap

The trick: Apply apical pressure ("pushing down") as you tie → anchors flap at new level.

What NOT to do: Simple loop suture over the tooth → pulls tissue coronally → defeats entire purpose.

6. Why Prep & Temp BEFORE Surgery
EXAM Two mandatory reasons:
1
Visualization: Temp removed → 360° view of prep margin → can spot Widow's Peaks and defects otherwise hidden.
2
Surgical Precision (The "Blueprint"): Surgeon removes bone based on where the prep margin ends (new crest ~3 mm apical to margin). Without the prep, the surgeon is "totally guessing" → either (A) inadequate removal = BW violation, or (B) excessive removal = unnecessary attachment loss.
7. Healing — Timing & Rebound

ESSAY Q4 Essay Topic 1, Q4: "Explain the length of time for healing prior to placing a restoration, the rationale, and consequences if insufficient time. Difference anterior vs posterior?"

LITERATURE Goldman et al. (1964) — Creeping Attachment (Periodontal Therapy, 3rd ed). Matter & Cimasoni (1976) — Creeping attachment after FGG, J Perio 47:574.
LITERATURE Van der Velden (1982) — Gingival Rebound, J Clin Perio 9:455-459. Showed 0.5–1.2 mm coronal migration over 12 months.

Immediately post-op: Flap sutured "right at the crest of bone." This is NOT the final position.

Coronal rebound: Gingival margin migrates coronally ~2 mm during healing → new healthy minimal sulcus forms → BW re-established at more apical level.

Posterior (Non-Esthetic)

6–8 weeks usually fine before final impression. Exact margin position less critical.

Anterior (Esthetic Zone)

Minimum 3 months — professor was adamant: "at least 3 months, and I mean at least." Tissue still migrating/maturing. Can have movement up to 12 months. "The more critical aesthetics are... we will wait longer."

Consequence of insufficient time: If you take impression too early, margin continues migrating coronally → crown margin becomes subgingival → esthetic failure (crown looks short), and margin may re-violate BW → chronic inflammation returns.

Papilla / Black Triangle — Tarnow's Rule

LITERATURE Tarnow, Magner, Fletcher (1992) — Landmark study on papilla fill based on bone-to-contact-point distance.

11. DME Critique & Extraction Protocol

Deep Margin Elevation — Professor's 3 Criticisms

(1) Violates core principle: crown margin finishes on restorative material, not sound tooth. (2) Doesn't solve the problem — if the DME composite invades BW, you still need CL; it just "ignores it." (3) Clinically prone to failure: overhang, open margin, BW violation. Professor called it a "disservice."

EXAM Anterior Tooth Extraction — Socket/Ridge Preservation

"Simple extraction" fallacy: Just pulling → socket collapse → "huge aesthetic significant problem."

Correct procedure (Ridge Preservation/GBR):
1
Atraumatic extraction — preserve all buccal plate
2
Socket debridement — remove all granulation tissue
3
Bone graft — particulate (allograft/xenograft) as scaffold to prevent collapse
4
Membrane — resorbable membrane over graft (GBR: blocks fast-growing gum, allows slow-growing bone to fill)
5
Coronally positioned flap — vertical releasing incisions → mobilize buccal flap
6
Primary closure — flap advanced over socket, sutured onto palate → seals graft from oral environment

Frenum: Do NOT do frenectomy at extraction time → could compromise graft healing. Address in 2nd procedure months later.

EXAM Professor's Golden Rule: "If you're not prepared to do all of this [graft, membrane, flap advancement] at the time of extraction... then you shouldn't be the person taking the tooth out."

Immediate implant? Professor said NO for massive buccal defect in esthetic zone. Too risky hoping bone "magically" grows over exposed implant. "I'd rather grow the bone first and then come back." CBCT useful AFTER extraction for planning, not at the extraction visit.

13. Occlusal Trauma — Overview & Definitions

ESSAY Q1 Essay Topic 4, Q1: "Define occlusal trauma and how it is diagnosed."

LITERATURE AAP Glossary of Periodontal Terms (2001): Occlusal Traumatism = "pathologic or adaptive changes which develop in the periodontium as a result of undue force produced by the masticatory muscles."

Key concept: TFO is specifically and ONLY a lesion of the periodontium (attachment apparatus = PDL + bone + cementum). Wear facets, fractures, root resorption are pathologic changes in tooth morphology — they are NOT occlusal trauma.

The Joshua Bell analogy: 1,000+ people "looked" but only 7 "saw." Clinically: don't just look at a bridge — actively SEE crossbite, diastema, caries, bone defects.

14. Mobility vs. Fremitus

ESSAY Q5-part Essay Topic 4, Q5: "Explain mobility vs. fremitus. When is mobility acceptable?"

Mobility

Tooth displacement from external force (applied by dentist). Assessed with 2 rigid instruments (mirror handles — NOT fingers, soft pads mask subtle movement). Apply alternating facial-lingual pressure + check vertical depression.

Fremitus

Tooth displacement from patient's own occlusal forces. Assessed with gloved index finger on facial of maxillary teeth. Patient "tap-tap-tap" (MIP) then "grind" (excursions). Feel for vibration/movement.

Miller Index (Mobility Classification)

GradeHorizontalVertical
Physiologic<0.2 mm (not clinically discernible)None
Grade 10.2 – 1.0 mmNone
Grade 21.0 – 2.0 mmNone
Grade 3>2.0 mmPresent (depressible)
EXAM If you can depress a tooth at all → automatically Grade 3, regardless of horizontal movement.

When Is Mobility Acceptable?

Mobility itself is NOT a disease. A certain increase in mobility can be accepted provided it does not disturb masticatory function or patient comfort. The key: is it stable (increased but not increasing) or progressive (increasing over time)?

EXAM Fremitus + Mobility on the same tooth = "a bad sign." Fremitus = the patient's own bite moves it. Mobility = the PDL is compromised. Together = strong proof that the patient's own functional/parafunctional habits are directly causing the pathologic mobility.
15. The Definitive Diagnostic Rule
EXAM To diagnose active occlusal trauma, you MUST have INCREASING mobility over time. This is the ONLY definitive clinical sign.
Increased (Stable / Adapted)

Tooth = Grade 1 today → still Grade 1 in 6 months. The periodontium has adapted. This is NOT active TFO.

Increasing (Progressive / Pathologic)

Tooth = "slight" Grade 1 today → "firm" Grade 1 or early Grade 2 in 6 months. Mobility worsening over time. THIS = active TFO.

Can you diagnose on first visit? YES — use patient's history: "A couple of days ago this tooth was not mobile at all, and today it is" = evidence of increasing mobility over time.

Signs Associated with TFO

Sign TypeFindings
Definitive ClinicalIncreasing mobility (progressive over time)
Other ClinicalWear facets, crazing, fractures, fremitus, migration
RadiographicWidened PDL space (classic sign), loss/indistinctness of lamina dura, angular/vertical osseous defects, root resorption, altered trabecular pattern

The rule: You can have the radiographic and other clinical signs, but if mobility is NOT increasing, you do NOT have active TFO — the periodontium is successfully adapting.

What IS the Lamina Dura?

NOT a specific anatomical structure visible histologically. It is a radiographic phenomenon — the radio-opaque white line seen because the X-ray beam passes tangentially through the full thickness of cortical bone forming the socket wall. "Loss of lamina dura" = demineralization of that cortical bone (from TFO lesion) → no longer appears as a distinct white line.

16. Competent vs. Incompetent Systems

Competent: Fully asymptomatic. Forces within adaptive range. Incompetent: Overloaded → breakdown at the "weakest link" in one of 3 areas:

1. TMJ
Pain, limited function, clicking/popping, subluxation, muscle tenderness, referred pain
2. Tooth Structure
Root resorption, cemental tears, cracking/fractures, faceting/wear
3. Attachment Apparatus
= Occlusal Traumatism (the ONLY one that = TFO)

"Weakest link" principle: Strong bone → weakest link = tooth → wear/fracture (NOT TFO). Weaker bone → weakest link = periodontium → TFO (mobility). Wear extent and TFO extent are NOT directly related — they're two separate breakdown possibilities.

17. Force Response: Light vs. Heavy

Light Force (Adaptive Range) — "Over-Bulked Crown" Example

1
Iatrogenic insult: New crown cemented with unadjusted palatal high spot → premature contact → excessive force hundreds of times/day.
2
Acute lesion: Tooth starts moving → increasing mobility. Patient: "My tooth is loose. It wasn't loose before!" (Pain variable, can't diagnose by pain alone.)
3
Adaptation: The tooth "moves to get away" from the force. PDL widens → inflammatory response → resolves and heals. Permanent PDL widening established.
4
Stable state: Tooth now has "increased" mobility (stable Grade 1) but is no longer "increasing." PDL remains widened but healed.
5
Reversibility (Treatment): Adjust occlusion → remove high spot → force eliminated → bone apposition fills in the space → PDL returns to original width → mobility returns to zero. "Completely reversible."

Heavy Force (Non-Adaptive Range)

Heavier force → larger lesion → wider PDL → greater mobility. Force exceeds adaptive capacity → tooth never adapts, lesion never resolves → increasingly mobile → progressive → tooth loss if not addressed.

18. Center of Rotation & Biomechanics

Healthy tooth: Center of rotation (fulcrum) = middle-to-apical third of root. Perio-involved tooth: Bone loss → fulcrum shifts apically toward new bone crest → longer lever arm from fulcrum to crown → same force creates much larger arc of movement → tooth appears dramatically more mobile. But the actual root movement within remaining bone may be stable. This is why a tooth can have "increased" mobility (Grade 2) yet be perfectly functional and stable for years — as long as that mobility is not increasing.

LITERATURE Coolidge (1930) — "Thickness of the Human Periodontal Membrane," JADA 24:1260. PDL width decreases with age (11–16 yrs: 0.23 mm crest; 51–67 yrs: 0.17 mm crest).
19. The TFO & Soft Tissue Rule

TFO does NOT cause (initiate) attachment loss.

TFO does NOT cause pocketing.

TFO does NOT cause recession.

20. Primary vs. Secondary Occlusal Trauma

ESSAY Q2 Essay Topic 4, Q2: "Differentiate primary vs. secondary occlusal trauma."

Primary Occlusal Trauma (POT)

Def: Lesion of the attachment apparatus caused by excessive forces on a periodontium with normal/adequate support.

Core problem: The FORCE is pathologic.

Reversible? YES — completely.

Treatment: Eliminate excessive force (occlusal adjustment/equilibration).

Example: High crown on healthy tooth → increasing mobility → adjust occlusion → mobility resolves.

Perfect example: Orthodontics (force applied → TFO created → retainer placed → heals completely).

Clinical signs: Increasing mobility, fremitus, migration.

Radiographic: Widened PDL, possible angular bone loss, indistinct lamina dura.

Secondary Occlusal Trauma (SOT)

Def: Lesion caused by normal (or excessive) forces on a periodontium weakened by periodontal disease (inadequate support).

Core problem: The BONE SUPPORT is inadequate.

Reversible? NO — not by occlusal adjustment alone.

Treatment: Splinting (compensatory — distributes force over larger root surface area).

Example: Tooth with 70% bone loss, mobile from normal chewing. No high spots.

BOARDS Full definition includes "(or excessive)" — professor disagrees but you must know it for boards.

LITERATURE Reinhart, Pao, Krejeci (1984) — J Perio Res 19:238. "PDL stresses in the initiation of occlusal traumatism." Key finding: Reduction of alveolar bone height has little effect on PDL stress until ~60% of bone support is lost. Especially true for multi-rooted molars. → A tooth with 10–40% bone loss is still biomechanically "adequate" → can still get POT, not SOT.
EXAM "Normal" ≠ "Pristine": A tooth with <1 mm of attachment loss that hasn't changed its biomechanics → still has a "normal" periodontium for POT purposes. POT doesn't require zero bone loss. You must distinguish "some" attachment loss from "inadequate" attachment loss (the 60% threshold).

21. TFO + Periodontal Disease — The Relationship

ESSAY Q3 cont. Essay Topic 4, Q3 continued: "Does occlusal trauma cause attachment loss? Explain the relationship with periodontal disease."

The Two Cardinal Rules

Rule #1: TFO does NOT CAUSE periodontitis. TFO is a sterile, non-bacterial lesion. Cannot create a periodontal pocket.
Rule #2: TFO CAN AGGRAVATE existing periodontitis. Acts as co-factor. Can "exacerbate the progression" and "augment the destruction." Does not "cause" — but can "exacerbate," "augment," or "speed up."

Glickman & Smulow Theory — "Co-Destruction"

Historical context: Glickman (Tufts) vs. Goldman (BU/GSDM) — fierce rivalry, whatever one said the other disagreed with.

Glickman's ZoneLocationPathology
"Zone of Irritation"Supracrestal tissues (free gingiva, JE, supracrestal CT)Periodontitis (plaque-induced)
"Zone of Co-Destruction"Attachment apparatus (PDL, cementum, alveolar bone)Occlusal trauma (force-induced, sterile)

The theory: TFO lesion in Zone of Co-Destruction "redirects" plaque inflammation from Zone of Irritation, pulling it deep into PDL, bypassing normal supracrestal route → "merger" → rapid angular bone loss.

CAVEAT "It's just a theory. It has not been proven to any great extent." Know the theory, know it's not proven dogma.

"Reduced" vs. "Diseased" — THE Critical Distinction

Animal studies show TFO induced on a healthy but reduced periodontium (successfully treated perio case) has no different effect than TFO on a healthy periodontium with normal bone levels. The co-destructive environment is NOT (Force + Bone Loss). It IS (Force + ACTIVE INFLAMMATION).

Clinical takeaway: A treated, healthy patient with permanent bone loss responds to forces (e.g., ortho) the SAME as a pristine patient.

Primacy of Plaque

Plaque is the "chief" cause of attachment loss. TFO alone does NOT cause attachment loss (apical migration of JE/pocketing). TFO alone causes "a change in the dimension of the PDL" and "a change in the quality of CT." Attachment loss with co-destruction is predominantly caused by plaque inflammation; TFO's role is minor, if it plays one at all.

22. Treatment of TFO

ESSAY Q4 Essay Topic 4, Q4: "How do you treat primary vs. secondary occlusal trauma?"

POT Treatment

Problem = excessive force → Reduce/eliminate the force. Occlusal adjustment (equilibration). The healing cascade: inflammation subsides → bone apposition at lamina dura → PDL returns to pre-force width → mobility returns to physiologic. 100% reversible.

SOT Treatment

Problem = inadequate bone support → Splinting. Cannot "adjust" your way out of SOT. Splinting distributes force over larger root surface area, changes the "unit" from one compromised tooth to a multi-rooted abutment. Compensatory, not curative. May also need perio therapy or extraction.

The Treatment Sequence — "Don't Jump" Rule

StepActionProcedure
A (FIRST)Eliminate inflammationSRP, prophy, OHI
B (SECOND)Eliminate/control occlusal etiologyOcclusal adjustment, splinting
CDevelop stable intra- & inter-arch relationshipsProsthetics, orthodontics
EXAM WHY "A" first? Inflammation itself causes mobility (inflammatory mediators → bone resorption → PDL breakdown). Must treat plaque inflammation first → re-evaluate → see how much mobility was from TFO vs. active perio infection. "Don't jump" to adjustment on a patient with heavy inflammation. Exception: obvious acute high-crown case.

Treat the Patient, Not the Radiograph

"Just because you have a CO-CR discrepancy, if you are asymptomatic and you're not seeing signs of disease... you don't need an occlusal equilibration." The "60-year-old patient" with a lifelong malocclusion, all 28 teeth, and no symptoms → the dentist is the problem, not the occlusion. Only treat confirmed pathology.

When confirmed, treatment options: occlusal equilibration, limited/complete orthodontics, prosthetics (splinting), or orthognathic surgery in extreme cases.

23. Ortho & Perio — The Malpractice Warning
EXAM LEGAL MALPRACTICE to start ortho on a patient with active, untreated periodontitis. Ortho = heavy force directly into active periodontal infection → co-destructive → "increase exponentially the risk" of catastrophic rapid bone loss.

Professor has been expert witness in these lawsuits. Orthodontist AND referring GDP both sued — "and they lose all the time." Why? No record of a full periodontal chart (probing, attachment levels, recession, inflammation, plaque) before treatment. The "it's not my job" defense for the GDP "doesn't fly."

Clinical mandates: (1) Full perio chart before ANY adult ortho referral. (2) Continue perio assessment DURING ortho treatment (patient can develop perio during tx from poor hygiene). (3) "CYA" with formal perio clearance letter. (4) Adolescents also need perio assessment — small subset suffer from periodontitis (aggressive).

24. Posterior Bite Collapse (PBC) & Flaring

PBC = a syndrome (not a disease entity). Faculty myth: "stable occlusion, locked in, don't need to replace the tooth" — professor: "And it's not true." PBC doesn't happen in every case, but "you don't know in advance" → recommend replacing the space for all patients.

Sequelae Cascade

Missing tooth → mesial tipping / distal migration → super-eruption of opposing → loss of posterior support → anterior flaring (bite forces redirected onto anteriors → push facially → spaces open).

Diagnosing flaring: ASK the patient: "Did you always have these spaces?" If "yes, since childhood" → natural. If "getting worse over recent years" → pathologic flaring/PBC.

The First Slide, Solved

5-unit bridge. Mesial abutment = lateral incisor. Large diastema to adjacent central. Catastrophic violation of Ante's Law (root surface area of abutments ≥ teeth replaced). Single small lateral incisor cannot support 5-unit bridge → tooth "getting beat up" → migrated away from force → extreme POT → system now adapted in a new (failed) state.

Master Framework: Subtractive vs Additive

Every perio surgical procedure is either subtractive (removing tissue/bone to reduce pockets) or additive (adding material to rebuild what's lost).

Subtractive Procedures
Subgingival root curettage (= SRP) · Gingival curettage (obsolete standalone) · External bevel gingivectomy · Internal bevel gingivectomy · ENAP (historical, not done) · Modified Widman flap · Osseous resective surgery · Distal wedge
Additive Procedures
Osseous graft · Guided tissue regeneration (GTR)

Key distinction (3 separate things people confuse): Open flap = access only, flap goes back where it was. Apically positioned flap (APF) = intentionally move tissue apically, usually WITH osseous surgery. Osseous surgery = modifying bone itself. Often combined but conceptually distinct.

The Subtractive Procedure Spectrum Essay 3

Listed least → most invasive. Understanding this spectrum IS the surgical decision-making framework.

1. Subgingival Root Curettage = SRP. Same thing, different name. Can be initial OR definitive therapy depending on reevaluation outcome.

2. Gingival Curettage — curette blade reversed to scrape inner pocket wall. No longer done standalone. Research shows no advantage over SRP alone. Happens inadvertently during SRP anyway. Don't bill separately.

3. External Bevel Gingivectomy — excise tissue from outside with #15 blade. TWO strict requirements: (1) sufficient KT to sacrifice and (2) no need to touch bone. If EITHER fails → wrong procedure. Maps to Coslet Type 1A (lots of KT + bone at normal distance from CEJ). Leaves raw open wound → slow healing → patients unhappy. Fleisher: "very few indications as standalone." Essay 1 Q3

4. Internal Bevel Gingivectomy — incision from gingival margin angling toward bone, thinning tissue. Same tissue reduction as external BUT as part of a FLAP. Wound covered when you close.

5. ENAP — historical only. Attempted to excise pocket lining for new attachment. Didn't work. Know the name exists.

6. Modified Widman Flap — flap for ACCESS to roots without intentionally repositioning tissue or removing bone. Clean roots under direct vision, replace flap original position. More conservative than osseous resective.

7. Osseous Resective Surgery — full combination: flap + debridement + osteoplasty/ostectomy + APF + suturing. The most comprehensive subtractive procedure.

8. Distal Wedge — tissue removal distal to last molar (tuberosity/retromolar pad area).

Why Bone Resorbs Differently by Location

Thick bone → infrabony defects (enough volume for inflammation to carve out a defect while leaving walls). Thin bone → horizontal loss (inflammation dissolves entire thin plate uniformly). Think: thick cliff → cave; thin wall → crumbles.

This is why: buccal bone (thin) → horizontal loss. Interproximal bone (thick) → craters/infrabony defects. Slides: "Most pockets are found interproximally."

Root proximity & marrow space: Where roots diverge = marrow = inflammatory pathway = infrabony defects. Where roots close together = thin cortical = horizontal loss.

Bony Defect Classification: 3-wall (best regen) → 2-wall → 1-wall (worst) → combination (most common)

Why Interproximal Pockets Are Deepest

Buccal/lingual: when attachment lost, gingival margin RECEDES (tissue shrinks back → shallow PD despite attachment loss). Interproximal: papillary tissue does NOT recede as much → deep POCKETING (tissue stays high while bone goes low).

Bone Architecture

Positive architecture (GOAL): Radicular bone APICAL to interdental septum. Natural scallop — higher between teeth, lower on buccal/lingual. More scalloping anteriorly (narrow teeth), flatter posteriorly (wider teeth).

Reverse architecture (PATHOLOGIC): Interproximal bone LOWER than buccal/palatal bone. Normal scallop inverted. Tissue follows this and creates persistent pockets regardless of hygiene.

Flattened architecture: Bone at same level everywhere. Intermediate — no scallop, no reversal.

MASTER PRINCIPLE "Soft tissue does NOT conform to underlying osseous topography." You CANNOT tell what bone looks like by looking at tissue. Tissue drapes over irregular bone like a tablecloth over a bumpy table. Must PROBE to find defects. Radiographs can miss them too (thick buccal plate masks interproximal defects on PA).

CEJ Discrepancy trap: When adjacent teeth have CEJs at different levels (tipping, supereruption), bone between them appears angled on radiograph. Looks like angular bone loss but may be normal. Check CEJ-to-crest distance on EACH tooth — if 1-2mm on both sides, it's just anatomy, not disease.

Bone Sounding

Anesthetize → push probe through tissue to bone. Horizontal probing from facial/lingual = locate crestal walls. Vertical probing = find base of defect. Maps 3D architecture before raising flap.

FLEISHER Does bone sounding with the anesthetic needle during injection — thinner than probe, easier to push through. "A little game I play to see if I can guess what it's gonna look like."

Ostectomy vs Osteoplasty Essay 1 & 3
Ostectomy
Removal of SUPPORTING bone (radicular and interradicular). Eliminates osseous defects. Trade-off: deliberately removing attachment. Used to drop bone level for biologic width or deep infrabony defects.
Osteoplasty
Removal of NON-SUPPORTING bone for reshaping. Thinning ledges, tori, exostoses, shallow craters, blunted interdental craters, small intrabony defects on B/L, enhanced flap placement. NOT removing attachment.

Evidence (Selipsky): Very little supporting bone is removed when resective techniques properly applied. Need for splinting should be determined PRIOR to procedure.

Osseous Resective Therapy

Objectives: Create contours permitting effective plaque control → create bone contours tissue will parallel → permit primary wound closure → expose additional clinical crown for restorative.

Disadvantages: Attachment loss from removing supporting bone, unaesthetic root exposure, hypersensitive roots.

Indications: Adequate remaining attachment after physiologic morphology established, no esthetic/anatomic/sensitivity limitations.

Contraindications: Inadequate remaining attachment, esthetic limitations, anatomic limitations, sensitivity limitations, alternative more effective therapy exists.

FLEISHER NO resective surgery in ANTERIOR region unless full prosthetic reconstruction. Aesthetic result of dropping bone + exposing roots in smile zone = unacceptable. Also: don't remove so much bone that a stable tooth becomes unstable.
Double Parabolization

For multi-rooted teeth — treat each root SEPARATELY (like two premolars). Drop bone on mesial root surface, come BACK UP at furcation, drop again on distal root surface. Each root gets its own parabolic curve with a vertical groove in interdental bone extending apically from the furca. Prevents furcation exposure.

Root trunk: Often narrowest for first molars (shortest distance CEJ → furcation entrance). "The relationship of the defect to the root trunk" matters more than depth in mm for multi-rooted teeth.

Hand instruments for fine-tuning: Ochsenbein chisel (widow's peaks, bony spines), back-action chisel (opposite direction access), Wedelstaedt chisel (incremental removal). Universal curette for small interproximal spicules.

Suturing Techniques Essay 1
1. Simple Interrupted

Enter outer aspect one side → pierce through → exit other side → tie knot. Surgeon's knot: 2 throws one direction (double first throw = friction hold) + 1 throw opposite direction = 3 throws minimum. Advantage: if one suture fails, others hold. "Very straightforward" but not ideal for most perio procedures.

2. Continuous Suture

Fleisher: "I don't like them. I never use them." One failure = entire suture line fails = disaster. Faster to place but all-or-nothing risk.

3. Vertical Mattress — THE Perio Suture

Fleisher: "probably the primary suture technique I'll use, particularly if I am apically positioning a flap."

Technique step-by-step: 1) Enter through buccal flap JUST apical to MGJ (not far into vestibule — prevents tissue bunching/aphthous ulcers). 2) As needle traverses, ROLL it — start perpendicular to bone, rotate to become PARALLEL with bone surface. 3) Grab periosteum (why you did split thickness). 4) Come out through periosteum. 5) Re-enter buccal tissue. 6) Go around to lingual, grab lingual tissue. 7) Come back to buccal. 8) Tie knot WHERE YOU FIRST ENTERED (most apical point, near MGJ).

WHY tie apically: Tying at first (apical) entry pulls tissue DOWN → secures flap in apical position. Tying at crest would pull coronally = defeats purpose.

Needle rolling technique: Use needle curvature — start perpendicular, roll to travel parallel along bone. Catches periosteum WITHOUT hitting bone (blunts needle tip + unnecessary). Center suture under peak of tissue for even tension.

Foundational Studies — Know COLD All Essays
Löe 1965 — Experimental Gingivitis LITERATURE

Design: 12 participants, healthy gingiva. Withdrew all oral hygiene → 10-20 days to develop gingivitis → reinstituted hygiene → 4-10 days to resolve.

PROVED: Plaque accumulation causes gingivitis. 100% developed it. 100% reversed. Universal, guaranteed, reversible.

EXAM TRAP Fleisher literally says: "I don't want anyone to write about how this study showed the relationship between gingivitis and periodontitis. IT DOESN'T." Löe proved plaque → gingivitis. Did NOT prove plaque → periodontitis. Did NOT prove gingivitis → periodontitis. Most gingivitis does NOT progress to periodontitis. Different diseases.
Socransky 1984 — Episodic Breakdown LITERATURE

Design: Longitudinal, observational. 64 patients, 0/3/6 years, NO treatment during entire period.

Old paradigm destroyed: Average annual loss = 0.18mm/site → looks like slow, continuous, progressive disease. WRONG.

What he actually found: Only 12% of sites lost significant attachment (>2mm) during entire 6 years. 88% stable. Of sites losing >2mm in first 3 years, only 40% showed further loss in next 3 years (60% went quiet). ~50% of sites stable in first 3 years showed loss in second 3 years (previously stable sites suddenly active).

Paradigm shift: Periodontitis is EPISODIC, not continuous. Sites break down in BURSTS — multiple mm in short period, then quiet for months/years. Cannot predict which sites will break down next.

Two models: 1) Random burst — breakdown random among susceptible sites, prior history doesn't predict future. 2) Asynchronous multiple burst — clustering of activity in relatively short period, prolonged remission months-years, then infrequent isolated bursts.

Clinical implication: Can't predict breakdown → 3-month recall is the defense. Monitor frequently, disrupt bacteria, catch new breakdown early. Posterior interproximal sites most susceptible.

Eke Prevalence Study (2009-2010) LITERATURE

Evolution of measurement: 1999-2000: 2 sites/tooth, 2 random quads → 19.5%. 2001-2004: 3 sites/tooth, 2 quads → 27%. 2009-2010: 6 sites/tooth, full mouth → 47%. Same population, same classification — only changed HOW THOROUGHLY they looked.

The gotcha: 47% used only 4 interproximal sites (CDC-AAP definition). When ALL 6 sites measured (including mid-B/L), actual prevalence = 56%. This detail was in the article body, NOT the abstract.

Breakdown: 8.7% mild, 30% moderate, 8.5% severe.

Slide MC answer: "What is the prevalence?" — answer is E (none of the above) because actual 6-point number is 56%. But D (all of the above) is also defensible since all three numbers are "correct" for their respective protocols.

Clinical: 1 in 2 adult Americans has periodontitis. If not doing complete 6-site full-mouth assessments, you're missing disease.

2025 Bridge Longevity Study LITERATURE

Design: 94 patients, 111 long-span bridges, average follow-up 17.9 years. Stage III/IV periodontitis. 90 tooth-supported, 21 tooth-implant supported.

Results: 25 bridges: patients dropped out (bridges still working). 18: patients died (still working). 16: failed. 52: still intact — average 20.4 years, range 13-30 years. 76% success rate.

Fleisher's thesis: Good perio surgery + good prosthetics + good maintenance = decades of function, even in severe periodontitis. Don't extract prematurely. Quality of work + patient compliance determines longevity.

Study Age Principle

"A study that's 1 year old can be irrelevant and a study that's 50 years old can be just as relevant today." Quality determines relevance, not date. Löe 1965 never disproven. Gargiulo 1961 still biologic width standard.

GTR / GBR CRAM SHEET — Lectures 4 & 5

Essay Topic 2 focused. All literature citations, slide details, and Fleisher pearls retained. Condensed for rapid review.

1. THE GOAL — Why GTR Exists

Standard perio treatment (SRP, flaps, osseous surgery) almost always produces repair — long junctional epithelium, reduced PD, but NO connective tissue reinsertion into previously diseased cementum. Patient is "healthy but compromised." GTR's goal = take them back to "healthy and uncompromised" — restore what was lost.

Three paradigm shifts each CLAIMED new attachment but couldn't prove it (probes + radiographs can't confirm CT reinsertion):

Era 1 (1890s–1950s): SRP — Younger 1899, McCall 1926, Orban 1948, Waerhaug 1952 → tissue tightening, not true new attachment.
Era 2 (1957–1978): Flaps — Prichard 1957, Rosling 1976, Polson & Heijl 1978.
Era 3 (1965–1981): Osseous grafts — Nabers & O'Leary 1965, Schallhorn 1977, Dragoo 1981.

Pattern follows Thomas Kuhn's "Structure of Scientific Revolutions" — shared beliefs → experiments → challenge → paradigm shift → repeat.

4 Ways to Assess New Attachment

Probe (unreliable — tissue tightening mimics it) → Radiograph (shows bone, not CT) → Re-entry (see bone directly, still can't confirm CT) → Histology = ONLY definitive method.

2. The Four Terms — Know These COLD

ESSAY Q1MCQ
REATTACHMENT = reconnecting surgically separated healthy tissue. Routine in every flap surgery at the edges beyond the defect. Not exciting — was never diseased.
REPAIR = long junctional epithelium (LJE). Body's default healing. Epithelium migrates down root surface, sticks like tape. Looks healthy clinically but NO CT fibers inserting. Actually protective — prevents worse outcome of gingival CT causing resorption.
NEW ATTACHMENT = CT fibers inserting into previously diseased cementum. The real deal. What GTR aims for.
REGENERATION = new attachment + complete restoration of ALL lost structures — new cementum, new PDL fibers, AND new bone. First described by Melcher 1969. The ultimate dream.
Mnemonic: RE-RE-NE-RE → REattach (healthy reconnect) → REpair (LJE default) → NEw Attachment (CT into diseased cementum) → REgeneration (full restoration). "Repair is the body's default. Regeneration is the dream. GTR overrides the default to achieve the dream."

3. The Four Cell Types & "The Race"

ESSAY Q2a

After surgery, 4 cell types race to the root surface. Whichever arrives first determines the healing outcome.

"Every Girl Plays Ball" — Epithelium (fastest, repair) → Gingival CT (fast, resorption) → PDL (moderate, new attachment) → Bone (slowest, ankylosis). "The fastest runner gives the worst prize."

Key insight: PDL cells and gingival CT cells migrate at roughly the same rate but are embryologically DIFFERENT — produce completely different outcomes. Epithelium winning by default is actually protective because it prevents gingival CT resorption.

GTR DEFINITION: Allowing certain cell types to participate in wound healing while excluding others, for the purpose of promoting new attachment. Physically block fast cells (epithelium + gingival CT) with a membrane so PDL cells win the race.

GTR: The cell race problem → membrane blocks fast cells → PDL cells win → true regeneration

4. The Landmark Studies — Karring, Nyman & Lindhe

ESSAY Q2

Know these names cold: Karring, Nyman, and Lindhe are primary authors on ALL of these.

Karring, Nyman & Lindhe 1980 J Clin Perio 7: 96-105

Method: 3 beagle dogs. Periodontitis induced to half root length. Roots notched, extracted, implanted into holes drilled in bone. Only bone cells could contact diseased upper portion (epithelium + gingival CT excluded).

Result: Diseased area (bone only) → no new attachment, root resorption + ankylosis. Apical area (PDL present) → attachment occurred.

Proved: Bone cells alone CANNOT produce new attachment.

Nyman et al. 1980 J Clin Perio 7: 39

Method: 2 monkeys, 28 teeth. Roots placed in grooves — one side facing bone, other side facing gingival CT.

Result: Side facing gingival CT → root resorption. Side facing bone → ankylosis.

Proved: Gingival CT causes root resorption on contact with root surface.

Nyman, Karring & Lindhe 1982 J Clin Perio 9: 257 ⭐ THE BREAKTHROUGH

Method: 3 monkeys. Removed buccal + approximal bone from mid-root to 2mm apical of marginal crest. Curetted roots. Placed millipore filter over defects on experimental teeth → blocked epithelium + gingival CT. Only PDL + bone could reach root. PDL faster → PDL dominated.

Result: New cementum with inserting collagen fibers on curetted root surface. TRUE NEW ATTACHMENT — confirmed histologically. Controls (no filter) → LJE (repair only).

Proved: PDL cells possess the ability to reestablish CT attachment. The barrier concept works.

Origin story: Nyman improvised mid-surgery in a chemistry lab, found a millipore filter in a drawer, cut it to fit. Entire field of GTR started from rummaging through a drawer.

Nyman et al. 1986 J Clin Perio 13: 145

Method: 4 monkeys. Angular defects to mid-root. Removed PDL + cementum. Elastic placed TIGHTLY (blocking coronal PDL migration) vs LOOSELY (allowing it). All covered with laterally displaced flaps.

Proved: PDL cells MUST migrate coronally along root for new attachment to form. Blocking migration = no new attachment.

5. Goldman Dry Ice Case — The Clinical "Aha" Moment

18-year-old, dry ice stuck to oral tissue → ripped off soft tissue → exposed vital bone on facial. Goldman did nothing (secondary intention healing). At 1 week: healing was NOT uniform — most robust tissue formation concentrated at the level of the PDL. Rest of bone surface healed slower/differently. This observation = birth of GTR concept: different cell types produce different healing at different speeds. Can we CONTROL which cells participate?

6. Gore-Tex Membrane — The First Clinical Barrier

ESSAY Q8

Material: PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) = medical grade stretched Teflon. Inert. Previously used in vascular surgery patches + artificial ligaments. No longer made by Gore — current brand = Cytoplast.

Two Structural Zones

Top (coronal): Open microstructure — stretched, porous. Designed to let flap tissue grow into it (prevent recession). Good idea, didn't work well in practice — still got recession. Trim from sides/bottom, preserve this portion.

Bottom (body): Occlusive membrane — dense, no cell penetration. Does the actual GTR work of blocking epithelium + gingival CT.

Animal Experiments Confirmed It

Control (no Gore-Tex) → epithelium migrated down root → LJE → repair only. Gore-Tex site → CT fibers inserting all the way up to CEJ → true new attachment confirmed histologically.

Ideal Barrier Material Properties (from slides)

Withstand cellular ingrowth • Nutrient/fluid diffusible • Excellent handling • Minimal tissue reaction • No allergic properties • Resist infection • Eventual absorption after healing. Gore-Tex meets all EXCEPT the last one (non-resorbable).

7. GTR Surgical Technique — 8 Steps

Directly on slides
  1. Incisions: Intrasulcular (NOT internal bevel — preserve MAX tissue + all KT). Generous vertical releases mesial to defect. Preserve interdental papilla. Excise pocket epithelium from inner flap.
  2. Flap reflection: Full thickness mucoperiosteal. Reflect PAST defect so bone exposed beyond defect margins (membrane needs 2-3mm overhang onto healthy bone).
  3. Debridement: Remove ALL granulation tissue. Plane roots to smooth, glassy finish. Thin tapered diamonds where hand instruments can't reach.
  4. Membrane shaping: Trim apical + lateral borders ONLY (preserve open microstructure). Must cover ENTIRE defect + extend 2-3mm apically beyond it.
  5. Suture membrane: Suture directly around tooth (Gore-Tex or Dexon suture). Snug but NOT so tight edges curl (curling → perforation). First round of suturing.
  6. Flap closure: Second round of suturing. Membrane COMPLETELY covered, 2-3mm below coronal flap margin. Often requires coronally advancing ("hiking") the flap. Interproximal first, verticals last.
  7. Post-op: CHX rinse daily. Follow up 2 weeks + 6 weeks. Membrane stays 4–6 weeks (up to 3 months if fully covered).
  8. Membrane removal: Anesthetize. Gentle tug for occlusive portion. Small incision if open microstructure has tissue ingrowth.

9. Clinical Pearls from Cases

Tissue "bubbling up" coronal to outer flap at membrane removal = proof PDL cells won the race. Membrane blocked epithelium + gingival CT. Bone cells too slow for 4-6 weeks. Can ONLY be PDL-origin tissue.

American vs Swedish approach: Swedes were purists (bone against root = bad per their studies → no graft). Americans combined bone graft WITH membrane. Works clinically because freeze-dried bone is NOT active living bone — it's a slow scaffold. PDL cells establish attachment on root surface BEFORE graft can cause resorption.

Managing expectations: Under-promise, over-deliver. Tell patient realistic risks BEFORE procedure. "I think this tooth is probably a loser but we can try" → patient thrilled if it works. "This will definitely work" → patient furious if it doesn't.

11. Bony Defect Classification

Most real defects are combination defects — character changes apical to coronal. More walls = more predictable regeneration.

3-wall (bone on mesial, distal, lingual; root = 4th wall) → most predictable, best blood supply from multiple directions.
2-wall (one bony wall lost) → intermediate.
1-wall (only hemiseptum on adjacent tooth) → least predictable, minimal containment + one-directional blood supply.

12. Grafting Materials — Complete Classification

ESSAY Q3

Source Classification (THE BIG FOUR)

Autograft = same individual (you are donor AND recipient). Intraoral (tuberosity, extraction site) or extraoral (iliac crest).
Allograft = same species, genetically dissimilar individuals (human → human, not identical twins). FDBA, DFDBA.
Xenograft = different species (bovine → human). Bio-Oss.
Alloplast = synthetic / inert foreign body. HA, TCP, bioactive glass.

Osteoconductive vs Osteoinductive HIGH YIELD

Osteoconductive = scaffold. Facilitates new bone from EXISTING bone. Trellis for a vine — provides structure for bone cells to grow along. Most grafts are at least this.
Osteoinductive = INDUCES new bone from undifferentiated cells or non-osteocytes. Recruits + transforms cells into bone-forming cells. Way more powerful.

How to test osteoinductivity: The muscle pouch experiment. Place graft in MUSCLE (zero osteocytes present). Wait months. If bone forms → osteoinductive. If not → just osteoconductive. Eliminates confounding variable of surrounding bone.

13. Key Literature for Grafting

Urist 1965 Science — Demineralized lyophilized bone induced new bone in rabbit muscle pouches. Discovered BMPs = the osteoinductive factor. Coined "Bone Morphogenetic Protein" in J Dent Res 1971.

Schwartz et al. 1996 — Tested 14 batches DFDBA from 6 bone banks. 3 of 6 banks → NO osteoinductive potential. Different lots from SAME bank varied. DFDBA osteoinductivity is unreliable.

Schallhorn et al. — Showed iliac bone + marrow autografts effective BUT caused root resorption. Freezing harvested bone controls resorption. Second surgical site = major disadvantage.

14. Vital Bone Graft Specifics

Iliac crest: Potent but causes root resorption (too bioactive). Controlled by freezing. Rarely used in perio now — too invasive.

Intraoral (tuberosity, extraction sites): Do NOT cause same resorptive problems as iliac. Limited volume. Cancellous > cortical for osteogenic potential because hematopoietic marrow.

Osteoid bone harvest trick: Extract tooth → let socket partially heal 2 weeks → harvest osteoid (newly forming) bone from socket. Loaded with active bone-forming cells. Strategic extraction timing creates harvest site for future grafting.

Vital cancellous bone may contain PDL cells if from recent extraction site → adds regenerative potential.

Cryopreserved (vital allograft): Not used in dentistry. Know definition only.

15. Non-Osseous Grafting Materials & Regenerative Enhancers

ESSAY Q9

GEM 21 (PDGF) — Fleisher's Favorite ⭐

What it is: Completely synthetic. Combines rhPDGF-BB (recombinant human platelet-derived growth factor) + β-TCP (beta tricalcium phosphate = osteoconductive carrier).
Mechanism (4 steps from slides):
1. PDGF released from β-TCP matrix → 2. Binds specific cell surface receptors → initiates intracellular signaling cascades → 3. Chemotaxis (directed cell migration) + mitogenesis (cell proliferation) of osteoblasts, PDL fibroblasts, AND cementoblasts → 4. Increased matrix synthesis → new alveolar bone + new PDL + new cementum. Angiogenesis continues.
Clinical results: Consistent, excellent. ALL of Fleisher's impressive GTR cases used GEM 21. Minimal complications.
Key advantage over PRP: Delivers a SPECIFIC, purified, genetically engineered growth factor at known concentration — targeted + reliable. PRP just concentrates whatever's in your platelets.

EMD — Emdogain (Enamel Matrix Derivative)

Source: Porcine (pig) tooth buds.
Contains: Amelogenins, ameloblastin, enamelin (low molecular weight proteins).
Mechanism: PDL cells exhibit enhanced protein production + cell proliferation in response to EMD. May mimic developmental cementogenesis (enamel matrix proteins play a role in natural cementum formation).
Fleisher's take: Skeptical mechanistically ("squirting gel from pig tooth buds?") but accepts the evidence: "the research shows it works pretty well."

PRP — Platelet Rich Plasma

How: Draw patient's blood → centrifuge → concentrate platelets at bottom → pipette out concentrate.
Growth factors: PDGF, TGF-β, EGF/TGF-alpha, IGF-1. Concentrates platelets 330–900%.
Theory: Amplify growth factor signaling at wound site. Combine PRP gel + bone graft → scaffold + signal.
Reality: Research DIDN'T bear it out. No increase in total bone formation. Faster early healing + better soft tissue closure, but NOT more bone. Logistics (phlebotomist, centrifuge, cost) may not justify marginal benefit.
Still useful as: Wound healing agent (excellent at maintaining flap closure in gaps), soft tissue management.
PRF (Platelet Rich Fibrin): Newer variation, supposedly more bioactive. "Sticky bone" = graft mixed with PRF.

BMP — Bone Morphogenic Proteins (Infuse/rhBMP-2)

What: Group of regulatory glycoproteins that stimulate differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts. Research focused on BMP-2, BMP-3 (osteogenin), BMP-7 (OP1).
Results: Most potent osteoinductive agent available. Dramatic ridge augmentation.
BUT — risk of "exuberant inflammatory response" (patient "blows up like a balloon"). Expensive. Dose-sensitive (too much = complications).
Fleisher sent his free Infuse samples BACK unused — GEM 21 already working great without the risks. "If what you're using works well, there's not much incentive to jump to a new product."

Alloplasts (Non-Osseous)

Calcium sulfate (plaster of Paris), calcium ceramics, bioactive glass polymers, HA, TCP. Inert biologic fillers. Osteoconductive only. "Unlikely to result in true regeneration" (directly on slide). Bioactive glass is NOT bioactive despite the name.

17. Sinus Lift Procedure

  1. Incision + vertical release on lateral maxillary wall
  2. Reflect flap → expose lateral sinus wall
  3. Create window in lateral wall (handpiece initially, then piezo for safety)
  4. Push Schneiderian membrane inward/upward to create space
  5. Check for tears — patient holds nose + breathes; membrane moves like breathing. Perforation = air bubbles/no movement. Repair tears with membrane.
  6. Pack xenograft (Bio-Oss) into space
  7. Place membrane over window → tack in place → suture closed

Bio-Oss processing: Treated at high temps >15 hours + several hours cleaning with strong alkaline solutions → highly purified anorganic natural bone mineral. Prion-free tissue (unlike brain/intestine/spinal cord from cattle).

Posterior Superior Alveolar Artery (branch of maxillary artery) can run through sinus window area. Three possible positions on CBCT: intrasinusal (below membrane), intraosseous (in the bone), superficial (outer cortex). Use CBCT to plan window placement. If severed → significant bleeding, crush bone edges to coagulate. Overall hemorrhage risk is RARE (terminal branches of peripheral vessels).

18. Socket Preservation

Fleisher's rule: "If you don't have the materials to regenerate this area, you have NO BUSINESS taking this tooth out." Extraction without grafting when buccal plate is lost = massive soft tissue collapse = aesthetic disaster = much harder to fix later.

  1. Extract tooth
  2. Place membrane on facial as a "retaining wall" (replaces missing buccal plate)
  3. Tack membrane in place
  4. Fill with bone graft material
  5. Coronally position flap for PRIMARY CLOSURE (vertical incisions past MGJ). Tension-free.

"Collagen plug and a prayer" does NOT work when buccal plate is lost. Fleisher is emphatic about this.

If you can't graft → don't extract. Refer instead. Worst outcome = GP extracts, sends to specialist → tissue already collapsed, ridge resorbed, reconstruction nightmare.

19. Fleisher's Clinical Philosophy (Essay Gold)

  • Give it everything on first attempt — graft + membrane + GEM 21. "I don't know that I need it, but I don't want to find out after the fact."
  • Going back around a tooth = destroys some new attachment gained (second flap). Usually satisfied with first result. Going back in edentulous area (GBR) = no attachment to lose, readily does second procedure.
  • Primary closure is CRITICAL — especially GBR (longer healing, exposure = no bone at that site).
  • Antibiotics: Active infection = yes. GBR procedures = yes. Osseous surgery = usually. Crown lengthening/CTG = no.
  • Hard tissue > soft tissue — bone doesn't shrink. CT grafts can shrink. Rebuild with bone first; CT graft only if needed for buccal contour.
  • Don't chase new products — need 10-15 years of data, not 5-6 months. If what you have works, stick with it.
  • Membrane without graft? Theoretically yes (blood clot + bone cells), but graft maintains membrane position, stabilizes clot, provides scaffold. "Why not use it?"
  • Success = minimal PD (2-4mm), not just radiographic appearance.

20. The Pseudopocket Callback

Fleisher ends GTR Part 1 by asking: "How do you tell a pocket from a pseudopocket?" Same answer since day one: find the CEJ, measure gingival margin relative to it. Not radiographs. Not probing depth alone. GM coronal to CEJ = possible pseudopocket. Must know the CAL formula: CAL = PD ± recession. Negative recession (GM coronal to CEJ) means you subtract.

The Prophy-Before-SRP Error

Fleisher was emphatic: doing a D1110 Prophy before SRP is "all wrong."

Why it's dangerous: Prophy removes only superficial calculus → marginal gingiva heals/tightens → traps deep subgingival pathogens + calculus → acute periodontal abscess risk.

Why it's unethical: Supragingival scaling + polishing are already part of SRP. Adding a preliminary prophy = unnecessary 5th visit + extra charge for a biologically redundant service.

Two Narrow Exceptions (Not Billed Separately)

1) Massive supra calculus — quick 5-min Piezo/Cavitron to improve visibility. 2) Excessive inflammation — brief superficial scaling to reduce bleeding before definitive SRP at next visit. Both are considered part of SRP, not billed as D1110.

BU clinic rule: Must use hand instruments (curettes) for SRP. Piezo/ultrasonic is adjunctive only. "Prophy part" = prophy cup + paste, NOT the piezo.

Correct Sequencing: Mixed SRP/Prophy Cases

D0180 comprehensive charting → identify which quads need SRP vs prophy.
SRP (D4341/D4342) on diseased quadrants FIRST — treats active disease.
D1110 Prophy on healthy/gingivitis quadrants LAST.
Fleisher preference: during final D1110, reclean previously SRP'd quads to same standard → uniform clean mouth before re-eval.

Gingivitis Case Diagnosis

Periodontitis diagnosis is based SOLELY on PD associated with CAL. Presence of calculus is "completely divorced" from SRP diagnosis. Heavy calculus + PDs ≤3mm + no CAL = Gingivitis → tx = D1110, NOT SRP.

Re-eval After Prophy?

Periodontitis (post-SRP): Mandatory 4–6 week re-eval. Gingivitis (post-prophy): Generally NOT needed; resolution assessed at next 6-month recall.

Exception — Atypical Gingivitis: Profuse/disproportionate bleeding despite minimal local factors → suspect systemic cause (uncontrolled DM, leukemia, bleeding disorders, HIV). Clean first → re-eval 2–4 weeks → if no improvement → physician referral. Based on clinical judgment, not a specific number.

Recall Charting

Full 360° probing required at recall. Acceptable to record only PDs ≥4mm (or changes from baseline), assuming stable 2–3mm depths are unchanged. Even ≤15% BOP = "pretty well" → reinforce OHI.

2. EVIDENCE & REALISTIC GOALS OF SRP

BOP Predictive Value

Lang et al. (1986) — BOP+ is NOT a reliable predictor of future breakdown (supports Socransky's random burst theory). BOP– IS a somewhat reliable indicator of stability/health.

Clinical application: Can't use BOP+ to predict which sites will worsen. Can use BOP– to reassure patient of stability.

Smoking exception: Nicotine vasoconstriction → fibrotic tissue → false BOP– even with active disease. BOP is less reliable in smokers.

Subgingival Microflora & Maintenance Interval

Harper & Robinson (1987) — SRP reduces motile microbes/spirochetes, but effect is temporary. Pathogenic flora will recolonize.

Clinical rationale for D4910 (Perio Maintenance): 3-month intervals are mandatory to disrupt maturing biofilm before it causes further CAL.

Essay Q3 (Non-Surgical Therapy): Lang 1986 + Harper & Robinson 1987 = your two go-to references for goals/evidence supporting SRP + why 3-month recall is needed.

Physical Limitations of SRP

Caffesse et al. (1986) — Surgical verification showed in pockets ≥5mm, clinicians inadequately debrided roots 65% of the time. Residual calculus found on root.

SRP is performed "blindfolded" by tactile sense. Complete removal is "rarely attained and somewhat unrealistic." Goal is NOT mechanical perfection — it's creating a surface sufficiently clean for tissue healing.

SRP as Initial vs. Definitive Therapy

Greenstein (1992) — SRP is both a definitive approach for mild-moderate perio AND initial phase before surgery for severe cases. Classification determined retrospectively at re-eval.

Mild-Moderate (5–6mm): SRP may be definitive if pockets resolve. Severe (8–9mm): SRP = Phase I to reduce inflammation before Phase II surgery. The 4–6 week re-eval is the moment you determine which it was.

Critical Probing Depth (CPD)

≈4.0–5.5mm threshold. Below CPD → better outcomes with SRP. Above CPD → better outcomes with surgery. At ~4.2mm, Widman flap surgery showed more CAL gain than SRP alone.

Components of Initial Therapy (Phase I)

Plaque control (OHI) → SRP + eliminate local factors (overhangs, defective restorations) → Antimicrobial/chemotherapeutic adjuncts (CHX, local antibiotics).

Hygienist Time

30-min appointment is "completely inadequate." Adult hygiene visit requires ≥1 hour (realistically 50 min clinical time).

Anesthesia: Overlapping Blanching Technique

Target = soft tissue only (not bone/pulp). Goal = comfort + hemostasis (blanching). Agents: Mepivacaine 3% (no epi) first → Lidocaine 2% w/ 1:100,000 Epi for working area.

Palatal: bend needle slightly → insert into mid-IP area on palate (overlapping B/L injection). Avoids "killer" direct palatal injection.
"Walk it out" from IP to line angle on palate, overlapping blanched areas.

NOT an antibiotic. 20mg = submicrobial. Does NOT kill bacteria. Mechanism: inhibits MMPs (Matrix Metalloproteinases) → stops enzymatic breakdown of connective tissue → prevents future attachment loss. Based on Golub's 1980s discovery.

Periodontal Breakdown Cascade

Bacterial products (LPS) → Host cells (neutrophils, macrophages) → Release PGE₂, IL-1β, IL-6, MMPs → Osteoclast activity + CT breakdown → Bone resorption → Pocket formation, CAL, mobility. Periostat blocks at the MMP step.

Misleading Marketing Data

Claim: 33–52% greater attachment gain with SDD vs. placebo (9 months, ≥7mm pockets). Reality: absolute difference = 0.38mm (1.55mm vs 1.17mm). Clinically irrelevant. True purpose = prevent future loss, not induce gain. FDA required gain metric for approval.

Patient Selection

Indicated: Aggressive perio, smokers, diabetics, immunocompromised, refractory patients (disease progresses despite optimal mechanical tx). Contraindicated: ANUG/ANUP — acute spirochetal infection requiring a "real antibiotic" (Metronidazole/Penicillin).

Pathogenesis Model

Health: Natural Enzyme Inhibitors > Tissue Destructive Enzymes. Disease: Risk factors (smoking, DM) → MMPs >> Natural Inhibitors → CT lysis + bone resorption.

Smoking stats: 53% of US periodontitis attributable to smoking (42% current, 11% former). 4× risk current smokers; 6× heavy (≥31/day). After ≥11 years cessation → risk = non-smoker.

Mobility Tipping Point: Not linear with attachment loss. A tooth with 50% bone loss can go from no mobility to severe mobility with very little additional loss. MUST warn new patients of this risk — or they'll blame YOU.

Flap Design Rules

Mobility: Vertical releases MUST extend beyond MGJ into alveolar mucosa (elastic tissue = freedom of movement). Blood supply: Base (apical) of flap must ALWAYS be wider than coronal aspect. Blood flows from base; wide base prevents ischemia/necrosis.

APF and Keratinized Tissue

APF preserves KT by moving existing band apically. Gingivectomy wastes KT (removes it). APF is preferred when KT is limited.

The Palatal Exception: APF is IMPOSSIBLE on the palate. No alveolar mucosa, no MGJ, no elastic tissue to release. Solution = submarginal incision (internal gingivectomy) to excise the pocket wall.

Why Most Pockets Are Interproximal

Facial/Lingual: Thin bone → inflammation → recession → pocket "eliminates itself." Interproximal: Papilla is "peaked" + supported by adjacent teeth → doesn't recede → tissue bridges over while bone is destroyed → deep pocket + interdental crater (2-wall defect).

Q3 Gingivectomy vs. Flap + bone: Gingivectomy alone = sufficient KT + no bone involvement + pseudopockets. If margin needs bone removal to establish BW → must do flap + ostectomy.
Q4 Healing time: 6–8 weeks minimum (up to 12 weeks for complete maturation). Also: loss of interproximal papilla → "black triangles" → prosthetic management.
ESSAY 2 — GTR/GBR
Q1 Define GTR: Allowing certain cell types to participate in healing while excluding others, for the purpose of promoting new attachment. Uses barrier membrane to exclude epithelium and gingival CT from root surface → allows PDL cells to repopulate → true regeneration (new cementum + PDL + bone).
Q2 Landmark Studies:
• Karring, Nyman, Lindhe (J Clin Perio, 1980) — Perio-affected roots implanted into bone. Epithelium excluded. Result: root resorption + ankylosis (bone directly contacts root). Conclusion: bone cells do NOT produce new attachment.
• Karring, Nyman, Lindhe (J Clin Perio, 1980) — Perio-affected roots implanted into gingival CT. Result: resorption. Conclusion: gingival CT does NOT produce new attachment.
• Nyman, Karring, Lindhe (J Clin Perio, 1982) — Millipore filter placed over debrided root after bone removal. Result: new cementum with inserting collagen fibers observed. Conclusion: PDL cells possess the ability to re-establish CT attachment.
• Nyman, Gottlow, Karring, Lindhe (J Clin Perio, 1986) — Elastic placed tightly vs. loosely around roots. Proved significance of coronal tissue growth for new attachment.
• Gore-Tex (PTFE) studies — Clinical/histologic proof that barrier membrane achieves regeneration in humans.
Q2a Four tissue interactions with root surface:
• Epithelium → long junctional epithelium (repair, NOT regeneration). Fastest-growing tissue — wins the race without a barrier.
• Gingival CT → resorption + ankylosis. Does NOT form new attachment.
• Bone → root resorption + ankylosis. Does NOT form new attachment.
• PDL → TRUE REGENERATION — new cementum with inserting collagen fibers. Only cell type that produces new attachment.
Q3 Grafting Materials:
• Autograft (vital, same individual): Intraoral (osseous coagulum, tuberosity) or Extraoral (iliac crest). Best osteogenic potential (vital cells). Disadvantage: 2nd surgical site (iliac); iliac → root resorption risk (controlled by freezing). Intraoral avoids resorption issues. Cancellous > cortical for osteogenic potential.
• Allograft (same species, different individual): Vital (cryopreserved iliac) or Non-vital. DFDBA (Demineralized Freeze-Dried Bone) — potentially osteoinductive via exposed BMPs (Urist, 1965). BUT: Schwartz et al. (1996) showed 3/6 bone banks had NO osteoinductive potential; different lots from same bank varied. FDBA (Mineralized) — osteoconductive scaffold.
• Xenograft (different species): Bio-Oss (anorganic bovine bone) — osteoconductive scaffold. Prion-free (bone classified as prion-free tissue). Processed at high temps >15 hrs + alkaline cleaning.
• Alloplast (synthetic): Calcium sulfate, HA, TCP, bioactive glass. Inert fillers. Unlikely to result in true regeneration.
• Osteoconductive = scaffold for new bone growth from existing bone. Osteoinductive = induces new bone from undifferentiated cells.
Non-osseous/Biologics: EMD (Emdogain — porcine enamel matrix, enhances PDL cell proliferation), PRP (platelet concentrates 330–900%, source of PDGF/TGF-β/EGF), GEM 21S (rhPDGF-BB + β-TCP — chemotaxis + mitogenesis of osteoblasts/PDL fibroblasts/cementoblasts → new bone/PDL/cementum + angiogenesis), BMPs (BMP-2, BMP-3, BMP-7 — stimulate mesenchymal stem cells → osteoblasts).
Membranes: PTFE (Gore-Tex, non-resorbable — gold standard but requires 2nd surgery for removal, risk of exposure), Titanium-reinforced PTFE (space-making), Resorbable membranes (collagen-based — no 2nd surgery but less predictable space maintenance). Requirements: biocompatible, occlusive, space-making, tissue integration, clinical manageability.
GTR vs. GBR: GTR = regeneration of periodontal attachment (cementum + PDL + bone) around teeth. GBR = regeneration of bone volume (implant sites, extraction sockets, ridge augmentation). Conceptually different targets.
ESSAY 3 — NON-SURGICAL/SURGICAL PERIODONTAL THERAPY
Q1 Goals/Evidence for SRP: Reduce pathogens (Harper & Robinson 1987), shift microflora to health-associated. BOP– = stability indicator (Lang et al. 1986). Reduce PD, achieve CAL stability. SRP = definitive for mild-moderate perio (Greenstein 1992).
Q2 Limitations → escalate to surgery: Caffesse et al. (1986) — 65% inadequate debridement in ≥5mm pockets. CPD ≈4.2mm — above this, surgery yields more CAL gain. Non-responsive sites at re-eval (persistent PD + BOP after SRP + adjuncts) → surgical intervention.
Q3 Clinical scenario (75yo, 5–7mm PDs, minimal recession, GM 1–2mm coronal to CEJ): Diagnosis = periodontitis (PDs + CAL present). Tx = comprehensive SRP (4 quads D4341), OHI, re-eval 4–6 weeks. At re-eval: responsive sites → D4910 q3 months. Non-responsive → consider surgery or LAAs. For a 30-year-old: same basic tx plan BUT Grade may be higher (C vs B) given severity at young age. More aggressive follow-up. Consider systemic adjuncts if aggressive pattern. May be more willing to pursue surgical correction for long-term stability.
Q4 Adjunctive therapies: CHX rinse (substantivity, but SLS interaction), Listerine (essential oils, may be superior for inflammation — Axelsson & Lindhe), Periostat (20mg Doxy BID — MMP inhibitor, NOT antibiotic, for high-risk patients), LAAs (Arestin, Atridox, PerioChip — at re-eval for non-responsive sites), SnF₂ toothpaste (anti-plaque, anti-gingivitis, anti-sensitivity), systemic antibiotics for aggressive/acute infections (Metronidazole, Amoxicillin, Azithromycin).
Q5 Patient compliance: Nyman 1977 — surgery fails without recall. Rosling 1976 — success depends on timely recall. Isidor & Karring 1986 — professional scaling q3 months compensates for variable home care. Axelsson & Lindhe 1981 — recall group maintained 99% stability over 6 years. Surgery creates maintainable architecture; does NOT cure perio. Without 3-month recall + home care = "waste of time."
ESSAY 4 — OCCLUSAL TRAUMA
Q1 Definition: Pathologic or adaptive changes in the periodontium from undue force by masticatory muscles (AAP Glossary 2001). The lesion = loss of alveolar/crestal lamina dura → widened PDL → clinically appears as increasing mobility over time. Diagnosis requires increasing mobility — NOT just increased mobility.
Q2 Primary vs. Secondary:
• Primary: Excessive force on NORMAL support. Reversible/repairable. Signs: increasing mobility + migration + fremitus + widened PDL on radiograph. Tx: reduce force (occlusal adjustment/equilibration). Once force reduced → PDL returns to pre-force width → mobility decreases.
• Secondary: Normal force on REDUCED (diseased) support. Usually NOT reversible. Must have attachment loss. Tx: splinting (compensates for lost support). Force itself isn't excessive — the periodontium can't handle normal forces.
• You can have primary occlusal trauma even WITH attachment loss (if excessive force is the issue).
Q3 Does occlusal trauma cause attachment loss? NO. Occlusal trauma does NOT initiate attachment loss, pocketing, or recession. Effects are limited to the attachment apparatus (PDL, lamina dura) — supracrestal soft tissues (CT attachment, JE) are NOT damaged by force. Co-destructive: Heavy force + active periodontal infection = can accelerate the rate of breakdown (but force alone doesn't initiate it). Animal studies: occlusal trauma on healthy but reduced periodontium has NO different effect than on normal periodontium (orthodontic studies confirm this). Reinhart, Pao, Krejeci (J Perio Res, 1984) — PDL stresses in initiation of occlusal traumatism.
Q4 Treatment: Primary → occlusal equilibration, limited/complete ortho, prosthetics. Secondary → splinting (compensates for lost support). Also address any coexisting periodontitis (SRP, surgery).
Q5 Mobility vs. Fremitus: Mobility = physical displacement of tooth when force applied by instruments (Grade 0–3: physiologic ≤0.2mm; Grade 1 = 0.2–1.0mm; Grade 2 = 1.0–2.0mm; Grade 3 = >2.0mm and/or vertical). Fremitus = vibration/movement felt when patient occludes/taps teeth together (functional test — detects trauma during function). Mobility is NOT disease. Increased but NOT increasing mobility = adapted, stable → acceptable if tooth doesn't disturb masticatory function or comfort. Tx needed only when mobility is increasing over time (occlusal trauma) or compromises function/comfort. Coolidge (JADA, 1930) — normal PDL width varies by age and location (crest: 0.17–0.23mm; mid-root: 0.12–0.17mm; apical: 0.16–0.24mm).

Light vs. Heavy Force

Light (adaptive): Constant force within adaptive range → PDL widens, heals, mobility increases but stabilizes (not increasing). Remove force → PDL returns to pre-force width → mobility normalizes.

Heavy (non-adaptive): Exceeds adaptive capacity → mobility continues increasing, never adapts.

Pathologic tooth wear ≠ occlusal trauma. Wear may occur but mobility may or may not be present. Extent of wear is not directly related to extent of PDL lesion.

Posterior Bite Collapse: Syndrome (not disease entity). Missing teeth → super-eruption → loss of contact/drifting → loss of posterior support → anterior flaring. Sequence of clinical findings, not a single diagnosis.

DETERMINING NECESSITY — Essay Q2 (Process of Elimination)

ESSAY "When removing a temp, you see bleeding and ulceration. How do you determine if CL is necessary or another problem exists?"

Other Causes of Bleeding/Ulceration (NOT biologic width)

  1. Excess subgingival cement — remove temp, clean out ALL cement, inspect
  2. Overextended/ill-fitting temp margins — evaluate margins, remake if too long
  3. Poor patient hygiene — re-instruct, give time to improve
  4. Rough/irregular temp surface — causes plaque retention

Fleisher's Diagnostic Process

  1. Rule out cement → clean everything
  2. Rule out temp margins → evaluate/remake
  3. Rule out hygiene → reinforce instructions
  4. Have patient RETURN → remove temp → reassess tissue
  5. If ALL variables eliminated and tissue STILL inflamed → biologic width violation CONFIRMED
THE RADIOGRAPH BOMB: Fleisher says measuring margin-to-bone distance on X-ray to diagnose BW invasion is WRONG. If the X-ray shows <2 mm but tissue looks PERFECT (pink, no bleeding) → BW has NOT been violated for THIS tooth. The Gargiulo numbers are averages. Clinical picture trumps radiographic measurement. Exception: if margin is literally AT the osseous crest → CL definitely needed. Same principle as "you can't determine attachment loss from a radiograph" (Dx lecture).
Temp quality matters: Bad temps with overextended margins create inflammation that gets misdiagnosed as BW violation → unnecessary CL. Spend as much time on the temp as the prep.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE — Step-by-Step

  1. Assess KT FIRST — determines entire incision design:
    • Enough KT → submarginal/internal bevel incision (sacrifice some KT)
    • Not enough KT → crevicular/sulcular incision (preserve every mm)
  2. Raise split-thickness flap — leaves periosteum on bone (needed for suturing + allows apical repositioning). Reflect to ~MGJ. Vertical incision for mobility (base wider than gingival margin).
  3. Distal wedge + debridement as needed. Clean granulation tissue.
  4. Refine contours — gingivectomy of interproximal tissue if transition isn't smooth.
  5. Osseous reduction — remove bone to achieve 3–4 mm from prep margin to crest. Diamond burs + hand instruments.
  6. Remove Widow's Peaks — bony spines at transitional line angles. Removed with Ochsenbein chisels. If left → tissue follows bone → bounces up coronally at peak → creates pocketing. "Tissue cannot conform to abrupt architectural changes in bone."
  7. Suture apically — enter through KT just coronal to MGJ → pass through periosteum (still attached to bone) → out → rotate around → buccal papilla → wrap lingual. Tie knot apically and push DOWN to secure flap in apical position. Tying from above pulls tissue coronally = defeats purpose.
WHY tooth MUST be prepped + temped BEFORE CL: (1) Visualization — with temp off, Fleisher sees 360° around tooth. (2) Precision — bone removal based on WHERE the prep margin ends. Without a prep, he's guessing. Yes, the restorative dentist re-preps after healing. Imprecise bone removal is worse.

Concept: Instead of extending crown prep past existing subgingival restoration, LEAVE the old restoration and build up the deep margin with composite to a more accessible coronal position. Crown seats on elevated margin.

Fleisher's take: "CL is not just for crowns — it's for any restorative material that impinges on BW." If the DME composite itself invades BW → traded one problem for another → still need CL. Narrow scenario where DME works: existing restoration JUST far enough from bone that it doesn't violate BW, but extending a crown prep apical to it WOULD violate it. "Most situations are not like that."

  4. If restorable → map FULL treatment sequence, assess cumulative risk

Physics — Three Concepts

Absorption: Photon strikes atom → electron jumps from ground (E1) to excited state (E2).

Spontaneous Emission: Electron falls E2→E1, releases ONE photon. Natural, uncontrolled, incoherent light.

Stimulated Emission: Incident photon hits already-excited electron → releases TWO identical photons (same energy, frequency, direction, coherent). THIS is the core mechanism behind laser light.

Wavelength & Penetration — THE Key Rule

HIGH YIELD Shorter wavelength = MORE penetration into tissue and bone (Ex: Nd:YAG 1064nm). Longer wavelength = LESS penetration (only 2-4 cell layers at surface) (Ex: Er:YAG 2940nm). All dental lasers: ~500nm to 10,600nm range.

Properties of Laser Light (4 properties)

Monochromatic — single wavelength/color. Coherent — identical wave shape in time and phase. Collimated — perfect parallel beam. Directional — tight, concentrated beam.

Photochemical (Biostimulation/LLLT): Stimulates mitochondria → ↑ATP → ↑cell metabolism → faster tissue repair, vasodilation, analgesia, anti-inflammatory. Non-contact, low power. Fluorescence at 655nm for caries detection.

Repair ("Healing by Scar")

FREE GINGIVAL GRAFT (FGG)

Full-thickness (epithelium + CT) from palate → transplanted to prepared bed. Template for sizing. Harvest 2mm from palatal teeth, ≥1mm thick. Watch for greater palatine artery.

Advantages

Rapid, predictable. Can combine with frenectomy. Excellent long-term stability (visible 20+ years). Can address 4 MGD problems simultaneously (recession + lack of KT + aberrant frenum + decreased vestibular depth).

Disadvantages

Poor color match (palatal tissue different — visible mismatch for decades). Two surgical sites. Post-op discomfort.

CONNECTIVE TISSUE GRAFT (CTG)

CT layer ONLY (no epithelium) → placed UNDERNEATH patient's flap. Gold standard with FGG for autogenous grafts.

Advantages

Excellent color blend (patient's tissue covers it). Predictable for RT1. Can use with any flap technique.

Disadvantages

Two surgical sites. Less KT gain than FGG (depends on recipient tissue). Need adequate palatal tissue. Three harvest methods: double incision, single incision, harvest FGG then de-epithelialize.

ALLODERM (Acellular Dermal Matrix)

Processed human dermis. Used instead of palatal harvest. Comparable outcomes to CTG. Hydrate 15-20 min.

EXAM MUST be completely covered by flap — if exposed, it necrotizes and smells like a dead animal. CTG can survive partial exposure. Alloderm CANNOT. Be especially careful in mandibular anteriors (thin tissue, easy perforation).

SECTION 8: PERIO EMERGENCIES

Classic Fleisher MCQ territory. 3-4 questions likely. Know NUG treatment, abscess differential, pericoronitis complications.

NUG/NUP

NUG = Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis. NUP = extends to periodontium (attachment loss). AKA Vincent's Infection / trench mouth.

Presentation: Surface necrosis with grey-white membrane. Punched-out papillae. Interproximal craters. Very painful. Spirochetes. Foul odor.

Risk factors: Poor OH, existing gingivitis, smoking, stress, immunosuppression, malnutrition. Mean age ~20 years (Barnes et al. 1973: 87% had poor OH).

Histology (Listgarten 1965): 4 layers from tooth outward: bacterial zone → PMN band → zone of tissue necrosis (dominated by spirochetes) → zone of spirochete infiltration.

CRITICAL DO NOT SRP FIRST. Tissue is extremely fragile. SRP = guaranteed tissue loss. Instead: systemic antibiotic (metronidazole, penicillin, or tetracycline) + very gentle debridement + CHX rinse (Peridex). Let antibiotic work FIRST → then gentle instrumentation later.

Other Acute Conditions

Herpetic Gingivostomatitis

Primarily children. 7-10 day duration. Sudden high fever + lymphadenopathy → red/swollen gingiva → mucosal vesicles. Incubation 10-14 days. Can mimic NUG. Tx: palliative (comfort until it runs its course). Acyclovir within first 3 days reduces symptom duration.

Pericoronitis

Inflammation around incompletely erupted tooth (usually mandibular 3rd molar). Operculum = red, swollen, tender. Pain may radiate to ear. Tx: irrigation + antibiotics if fever. I&D if fluctuant.

EXAM Ludwig's Angina = rapidly expanding, potentially LIFE-THREATENING infection of submandibular/sublingual spaces. Unlikely but possible complication of pericoronitis. Symptoms: severe neck pain/swelling, fever, dysphagia, stridor (high-pitched breathing from airway blockage).

Aphthous Stomatitis

80% are minor (mucosal/non-keratinized tissue, white with red halo, <1cm, heals 10-14 days). Major = weeks to heal. Herpetiform = up to 100 lesions at once. Etiology unknown (stress, trauma, allergies). Tx: palliative — topical steroid, Orajel.

Periodontal Abscess

Definition: Acute destructive process → localized pus communicating with oral cavity through gingival sulcus. NOT from tooth pulp.

Most common symptom: PAIN. Usually associated with pre-existing pocket that became occluded (foreign body, superficial healing blocks drainage).

Stat: 85% of toothaches are PULPAL. <15% are periodontal abscesses. Always vitality test.

Microbiology: 66% gram-negative, 65% anaerobic. P. gingivalis + P. intermedia in 90% of abscesses.

The Landmark Study

LIT Axelsson & Lindhe 1981 (J Clin Perio): 77 patients post-perio tx. 52 in SPT (q2mo×2yr, then q3mo×4yr) vs 25 returned to GP. SPT group: 99% surfaces stable over 6 years. Non-recall: only 45%. SPT can be done in GP office, not just perio office.

6-month recall for healthy patients: ZERO scientific evidence. Originated from 1890s women's magazine, picked up by Colgate. Convention, not science.

3-month recall for perio patients: OVERWHELMING evidence (U Michigan, U Gothenburg, U Minnesota, Loma Linda).

SPT Protocol — 4 Steps

"Every Monkey Tries Picking Fleas" = Examination, Motivation, Treatment, Polishing/Fluoride
  1. Examination, re-evaluation, diagnosis — probe EVERY site EVERY time
  2. Motivation, re-instruction, instrumentation — reinforce OHI, clean supra AND subgingival
  3. Treatment of reinfected sites — suppuration = STRONGEST sign of active breakdown. Thorough SRP under anesthesia ± local antibiotics ± surgery. Follow up 2 weeks.
  4. Polishing, fluoride, determine future SPT interval

Root Sensitivity Treatments

SuperSeal (Fleisher's preference)

Potassium oxalate → forms calcium oxalate crystals that physically block dentin tubules. ONE application. Instantaneous. No soft tissue irritation. ~80-90% success rate.

Gluma

Resin occlusion of tubules. Contains glutaraldehyde (irritates soft tissue). Requires 2-3 applications. Effective but more complex.

Mechanism (hydrodynamic theory): Fluid-filled dentin tubules → rapid bidirectional fluid movement → stimulates pulp nerve endings → pain. Block the tubules → block the fluid movement → block the pain.

Peri-Implant Maintenance Pearls

  • Probe implants — areas probed don't break down more than non-probed
  • Subgingival cement = major problem causing peri-implantitis. Use minimal cement, don't overfill, consider screw-retained
  • OraVue endoscope: SRP not effective below 5mm. Endoscope allows direct visualization without surgery
