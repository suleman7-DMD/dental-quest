1. Bone Homeostasis → Destruction → Disease

Bone is always remodeling. Tip the balance toward resorption and you get disease. The CAUSE of that tipping determines everything downstream.

ALVEOLAR BONE HOMEOSTASIS
Constant balance: Osteoblasts (build) vs Osteoclasts (destroy) When formation ≥ resorption → healthy bone turnover. When resorption > formation → net bone loss.
Think of it like a bank account — deposits and withdrawals. Health = balanced budget. Disease = spending more than you earn.
What tips the balance toward resorption?
RESORPTION > FORMATION = BONE BREAKDOWN Three distinct causes, each with different mechanisms and different treatment implications. All three can coexist in the same patient. → Essay 4: Occlusal Trauma
1. PLAQUE-INDUCED
Biofilm accumulates on tooth surfaces Bacteria trigger host inflammatory response. The INFLAMMATION destroys bone, not the bacteria directly.
Your immune system is the wrecking ball — bacteria just provoke it.
→ GINGIVITIS Inflammation confined to gingiva. >10% BOP, PD <3mm, NO CAL. 100% REVERSIBLE.
→ ± PERIODONTITIS Inflammation extends to bone/PDL. Interdental CAL ≥2 non-adjacent teeth. IRREVERSIBLE bone loss.
Plaque = PRIMARY etiology of attachment loss → Treatment Pipeline → NSPT/SPT
LIT Löe 1965: Experimental Gingivitis — 100% got gingivitis w/o OH, 100% reversed when OH resumed. Did NOT prove gingivitis → perio.
LIT Socransky 1984: Periodontal destruction is EPISODIC — bursts of destruction then quiescence, not continuous. Only 12% of sites lost >2mm over 6 yrs.
Disease begins INTERPROXIMALLY The interdental area (septal vessels) is where initial osseous lesions form. This is why most pockets are interproximal. → Defect Types
2. SYSTEMIC FACTORS
MODIFY host response — don't cause perio alone These accelerate breakdown that plaque initiated. They're risk modifiers, not independent causes.
Smoking Vasoconstriction → less BOP (masks disease). Impairs neutrophil function. Delays healing post-surgery.
Smoker can have severe bone loss but "look" healthy because tissue is fibrotic and doesn't bleed.
Diabetes HbA1c ≥7.0% = Grade C modifier. Elevated glucose → AGEs → hyperinflammatory state. Impaired wound healing.
Immunosuppression HIV, organ transplant meds, chemo — all reduce host defense capacity.
Genetics Hyper-responsive macrophage phenotype (elevated PGE2, IL-1β). Can't modify — just accounts for why some patients break down faster.
LIT Tomar & Asma 2000: 53% of US periodontitis attributable to smoking. Current smokers = 4× risk. ≥11 yrs quit = returns to non-smoker risk level.
3. TRAUMATIC OCCLUSAL FORCE
Affects ATTACHMENT APPARATUS ONLY PDL + alveolar bone. Does NOT affect supracrestal soft tissues (JE + CT attachment stay intact).
Attachment apparatus = the stuff that holds the tooth in bone. Supracrestal tissues = the gingival cuff above bone. TFO hits the anchor, not the cuff.
Does NOT cause pocketing
Does NOT cause recession
Does NOT initiate attachment loss
BUT: Co-destructive with ACTIVE infection Heavy force + active periodontal infection = exacerbated destruction rate. This is the Glickman concept. → FULL TFO breakdown: Essay 4
Primary vs Secondary TFO Primary = excessive force on NORMAL periodontium → reversible. Secondary = normal force on WEAKENED periodontium → not reversible. → Full details §14
ESSAY 4 Glickman: Zone of Irritation (supracrestal, plaque-driven) vs Zone of Co-Destruction (infrabony, force changes pathway of inflammation). Unproven theory but testable concept.
BONE RESORPTION PATTERN — determined by bone THICKNESS at the site The morphology of the investing root and its relationship to the volume, mineralization density and shape of surrounding bone are major factors in determining osseous lesion formation. Pathway of inflammation + vascular network from gingival inflammatory lesion into underlying alveolar process also matters. → Defect Classification → Osseous Surgery
THIN BONE (buccal/lingual plates) Entire thin plate dissolves uniformly → HORIZONTAL LOSS. Even reduction like a wall crumbling down evenly. Tissue follows bone and RECEDES → shallow PD despite real attachment loss.
This is why a patient can have severe bone loss buccally but only probe 3mm — the tissue receded WITH the bone.
→ Suprabony pocket
THICK BONE (interproximal septum) Enough volume for inflammation to carve a DEFECT while walls remain standing → VERTICAL/INFRABONY LOSS. Irregular craters and defects. Papilla does NOT recede → DEEP POCKETING (tissue stays high while bone goes low).
This is why most deep pockets are interproximal — thick bone gets carved into craters instead of dissolving evenly.
→ Infrabony pocket → Wall defects

2. Bone Defect Classification

WHERE and HOW bone was destroyed determines what you can do about it. Number of remaining walls = regenerative potential. This feeds directly into your surgical decision. → Osseous Surgery → GTR/GBR

BONE DESTRUCTION CREATES DEFECTS
SUPRABONY DEFECT = HORIZONTAL BONE LOSS Bone crest is at the same level across multiple teeth. Base of pocket sits ABOVE (coronal to) bone crest. Bone destroyed evenly — no walls to classify. Treatment: gingivectomy, SRP, or simple flap. No regeneration possible (nothing to contain a graft). → Suprabony pocket → Osseous resection
INFRABONY DEFECT = VERTICAL BONE LOSS Pocket extends BELOW the bone crest INTO a defect carved by disease. Classified by number of remaining bony walls. The walls determine containment → containment determines regenerative potential. MUST raise a flap to access. Cannot treat blind. → Infrabony pocket → Flap required
What is a "wall"?
The original tooth socket has bony walls surrounding the root: buccal, lingual, mesial, distal. The root surface itself acts as the final wall. When periodontitis destroys bone, it eats away some walls but may leave others standing. The NUMBER of remaining bony walls tells you how CONTAINED the defect is — and containment is EVERYTHING for regeneration.
INFRABONY DEFECTS — Classified by walls remaining
3-WALL DEFECT
3 bony walls remain intact Root = 4th wall. Trough/trench shape. MOST CONTAINED defect.
Imagine a narrow trench dug between walls — the graft material sits in there like concrete in a form.
Blood supply from 3 directions → best healing environment
Graft material stays in place (self-containing)
Regeneration: BEST prognosis GTR most predictable here. Membrane stabilizes easily. → GTR
2-WALL DEFECT
= INTERDENTAL CRATER MOST COMMON perio defect. Both interproximal (mesial + distal) walls lost. Buccal + lingual walls intact. Crater between two teeth.
Disease ate through the septum between teeth but left the cheek-side and tongue-side bone standing.
Less contained than 3-wall → graft can escape laterally
Regeneration: MODERATE Possible with biologics (GEM 21, EMD). May need membrane + graft combo. → GTR + biologics
Resection option: Shallow craters → resect base to most coronal position in septum. High-speed carbide/diamond burs + generous water. → Osseous Surgery
1-WALL (Hemiseptal)
Only 1 bony wall remains LEAST CONTAINED. Minimal blood supply from only 1 direction. Graft won't stay — nothing to hold it.
Like trying to build a sandcastle with only one wall — sand just falls out the other sides.
Regeneration: WORST prognosis → Best treated with RESECTION. Remove bone to establish even architecture. → Ostectomy
COMBINATION
Character CHANGES from base to top MOST COMMON in real clinical practice. At the BASE (deepest): 3-wall (most contained). MIDDLE: transitions to 2-wall. At the TOP (most coronal): 1-wall (least contained).
Treatment = DUAL APPROACH RESECT the uncontained coronal portion (1-wall top) — when resection won't jeopardize adjacent teeth. REGENERATE the contained apical portion (3-wall base) — predictable with GTR. → Osseous → GTR
LIT Fleisher slides: "Since most periodontal defects offer a morphologic combination in appearance: more contained at the base, less so at the coronal part" → resect top + regenerate bottom.
CIRCUMFERENTIAL (Moat/Cup) DEFECT — 360° bone loss around tooth like a moat around a castle. Poor regeneration prognosis — no walls = no containment. Often indicates extraction or guarded prognosis.
If you see bone loss going all the way around a tooth on a PA radiograph — that tooth is in serious trouble.
BONE ARCHITECTURE — Tissue follows bone. ALWAYS.
Positive architecture = interproximal bone MORE coronal than radicular bone = HEALTHY = the goal of surgery. Think: bone is higher between teeth than on the cheek/tongue sides — this is the natural scallop.
Reverse architecture = interproximal bone LOWER than radicular = PATHOLOGIC = causes pocketing = must correct surgically. Disease ate away the interproximal bone, flipping the natural contour.
Flattened = same level everywhere = intermediate state.
Critical point: You CANNOT determine bone architecture by looking at tissue. Must PROBE or RAISE A FLAP to assess. Gingival tissues do not conform to abrupt architectural changes in bone produced by disease or surgery. → Osseous recontouring goal
ROOT TRUNK — Critical for multi-rooted teeth The depth of a periodontal defect in mm is NOT as significant for multi-rooted teeth as the relationship of the defect to the root trunk. Root trunk dimension is often NARROWEST for the first molar. A shallow defect (1-3mm) within root trunk dimensions is manageable. A deep defect (>3mm) extending apical to root trunk → furcation involvement → dramatically worse prognosis. → Crown Lengthening considerations

3. Pocket Types — Where the Pocket Bottom Sits Relative to Bone

This determines everything about treatment. Fleisher's GTR lecture: "How can you tell if there is a pocket or a pseudo-pocket?" — this is foundational.

PROBING SHOWS INCREASED PD — What kind of pocket is it?
FALSE POCKET (Pseudopocket)
Excess soft tissue creates an artificially deep reading Probe does NOT extend past CEJ. NO attachment loss. Bone level is NORMAL on radiograph. Gingiva is just swollen or overgrown.
How to confirm: Gingival margin is coronal to CEJ + radiograph shows normal bone level. Probing depth alone is NOT enough — you MUST assess gingival margin position relative to CEJ.
Treatment: OHI may resolve (if inflammation-caused). Gingivectomy if tissue is fibrotic/overgrown. Altered passive eruption is a common cause. → Coslet Classification
EXAM Fleisher's email: "lots of ppl got the Q wrong about how to confirm pseudopocket." Need BOTH probing + radiograph. Neither alone is sufficient.
SUPRABONY POCKET
TRUE pocket — CAL present Probe extends past CEJ (attachment loss confirmed). BUT the pocket bottom sits ABOVE (coronal to) bone crest. Soft tissue problem is worse than bone problem.
Associated with HORIZONTAL bone loss Even bone destruction across the board. No infrabony component. → Suprabony defect
Treatment options: Gingivectomy (if adequate KT + no bone work needed), SRP, or simple flap. Regeneration NOT indicated (no contained defect). → Incision: External bevel → KT Decision
INFRABONY POCKET
TRUE pocket — pocket extends BELOW bone crest Probe extends past CEJ AND into a bony defect. Bone has been eaten away in irregular pattern creating a crater or defect below the crest.
Classified by walls remaining 1-wall, 2-wall, 3-wall, or combination defect. Number of walls = regenerative potential. → Defect Classification
MUST RAISE A FLAP to access and treat. Cannot debride infrabony defect blind. Need direct visualization. → Flap Selection
Treatment depends on defect morphology: Contained (3-wall) → regenerate with GTR. Uncontained (1-wall) → resect with osseous surgery. Combination → resect top + regenerate bottom. → Osseous → GTR
CLINICAL: How do you actually CONFIRM pocket type?
Step 1: Probe the site → get PD reading.
Step 2: Locate CEJ → determine gingival margin position relative to CEJ.
 • Margin APICAL to CEJ = recession = ADD recession to PD for total CAL.
 • Margin CORONAL to CEJ = tissue covering CEJ = SUBTRACT that distance from PD.
Step 3: Take radiograph → assess bone levels relative to CEJ.
 • Bone normal (1-2mm apical to CEJ) + deep PD = pseudopocket.
 • Bone loss visible + deep PD = true pocket (supra or infrabony depending on defect pattern).
Step 4: Bone loss even across teeth = suprabony. Bone loss irregular/cratered = infrabony.
Remember: Probing ALONE can lie (pseudopockets inflate PD; recession hides severity). Radiographs ALONE can lie (only show interproximal bone well, can't see buccal/lingual). TOGETHER they give you the real picture.
EXAM Fleisher's charting email: "They must identify the location of the gingival margin relative to the CEJ. This is the ONLY way."

5. NSPT & SPT — The Foundation Everything Else Sits On

Non-surgical periodontal therapy = SRP + adjuncts. Supportive periodontal therapy = lifelong maintenance. Neither sexy, both non-negotiable. Without SPT, even perfect surgery fails.

SCALING vs ROOT PLANING — They're different
SCALING Removal of deposits (plaque, calculus) from tooth surfaces — both supra and subgingival. Mechanical disruption of biofilm. You're cleaning the outside of the tooth.
ROOT PLANING Instrumentation of the ROOT SURFACE specifically — removing diseased/contaminated cementum and creating a smooth, hard, clean surface that is biologically compatible with reattachment. You're preparing the root for healing.
Scaling = cleaning the house. Root planing = sanding and refinishing the floors so new carpet can stick.
Goals of SRP Disrupt and reduce subgingival microflora. Remove calculus and endotoxin-contaminated cementum. Create a biologically acceptable root surface. Reduce inflammation → pocket reduction through tissue shrinkage (not bone regrowth). Allow tissue to reattach or form a long junctional epithelium.
LIT Greenstein 1992: Despite limitations, SRP remains the definitive approach for mild-moderate periodontitis and the starting point before any surgical intervention.
THE CATCH — SRP has a ceiling In pockets ≥5mm, clinicians inadequately debride roots 65% of the time (Caffesse 1986). Deeper the pocket, worse the access, more residual calculus left behind. This is WHY surgery exists — not because we want to cut, but because we physically cannot clean deep enough blind. "Closed" periodontal procedures have inherent limitations in effectively treating established periodontitis, as evidenced by findings upon flap reflection.
You wouldn't try to clean the back of a 10-foot closet through a 2-inch crack in the door. At some point you need to open the door. That's flap surgery.
→ Critical PD threshold
IMPORTANT NUANCE — Shallow pockets can LOSE attachment after SRP In shallow pockets (≤3mm), instrumentation trauma can cause more harm than the disease. Mechanical trauma from aggressive scaling at shallow sites → tissue damage → net CAL loss. Deep pockets show gains, shallow pockets can show losses. This is why you don't SRP a 2mm pocket.
LIT Lindhe: Sites with initial PD <4.2mm did better with scaling alone than with flap surgery (over-treatment is real).
ADJUNCTS TO SRP — Locally Administered Agents
Used at NON-RESPONSIVE sites after SRP re-eval, NOT as first-line These are for sites that didn't respond to Phase I. You don't skip to LAAs — you SRP first, re-evaluate, THEN deploy these at persistent ≥5mm + BOP sites.
ARESTIN Minocycline microspheres. Placed subgingivally. Sustained release.
ATRIDOX Doxycycline hyclate 10% gel. Flows into pocket, solidifies.
PERIO CHIP Chlorhexidine gluconate 2.5mg biodegradable chip.
ACTISITE Tetracycline fiber (older, less common).
SUPPORTIVE PERIODONTAL THERAPY (SPT) — D4910
SPT = LIFELONG q3mo recall for any patient with history of periodontitis Not a "cleaning." It's re-assessment (new probing, BOP, radiographs as needed) + instrumentation at sites showing re-inflammation + reinforcement of OHI. Starts when Phase I (or Phase II) is complete. This is the single most important thing for your patient's long-term prognosis.
LIT Axelsson & Lindhe 1981: Patients with regular SPT maintained 99% of teeth vs 45% without SPT over 6 years. | Nyman 1977: Without recall, surgery fails regardless of technique — pocket depths rebound to pre-surgical levels in 2 years. | Rosling 1976: With recall q2wk, patients maintained surgical reductions + gained attachment.
WHY q3mo specifically? Harper & Robinson 1987: SRP reduces pathogenic flora but TEMPORARILY — recolonization occurs within ~3 months. Professional cleaning at 3-month intervals at least partly compensates for variations in self-performed plaque control (Isidor & Karring 1986, Ramfjord 1982). → Essay 3: SPT rationale
SYSTEMIC ANTIBIOTICS — When indicated
Aggressive/Stage III-IV Grade C periodontitis — systemic adjuncts Amoxicillin + Metronidazole (tandem or serial) for treatment of aggressive forms. Azithromycin: effective against anaerobes + gram-negative bacilli, concentrates in periodontal tissues at levels significantly higher than normal gingiva. Recent data suggest effective adjunctive therapy for increasing attachment levels in aggressive periodontitis.
Systemic antibiotics aren't for every perio patient — they're for the ones where local measures aren't enough because the bacterial load or host response is out of control.

6. Incision Decision Tree

The direction of the blade tells you the PURPOSE of the cut. This section feeds directly into flap selection and procedure choice. → KT Decision → Flaps

INCISIONS — Two fundamental orientations
HORIZONTAL (Mesiodistal) Runs along the arch. This is where you choose BEVEL DIRECTION — and bevel direction determines the entire procedure.
Horizontal = running with the arch like a train on tracks. The bevel is which way the blade is angled as it cuts.
VERTICAL (Releasing) Incisions Perpendicular to arch. Extends from gingival margin into vestibule PAST the MGJ. Purpose = FLAP MOBILITY. Allows flap to be reflected and repositioned. Base ALWAYS wider than coronal — trapezoid shape preserves blood supply from the base.
Vertical releases are the "hinges" that let your flap door swing open. Narrow base = choke blood supply = flap dies.
HORIZONTAL INCISION → Two types based on BEVEL DIRECTION
EXTERNAL BEVEL
Direction: APICAL → CORONAL Blade starts OUTSIDE the gingiva on the apical/outer surface, angles inward toward base of pocket. Target = just below base of pocket.
Going coronal = going outward = EXTERNAL. The blade faces outward/externally.
Procedure = GINGIVECTOMY Goal = POCKET ELIMINATION. You're excising the ENTIRE pocket wall — cutting it off. The diseased tissue is REMOVED, not reflected.
Healing = SECONDARY INTENTION Raw open wound left behind. No flap to cover it. Tissue heals by granulation from the wound base up.
Makes sense — you cut the tissue OFF, so there's nothing left to close over the wound. It heals like a scraped knee.
Requirements (ALL must be met): 1. Sufficient KT to sacrifice (you're removing tissue permanently). 2. NO bone work needed (you don't get access to bone with this). 3. SUPRABONY defects ONLY (no infrabony component). If ANY requirement fails → WRONG procedure. → KT Decision: Plenty KT + No bone → Crown Lengthening: Coslet 1A only
EXAM Both gingival curettage and gingivectomy are utilized with suprabony defects only. You do NOT gain access to bone defect with either procedure. WHY? Because flap procedures were designed to gain access to bone defects.
INTERNAL BEVEL
Direction: CORONAL → APICAL Blade starts at or near gingival margin, angles inward toward crest of bone. Target = crest of bone.
Going apical = going inward = INTERNAL. The blade faces inward/internally toward bone.
Procedure = ALL FLAP SURGERIES Goal = POCKET REDUCTION. Tissue is reflected as a flap, not excised. Pocket elimination can be achieved by flap REPOSITIONING (APF). Internal bevel is the incision for osseous surgery, GTR, crown lengthening — everything. → Flap Classification → Osseous Surgery
Healing = PRIMARY INTENTION Wound is COVERED/CLOSED by the flap. Tissue is sutured back. Heals under a protective cover.
You opened a door, did the work, then closed the door and stitched it shut. Much better healing environment.
3 subtypes — determined by starting position relative to gingival margin:
SUBMARGINAL Starts 1-2mm FROM margin (not in sulcus). Use when you have PLENTY of KT — can afford to sacrifice some. Removes a collar of tissue. → KT: Plenty + bone
CREVICULAR (Sulcular) Starts IN the sulcus hugging the tooth. Use when you have LIMITED KT — preserve every mm. No tissue sacrificed. → KT: Limited + bone
PARAMARGINAL PALATE ONLY. Multiple parallel incisions to thin palatal tissue. Palate is ALL keratinized tissue so always safe to excise. Used to reduce tissue bulk palatally.
What determines which internal bevel subtype?
Factor 1: KT width. Plenty → submarginal (can sacrifice). Limited → crevicular (preserve). Palate → paramarginal (all KT, always safe).
Factor 2: Interdental probing depth. Disease begins interproximally. Maximum PD should be in interdental regions. Deeper PD = need more tissue reduction with the internal bevel incision → influences how far from margin you start.

7. The KT Decision Tree — Fleisher's 2 Questions for EVERY Surgical Case

These two questions determine your incision, your flap type, and your procedure. Master this and the surgical decision almost makes itself.

Q1: HOW MUCH KERATINIZED TISSUE DO I HAVE?
PLENTY (>3-4mm) Tissue to spare. Can sacrifice some and still have adequate KT for long-term health.
LIT Lang & Löe 1972: Min 2mm KT (1mm attached + 1mm free gingiva) needed for gingival health.
LIMITED (<2-3mm) Every mm is precious. Cannot sacrifice any. Must preserve or augment.
LIT Serino 1994: 3mm recession worsened 67% of the time. 4mm recession worsened 98%.
Q2: DO I NEED TO TOUCH BONE?
YES — Need bone access Osseous surgery, crown lengthening, GTR/GBR, defect debridement, implant placement. → Osseous → CL → GTR
NO — Soft tissue only Pocket from excess tissue, pseudopocket, tissue overgrowth, altered passive eruption.
4 COMBINATIONS → 4 ANSWERS
PLENTY KT + NO BONE needed → External gingivectomy Simplest procedure. Excise excess tissue. Open wound (secondary intention). Just pocket elimination. → External bevel incision → Coslet 1A (lots KT + bone normal)
PLENTY KT + NEED BONE access → Internal bevel (submarginal) → raise flap → osseous surgery → APF Can sacrifice a collar of KT with the submarginal incision because you have enough. Full thickness reflection to access bone. APF carries remaining KT apically. → Submarginal incision → FTF/Combined flap
LIMITED KT + NEED BONE access → Crevicular incision → preserve ALL KT → split-thickness → APF carries KT apically Cannot sacrifice ANY tissue. Start incision in sulcus. Split-thickness reflection preserves periosteum. APF repositions flap apically over periosteum "carpet." → Crevicular incision → PTF/Combined flap
LIMITED KT + NO BONE needed → Is surgery even needed? If yes, need to ADD tissue FIRST. Consider: FGG to increase KT, CTG for root coverage, orthodontic forced eruption. Can't do pocket surgery if you don't have tissue to work with. → MGD: FGG/CTG
PALATE EXCEPTION: APF is IMPOSSIBLE on the palate. No MGJ on palate = no elastic alveolar mucosa = no mobility to reposition. All palatal tissue is KT → always safe to excise. Paramarginal incision to reduce bulk. You can NEVER move palatal tissue apically — it has nowhere to go.
The palate is like a brick floor — you can't slide it anywhere. Buccal tissue is like a rug on a hardwood floor — slides freely once you free it.

8. Flap Classification — What Are You Reflecting Off Bone?

Once you've made your incision and start reflecting — the TYPE of flap determines what you can DO. This connects to incisions (§6), KT decisions (§7), and every surgical procedure downstream.

FLAP ELEVATED — WHAT TYPE?
3 categories based on WHAT LAYERS come off the bone
FULL THICKNESS (FTF)
= Mucoperiosteal Flap Take EVERYTHING off bone: epithelium + CT + periosteum. Bare bone exposed. Blunt elevation (periosteal elevator).
You're peeling the entire covering off the bone like removing wallpaper, backing and all.
Advantages: ✓ Direct bone access — can see and modify bone. ✓ Less technique sensitive, faster. Disadvantages: ✗ Hard to REPOSITION (no periosteum left as bed to slide over). ✗ Bone exposed → slight resorption risk from drying/trauma.
USE WHEN: Need to SEE or MODIFY bone Osseous surgery (ostectomy/osteoplasty). GTR membrane placement. Crown lengthening assessment. Crater/wall defect debridement. Implant placement. Any resective procedure. → Osseous Surgery → GTR → Crown Lengthening
SPLIT THICKNESS (PTF)
= Partial Thickness = Mucosal Flap Take outer layers only: epithelium + some CT. PERIOSTEUM STAYS ON BONE. Sharp dissection (scalpel — more technique sensitive).
You're peeling the wallpaper off but leaving the backing (periosteum) stuck to the wall (bone).
Advantages: ✓ Allows REPOSITIONING — periosteum = "carpet" to slide flap over. ✓ Maintains bone blood supply (periosteum protects bone). ✓ Periosteum = ANCHOR for vertical mattress sutures. Disadvantages: ✗ Technique sensitive — risk of perforation (thin tissue can tear). ✗ Slower, more difficult dissection. Contraindications: Thin gingiva <1mm (too thin to split). Shallow vestibule (no room to reposition).
USE WHEN: Need to REPOSITION flap Apically positioned flap (APF) — the PTF slides apically over periosteum. Coronally advanced flap (CAF) for root coverage. Mucogingival surgeries. Pocket elimination flap. When crestal bone margin is THIN → protect it (periosteum prevents resorption). When fenestration or dehiscence is present. → Vertical mattress suture → MGD procedures
SPLIT-FULL-SPLIT (Combined)
Best of both worlds. Flap character CHANGES by zone. 1. SPLIT coronally — at the papilla area. Preserve periosteum for suturing and graft bed. Sharp dissection. 2. FULL at bone level — need to SEE where bone crest is. Blunt elevation. Bare bone exposed at the working zone. 3. SPLIT apically — gain mobility. Release past MGJ into elastic alveolar mucosa. Allows flap advancement or apical positioning.
Start careful (split), go aggressive at the work zone (full to see bone), then go mobile (split to free the flap for movement).
USE FOR: Crown lengthening (need to see bone AND reposition flap). → CL CAF for root coverage (Bernimoulin 1981). → MGD Mucogingival surgeries requiring bone access + repositioning. FTF coronal flap + PTF apical flap in one design.
KEY CONCEPT: WHY SPLIT THICKNESS ALLOWS REPOSITIONING
Full thickness = flap includes periosteum → nothing left on bone to slide over. Like pulling up the carpet AND the padding — bare concrete, nothing slides.
Split thickness = flap freed from bone BUT periosteum stays as a "CARPET." Flap slides over periosteum in any direction — like sliding furniture on a smooth floor. Release PAST the MGJ into alveolar mucosa (elastic tissue) for maximum mobility. This is why vertical mattress sutures grab periosteum — it's the anchor left behind on bone that holds the repositioned flap in its new position. → Vertical Mattress Suture technique
DEFINITIONS — The tissue layers you keep confusing
Periosteum = thin cling-wrap layer directly on bone surface. Contains osteoprogenitor cells. Stays behind in split-thickness. Becomes anchor for vertical mattress sutures. Protects bone from resorption when left intact.
Keratinized tissue (KT) = tough, pink, stippled tissue from gingival margin to MGJ. Firm and protective. Like skin on bottom of foot — built to take abuse.
Alveolar mucosa = red, shiny, elastic tissue BELOW the MGJ in the vestibule. Movable. Stretchy. Where vertical releases extend into. Where you gain flap mobility.
MGJ (Mucogingival Junction) = the LINE between keratinized tissue and alveolar mucosa. Visible clinically as a color/texture change (pink stippled → red shiny). Fixed landmark — doesn't move with disease.
Attached gingiva = the KT that is bound to underlying bone/periosteum. Measured from base of sulcus to MGJ. NOT the same as total KT width (which includes free gingiva).

9. Osseous Surgery — Reshaping Bone to Create Physiologic Architecture

The term osseous surgery encompasses varied forms of therapeutic procedures to manage bony lesions resulting from periodontal disease. The goal: positive architecture, minimal PD, maintainable contours. → Defects → Crown Lengthening

ESSAY 1 + 3 Osseous surgery concepts are tested in Crown Lengthening (Essay 1: "when gingivectomy alone vs flap + removing bone") and NSPT/Surgical Therapy (Essay 3: "rationale for surgical perio therapy").
OSSEOUS SURGERY — OBJECTIVES
Objectives of Osseous Resective Therapy (from Fleisher slides): 1. Create contours that permit patients to accomplish effective plaque control. 2. Create bone contours which gingival tissue will parallel (tissue follows bone — ALWAYS). 3. Permit primary wound closure. 4. Expose additional clinical crown for restorative treatment. Objectives of Pocket Elimination: Minimal probing depths → complete debridement possible during maintenance → effective daily home care → predictable use of brushing, flossing, adjuncts. The establishment of a shallow gingival sulcus by pocket elimination is a prerequisite for adequate plaque control and preventive maintenance.
Osseous Resective Therapy — Indications: Adequate remaining attachment once physiologic morphology is established. No esthetic limitations. No anatomic limitations. No root hypersensitivity limitations. Contraindications: Inadequate remaining attachment (would leave tooth with no support). Esthetic limitations (anterior). Anatomic limitations (mental foramen, IAN, sinus). Root hypersensitivity limitations. Alternative, more effective therapy available (e.g., GTR for contained defect). Fleisher: NO resective surgery in anterior UNLESS full prosthetic reconstruction planned.
Disadvantages of Resective Therapy: Attachment loss caused by removal of supporting bone. Un-esthetic root exposure. Hypersensitive roots. These are real trade-offs — you're sacrificing bone to create architecture that's maintainable long-term.
THE TWO TYPES OF BONE REMOVAL — Know cold
OSTECTOMY Removal of SUPPORTING (radicular + interradicular) bone to eliminate osseous defects. CAUSES attachment loss — you're removing bone that was holding the tooth in place. Done to establish biologic width, eliminate deep defects, or create physiologic architecture. This is the "costly" move in osseous surgery.
Ostectomy = cutting into the foundation. Necessary sometimes, but expensive in attachment currency.
OSTEOPLASTY Removal of NON-SUPPORTING bone for reshaping purposes. NO attachment loss. Reshaping ledges, tori reduction, thick heavy bony exostoses, blunted interdental craters, widow's peaks, enhanced flap placement with improved alveolar contours.
Osteoplasty = cosmetic recontouring. You're smoothing the surface, not cutting into the structure.
LIT Selipsky 1976 (Dent Clin North Am): Very little supporting bone is removed when resective techniques are properly applied. The need for splinting due to diminished attachment levels should be determined PRIOR to the procedure.
OSSEOUS MANAGEMENT — Step by Step
Osseous Management Begins with: Elevation of buccal and lingual flaps. Maintain appropriate dimension of keratinized gingiva. Margins thinned and parabolic in shape with an internal bevel incision. Then: Debride defects → assess bone architecture → determine what needs osteoplasty vs ostectomy → recontour → APF. → Internal bevel incision → Flap type
APF + OSSEOUS RESECTION — The Complete Package Apically positioned flaps in conjunction with osseous resection provide: 1) Predictability. 2) Achieve minimal probing depths. 3) Potential to increase the zone of attached gingiva (as flap moves apical, MGJ doesn't → net KT gain). 4) Stable base for restorative dentistry. → PTF for APF → Vertical mattress suture for APF
SPECIFIC OSSEOUS RECONTOURING CONCEPTS
WIDOW'S PEAK Tiny bony spike at the transitional line angle. MUST remove — if left, tissue follows bone, bounces up coronally at peak → creates isolated deep pocket that's impossible to maintain. Removed with: Ochsenbein chisel (hand instrument). Goal: Establish flat or horizontal mesiodistal crest at line angles.
DOUBLE PARABOLIZATION Treat a molar as "two premolars." Carve separate parabolic curves over mesial root + distal root. Bone comes UP at furcation (stays intact). Each root gets its own scallop. Purpose: Prevents furcation exposure. Vertical groove in interdental bone extending apically from furca.
Instead of one big arch over the whole molar, you make two mini-arches — one per root — keeping the furcation ridge intact.
Shallow Craters (1-3mm deep) Within root trunk dimensions when associated with multi-rooted teeth. Treated with high-speed carbide or diamond burs + generous water — remove bone so base of crater becomes most coronal position in interdental septum. Essentially flatten the crater. → 2-wall defect
Radicular Bone Recontouring Facial and lingual surfaces contoured by ostectomy/osteoplasty to produce parabolic architecture similar to what existed prior to disease. Radicular bone thickness depends on tooth-to-bone relationship. As mesiodistal dimension increases posteriorly → bone and gingiva accept a FLATTER morphology. Degree of scalloping influenced by tooth form and root anatomy.
Goal of completed resective osseous surgery: Radicular bone should be APICAL to the interdental septum = positive architecture. Bone between adjacent teeth may need to be removed to avoid a precipitous change in osseous morphology (you can't leave a cliff between treated and untreated bone — tissue won't follow abrupt changes). For proper physiologic contour, radicular bone is apical to interdental septum at completion.
SUBTRACTIVE vs ADDITIVE — Every procedure falls in one camp
SUBTRACTIVE PROCEDURES Remove tissue/bone to reduce/eliminate pockets: 1. SRP — subgingival root curettage. Least invasive. Can be definitive. 2. Gingival curettage — scrape inner pocket wall. OBSOLETE standalone. 3. External gingivectomy — suprabony ONLY. Adequate KT + no bone work. →§6 4. Internal bevel gingivectomy — same reduction but as part of a flap. 5. ENAP (Excisional New Attachment Procedure). 6. OFD (Open Flap Debridement) — access only, flap returns to original position. 7. Modified Widman Flap — access without repositioning or bone removal. 8. Osseous Resective Surgery — full combo: flap + debridement + osteoplasty/ostectomy + APF. Most comprehensive. 9. Distal Wedge — tissue removal distal to last molar.
ADDITIVE PROCEDURES Add material to rebuild lost structures: 1. Osseous Graft — scaffold to replace bone. Auto/allo/xeno/alloplast. →§11 2. GTR — membrane around TEETH. PDL cells regenerate. ~4-6 weeks. →§11 3. GBR — membrane in EDENTULOUS areas. Osteoblasts. Up to 6 months. →§11 4. Biologics — GEM 21, EMD, PRP, BMP. Enhance regeneration. 5. Soft tissue grafts — FGG, CTG, Alloderm. Add KT or root coverage. →§13
Periodontal surgical procedures are either subtractive or additive in nature. Pocket reduction can occur by either approach. — Fleisher slide
LASERS IN OSSEOUS SURGERY (Dr. Ahn) Erbium lasers (Er:YAG) becoming popular for bone surgery — more precision, better access, reduced collateral damage, no vibration (increases surgical precision), eliminates noise of burs. Currently Er:YAG is safe and useful for periodontal bone surgery such as osseous removal or recontouring when used with saline irrigation. CO2 and Erbium family also used for surgical pocket therapy: calculus removal, detoxification of root surface and bone, removal of granulation tissue. Advantages: better furcation access, hemostasis, less post-op discomfort, faster healing. → Post-therapy: don't re-probe before 3mo

10. Crown Lengthening — Essay Topic 1

A restoratively driven periodontal procedure. The essay tests biologic width, indications/contraindications, gingivectomy vs flap+bone, healing times, and complications.

ESSAY 1 Crown Lengthening — 5 sub-questions. Must include: biologic width concept, how to determine if CL is needed vs other problems, indications/contraindications, gingivectomy vs flap+bone (Coslet), healing time rationale, and 3 complications with remedies. Must cite literature.
BIOLOGIC WIDTH (now called Supracrestal Attachment)
Biologic Width = the non-negotiable zone of tissue attachment above bone crest From bone crest coronally: ~1mm CT attachment (connective tissue fibers inserting into cementum) + ~1mm junctional epithelium (JE) = ~2mm of biologic width. Then the sulcus (~1mm) sits on top of that. So from bone crest to gingival margin ≈ 3mm total. Restorative margins MUST respect this. If a margin violates BW → chronic inflammation, bone resorption, pocket formation. How much distance from restorative margin to crest of bone? ± 4mm (allows 2mm BW + 1mm sulcus + ~1mm of clearance for ferrule/impression).
Think of BW as the body's "do not disturb" zone. It will ALWAYS try to re-establish itself. If you put a crown margin in this zone, the body will eat bone away until it gets its 2mm back — but now your margin is exposed and you're worse off than you started.
LIT Gargiulo, Wentz & Orban 1961 (J Periodont 42:8): Established the dimensions of the dentogingival junction — the foundational measurements for BW.
WHEN IS CROWN LENGTHENING NEEDED?
Essay Q2: "You remove a temp, see bleeding and ulceration. How do you determine if CL is necessary or another problem exists?" Other problems that cause bleeding/ulceration in the sulcus: 1. Plaque-induced gingivitis (inadequate OH around temp). 2. Open margins on temp (overhanging or short). 3. Excess cement or temp material subgingivally. 4. Allergic reaction to temp material. 5. Occlusal trauma on temp. How to distinguish: Probe carefully. Take a PA radiograph. Measure distance from restorative margin to bone crest. If margin is within 2mm of bone → BW violation → CL needed. If margin is 3-4mm from bone and you still have inflammation → it's one of the other causes above. Fix the OTHER cause first. Only do CL if you've ruled out everything else AND confirmed BW invasion.
Don't jump to CL. The exam wants to see that you THINK before you cut. Check the simplest explanations first.
Indications for Crown Lengthening: Tooth decay at or below gingival margin. Tooth fracture below gingival margin (with adequate remaining support). Teeth with excessive wear. Teeth with insufficient interocclusal space. Delayed (altered) passive eruption. Contraindications: Inadequate remaining support post-surgery. Esthetic concerns that can't be managed. Anatomic limitations (furcation proximity, adjacent teeth compromised). Root hypersensitivity. When alternative therapy is more effective (e.g., orthodontic forced eruption, deep margin elevation). → Osseous resective contraindications match
GINGIVECTOMY ALONE vs FLAP + BONE REMOVAL
This is directly answered by the Coslet 1977 classification
COSLET 1A Lots of KT + Bone is at NORMAL position (NOT at CEJ). → Gingivectomy alone. Just remove excess tissue. Bone doesn't need to be touched. Simplest scenario. → External bevel
COSLET 1B Lots of KT + Bone is AT THE CEJ. → Gingivectomy FAILS here. If you just remove tissue, you violate BW (bone is too high). Must do flap + osseous surgery to move bone apically AND create 4mm distance. → Ostectomy needed
COSLET 2A Minimal KT + Bone normal. → CAUTION. Gingivectomy would remove tissue you can't afford to lose → creates KT deficiency. May need to augment KT (FGG) before or during CL. → MGD: Add KT first
COSLET 2B Minimal KT + Bone AT CEJ. → MOST COMPLEX. Need osseous surgery (move bone) + tissue management (preserve/augment KT). Combined flap approach likely needed. Highest complication risk. → Combined flap
ESSAY 1 Q3 The Coslet classification IS the answer to "when gingivectomy alone vs flap + bone removal." Know all 4 types cold — the exam will probably describe a scenario and you identify which Coslet type it is.
HEALING TIME — How long before placing final restoration?
How long for healing? 6-8-12 weeks or more. Rationale: The relationship of the alveolar crest to the base of the gingival sulcus, with the intervening CT and epithelial attachment, is stable and predictable in the presence of optimal plaque control and effective recall therapy. BW needs time to re-establish. If you prep and impress too early → margin position is unstable → BW hasn't settled → gingival margin will shift → exposed margins, re-inflammation. Posterior vs Anterior: Anterior takes LONGER. More esthetic concern → need tissue to fully mature and stabilize before committing to a final margin position. Posterior is more forgiving. Consequences of insufficient healing time: BW re-establishment continues post-restoration → bone resorption → margin exposure → pocket formation → treatment failure. You'll end up needing to redo the crown AND possibly redo the CL.
LIT Creeping Attachment: Goldman 1964, Matter & Cimasoni 1976: 0.5-1.2mm over 12 months. | Gingival Rebound: Van der Velden 1982: regeneration of interdental soft tissues following denudation.
COMPLICATIONS — Need 3 with remedies
1. Violation of adjacent teeth Bone removal to create 4mm distance affects neighboring teeth too → creates defects on teeth that were healthy. Remedy: Pre-surgical planning with radiographs. Consider orthodontic eruption as alternative if adjacent teeth would be compromised.
2. Root sensitivity Exposed root surfaces post-surgery → dentin hypersensitivity. Remedy: Desensitizing agents (fluoride varnish, potassium nitrate). Time — usually improves as cementum re-mineralizes and tubules occlude.
3. Esthetic compromise Especially anterior — longer clinical crowns, visible root surface, tissue asymmetry, "black triangles" from papilla loss. Remedy: Careful surgical planning. Consider orthodontic alternative. May need gingival prosthetics in severe cases. This is why Fleisher says NO resective surgery anterior unless full prosthetic reconstruction.
Alternative to CL: Orthodontic Forced Eruption Move the tooth coronally with ortho forces, bringing bone and tissue with it (or not, depending on rate of eruption). Then you have more tooth structure above bone level WITHOUT removing bone from adjacent teeth. Useful when CL would damage neighbors. Also: Deep Margin Elevation — conservative technique for restoring teeth with subgingival margins. Composite is placed to elevate the margin to a supragingival position, avoiding CL entirely. Gaining popularity as an alternative. → Ortho + perio: stable reduced periodontium responds normally to ortho

11. GTR / GBR — Essay Topic 2

Same materials, different location, different biology, different timing. The essay tests: GTR concept + landmark studies, cell-root interactions, graft materials, GBR vs GTR differences, complications, barriers, and biologics.

ESSAY 2 Guided Tissue/Bone Regeneration — 9 sub-questions. Must cover: GTR definition, landmark studies (Karring, Nyman), 4 cell type interactions with root surface, graft materials (advantages/disadvantages of each), GBR concept + how it differs from GTR, GBR indications, complications + remedies, barrier types, and biologics comparison (GEM 21, PRP, EMD, BMP).
THE ULTIMATE GOAL IN PERIODONTICS
To reestablish a periodontium to its original state of health. Health (uncompromised) → Disease → Health (compromised) is what we usually achieve. But the dream: Disease → Health (UNCOMPROMISED). In order to obtain uncompromised health in a periodontally diseased dentition, new attachment must take place.
4 KEY TERMS — Know cold for the essay
REATTACHMENT Reconnecting surgically separated HEALTHY tissue. Routine after any flap. The tissue was never diseased — just cut and put back. Not exciting.
Like gluing a clean break in a plate — both sides are intact, you're just reconnecting them.
REPAIR Long Junctional Epithelium (LJE). The body's DEFAULT healing. Epithelium races down the root and sticks with hemidesmosomes. No CT fibers inserting into cementum. Protective but NOT ideal — LJE is fragile and doesn't restore full function.
Like covering a wound with saran wrap — protects it, but it's not real skin.
NEW ATTACHMENT CT fibers inserting into PREVIOUSLY DISEASED root surface (cementum). THIS IS THE GOAL of GTR. Real functional connection between CT and a root that used to be diseased. This is what proves regeneration is possible.
Like growing real new wood grain across a crack in a plank — structural, functional, real.
REGENERATION The biologic process by which the architecture and function of lost tissue is COMPLETELY returned. New cementum + new PDL + new alveolar bone. The complete dream. (Melcher 1969)
New attachment is part of regeneration. Regeneration is the whole package — bone, PDL, cementum, all brand new.
THE 4 CELL TYPES — "THE RACE" (Essay Q2a)
Whichever cell type arrives at the root surface FIRST determines the healing outcome
EPITHELIUM FASTEST. Migrates down root surface rapidly. → Long Junctional Epithelium (LJE) = REPAIR (body's default). Actually protective — prevents other cell types from causing damage. But NOT new attachment.
The sprinter who wins the race but gives you the consolation prize.
GINGIVAL CT FAST (second after epithelium). → ROOT RESORPTION WORST OUTCOME. Gingival CT cells treat root surface as foreign body and resorb it. Catastrophic.
LIT Nyman 1980 (JCP 7:39): Roots implanted into gingival CT → root resorption. Proved gingival CT must be EXCLUDED from root surface.
PDL CELLS MODERATE speed. → NEW ATTACHMENT THE GOAL. PDL cells are the ONLY cells capable of forming new cementum with inserting CT fibers on previously diseased root surface.
LIT Nyman 1982 (JCP 9:257): Millipore filter placed → new cementum with inserting collagen fibers. Proved PDL cells possess the ability to reestablish CT attachment.
BONE CELLS SLOWEST. → ANKYLOSIS Root fuses directly to bone. No PDL space. Tooth becomes non-vital, can't respond to ortho, prone to resorption.
LIT Karring 1980 (JCP 7:96): Roots implanted into bone → no new CT attachment, characterized by root resorption and ankylosis.
GTR = Guided Tissue Regeneration Definition: Allowing certain cell types to participate in the healing process while EXCLUDING other types, for the purpose of promoting new attachment. A membrane (barrier) is placed to BLOCK the fast cells (epithelium + gingival CT) → gives PDL cells time to migrate coronally along the root → new cementum + new PDL + new bone = REGENERATION.
LIT Karring 1980: bone cells on root → resorption/ankylosis. | Nyman 1980: gingival CT on root → resorption. | Nyman 1982: millipore filter → new attachment PROVED. | Nyman 1986 (JCP 13:145): PDL must migrate CORONALLY — elastic placed at defect base blocked coronal migration → no new attachment. Proved directionality of PDL cell migration is critical.
GTR vs GBR — The key conceptual difference
GTR — Around TEETH Goal: new attachment on root surface. Key cells: PDL cells (the only cells that can form new cementum + inserting fibers). Membrane duration: ~4-6 weeks (sufficient for PDL cells to do their work). Success metric: PD reduction 2-4mm, new attachment gain, radiographic bone fill. → Best in 3-wall defects (most contained)
GBR — EDENTULOUS areas Goal: bone VOLUME for implant placement. Key cells: Osteoblasts ONLY (no root surface → no PDL → no new attachment needed). Membrane duration: up to 6 MONTHS (bone formation is SLOW). Success metric: adequate bone height/width for implant. Indications: Immediate implant placement, extraction socket preservation, ridge augmentation. → No wall classification — edentulous ridge
GRAFT MATERIALS — What goes under/with the membrane?
AUTOGRAFT Same patient. GOLD STANDARD. ONLY graft with ALL 3 properties: osteogenic + osteoinductive + osteoconductive. Sources: Intraoral (osseous coagulum, tuberosity, extraction sites = vital cancellous bone = best osteogenic potential). Extraoral (iliac crest — more volume but: second surgical site, root resorption complication, freezing controls resorption). Advantage: No rejection, all 3 properties. Disadvantage: Second surgical site, limited volume intraorally.
Vital cancellous bone contains hematopoietic marrow (tuberosity, recent extraction site) → better osteogenic potential than cortical bone.
LIT Schallhorn: showed effectiveness of iliac bone and marrow autografts. Intra-oral vital sites do NOT cause the same resorptive problems as iliac crest.
ALLOGRAFT Cadaver bone (same species, different individual). FDBA (Mineralized Freeze Dried Bone Allograft) = osteoconductive ONLY. Scaffold function. DFDBA (Demineralized FDBA) = THEORETICALLY osteoinductive (demineralization exposes BMPs per Urist) but UNRELIABLE. Advantage: No second surgical site, off-the-shelf. Disadvantage: Variable osteoinductivity by batch AND by bone bank. No osteogenic cells (non-vital).
LIT Schwartz 1996: examined 14 batches of DFDBA from 6 bone banks — bone from 3 of 6 banks showed NO osteoinductive potential. Even different lots from same bank had different levels. DFDBA osteoinductivity is a gamble.
XENOGRAFT Different species. Bio-Oss® = anorganic bovine bone. Osteoconductive ONLY (scaffold). Excellent for sinus lifts. Prion-free (bone classified as prion-free tissue; undergoes high-temp processing >15hrs + strong alkaline cleaning). Advantage: Consistent quality, abundant supply, excellent scaffold properties. Disadvantage: Osteoconductive only — no cells, no induction.
LIT Urist 1965 (Science): Discovered that demineralized bone implanted in muscle pouches in rabbits induced new bone formation → named Bone Morphogenetic Proteins (BMPs).
ALLOPLAST Synthetic materials. Hydroxyapatite (HA), tricalcium phosphate (TCP), bioactive glass polymers, calcium sulfate (plaster of Paris). Osteoconductive ONLY. Inert biologic fillers. "Unlikely to result in true regeneration." Advantage: No disease transmission risk, unlimited supply, consistent. Disadvantage: Just a scaffold. No biologic activity beyond space maintenance.
OSTEOCONDUCTIVE
= PASSIVE scaffold. Provides structure for bone cells to grow along. Like a trellis for a vine. Most grafts are at least this. Test: does bone grow on it when placed next to existing bone? All grafts pass this test.
OSTEOINDUCTIVE
= ACTIVELY recruits + transforms undifferentiated mesenchymal stem cells INTO osteoblasts. Way more powerful. Test: muscle pouch experiment — if you implant the material into muscle (no bone nearby) and bone STILL forms = osteoinductive. Only autograft reliably passes this. DFDBA theoretically but unreliably.
BARRIER MEMBRANES — Types, advantages, disadvantages
NON-RESORBABLE: GORE-TEX (ePTFE) Made from medical-grade stretched Teflon (polytetrafluoroethylene). Inert — used in vascular surgery and artificial ligaments. Gold standard historically for GTR. Advantages: Predictable barrier function, space-making (titanium-reinforced versions available), well-studied. Disadvantages: REQUIRES SECOND SURGERY for removal. Exposure risk → infection. If exposed → must trim or remove early. Ideal barrier properties: Biocompatible, occlusive (blocks cell migration), space-making, tissue integration, clinical manageability, minimal tissue reaction, no allergic properties, resistance to infection. Duration: Clinical trials show barrier may remain up to 3 months, but sufficient healing occurs 4-6 weeks. If material can't be removed with gentle tug → small incision for access dissection.
RESORBABLE MEMBRANES Collagen membranes (porcine/bovine derived), polylactic acid, polyglycolic acid. Advantages: NO second surgery — membrane dissolves. Simpler for patient. Lower complication rate from no retrieval procedure. Disadvantages: Unpredictable resorption rate (may dissolve too fast before regeneration is complete). May not maintain space as well as ePTFE. Less rigid. Use cases: GBR around implants (collagen membrane + particulate graft + tacs for stabilization), socket preservation.
GTR/GBR COMPLICATIONS — with remedies
Infection Remedy: antibiotics, possibly early membrane removal, debridement.
Membrane perforation/exposure Remedy: trim exposed portion, CHX rinse, monitor. If severe → remove early.
Recession or flap sloughing Remedy: ensure tension-free flap closure at surgery. If occurred → may need soft tissue grafting later.
Gingival irregularities Remedy: gingivoplasty after healing complete to re-establish physiologic contours.
BIOLOGICS — Enhancement agents (Essay Q9)
GEM 21 (rhPDGF-BB + β-TCP) Platelet Derived Growth Factor (recombinant human). Released from β-TCP matrix into surrounding environment → binds cell surface receptors → cascade of intracellular signaling → CHEMOTAXIS (directed cell migration) + MITOGENESIS (cell proliferation) of osteoblasts, PDL fibroblasts, and cementoblasts → increased matrix synthesis → new alveolar bone, new PDL, new cementum. Angiogenesis continues. Fleisher's favorite. Consistent results. Completely synthetic — no disease transmission risk.
GEM 21 is like sending out a GPS signal that tells stem cells "come HERE and turn into bone/PDL/cementum cells." Then gives them the scaffolding to build on.
EMD (Emdogain) Enamel Matrix Derivative. Harvested from porcine tooth buds. Contains amelogenins, ameloblastin, enamelin. In vitro: PDL cells exhibit enhanced protein production and cell proliferation. "Research shows it works." Mimics developmental signals that originally formed the tooth's attachment.
EMD tricks the PDL cells into thinking they're back in embryonic development — "Hey, you're a tooth bud again, start building attachment apparatus!"
PRP (Platelet Rich Plasma) Patient's own blood → centrifuged → platelet concentrate (330-900% above baseline). Contains PDGF, TGF-β, EGF, IGF-1. Combine PRP/gel with bone graft → local concentration of growth factors increased within wound. Bottom line: Faster healing but NO additional bone formation. Soft tissue heals beautifully. Bone results = same as without PRP.
PRP makes the wound heal faster and prettier, but doesn't actually give you MORE bone. Good supplementary, not a game-changer for regeneration.
BMP (Infuse — rhBMP-2) Most potent osteoinductive agent available. Stimulates differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts. BUT: "exuberant inflammatory response" — patient swells MASSIVELY. Fleisher sent it back ("I'm not using this"). Research focused on BMP-2, BMP-3 (osteogenin), BMP-7 (OP1).
BMP is the nuclear option — most powerful but most side effects. Like using a sledgehammer when you needed a finish hammer.

12. Suture Selection & Technique

Where you TIE determines where the tissue ENDS UP. Suture choice connects directly to flap type and procedure. → Flap Classification

SUTURE TECHNIQUES
SIMPLE INTERRUPTED Basic reapproximation. Surgeon's knot: 2+1 = 3 throws minimum. If one fails → others hold (independent).
Each stitch is its own unit — lose one, the rest stay. Like individual buttons vs a zipper.
CONTINUOUS SLING Fleisher: "NEVER use." One continuous thread through multiple sites. If one failure point → TOTAL FAILURE. Entire flap can dehisce.
Like a zipper — one tooth snags and the whole thing comes undone.
VERTICAL MATTRESS THE perio suture. Fleisher's primary choice for APF. Provides mechanical advantage for repositioning flap in any direction. Needle penetrates through flap and through PERIOSTEUM still attached to bone. → Why PTF leaves periosteum as anchor
VERTICAL MATTRESS — Step by step for APF
1. Enter buccal flap JUST apical to MGJ (in alveolar mucosa).
2. ROLL needle → perpendicular to PARALLEL with bone.
3. Grab PERIOSTEUM — this is why you did split thickness (periosteum is left behind ON bone as your anchor).
4. Pass through to lingual, grab lingual tissue.
5. Come back to buccal side.
6. TIE WHERE YOU FIRST ENTERED (apical location).
7. Push DOWN while tying → flap stays in its new apical position.
The suture grabs periosteum like an anchor bolt in a wall, then pulls the flap down to where you want it and holds it there. The knot being APICAL means downward force keeps the flap apically positioned.
MATERIAL — often two types in ONE surgery
FLAP (in KT zone) = NON-RESORBABLE Remove at 1 week. PTFE (monofilament — no wicking, no bacterial migration along thread, premium choice) or Silk (braided — wicks bacteria, cheaper, adequate).
Non-resorbable in KT because you WANT to come back and remove them — lets you check the healing and ensures they don't hang around longer than needed.
VERTICAL RELEASES (in mucosa) = RESORBABLE Dissolves on its own — don't want to dig into vestibular mucosa to remove sutures. Gut (3-4 days), Chromic gut (~6 days), Vicryl (>7 days, braided multifilament), Monocryl (monofilament).
Resorbable in mucosa because it's soft, elastic tissue deep in the vestibule — much harder to access for removal, so let it dissolve.
NEEDLE: REVERSE CUTTING Cutting edge on OUTER curve of the needle. When your hand pulls up, the sharp edge faces AWAY from tissue → inner curve (facing the tissue/flap) is DULL → prevents tear-through. Conventional cutting = cutting edge on INNER curve = BAD — slices through the flap as you pull through.
Reverse cutting is like a seatbelt — the pressure pushes against the smooth side. Conventional cutting is like a cheese wire — it cuts right through what you're trying to hold.
Various needle shapes: half curve, straight needle, 5/8 circle.

13. Mucogingival Defects (MGD) — Dr. Pani

What's wrong → How bad → What caused it → Fix the cause → Then choose technique. Connects to KT decisions (§7), flap types (§8), and crown lengthening (§10).

MGD PATIENT — STEP 1: Identify the condition
Armitage 1999: VIII-B Mucogingival Deformities and Conditions Around Teeth — 7 Categories: 1. Gingival/soft tissue recession (facial/lingual surfaces OR interproximal/papillary). 2. Lack of keratinized gingiva. 3. Decreased vestibular depth. 4. Aberrant frenum/muscle position. 5. Gingival excess (pseudopocket, inconsistent margin, excessive gingival display, gingival enlargement). 6. Abnormal color. 7. (2017 update adds: periodontal biotype — thin scalloped, thick scalloped, thick flat).
STEP 2: Classify recession severity
Miller 1985 → replaced by Cairo Recession Types (RT) in 2017
RT1 (= Miller I + II) Interproximal (IP) attachment INTACT. No IP CAL. No IP bone loss. → 100% root coverage achievable. IP attachment intact = blood supply available for graft = graft will survive.
The papilla between teeth is healthy and tall → graft has a blood supply from both sides → predictable coverage.
RT2 (= Miller III) IP CAL present but ≤ facial/lingual CAL. → PARTIAL coverage possible. Amount of coverage limited by IP bone level.
Some papilla loss → blood supply partially compromised → graft can only survive as far as the remaining IP bone.
RT3 (= Miller IV) IP CAL > facial/lingual CAL. Severe IP bone/tissue loss. → Root coverage NOT predictable.
IP attachment is so far gone that there's no blood supply foundation for a graft. The graft literally has nothing to feed it.
Logic: Interproximal attachment = blood supply for graft. Lost IP = no blood = graft dies at that level. The IP bone level is the ceiling for how much coverage you can achieve.
STEP 3: ELIMINATE ETIOLOGY FIRST — Before ANY surgery
If you skip etiology elimination → even PERFECT surgery fails. Stop aggressive brushing. Fix flossing technique. Eliminate frenal pull (frenectomy). Treat periodontal disease first. Stop factitious habits (nail biting, picking, scratching). SKIP THIS → SURGERY FAILS.
EXAM Pani's closing case: Perfect FGG performed → recession RETURNED → patient was scratching gingiva with fingernails. Factitious habit destroyed the surgical result. Always ask about habits.
Etiology of recession (from Pani's slides): TRAUMA: Tooth brushing, floss, factitious injury, poorly designed partials, direct trauma from malocclusion, chemical trauma. PATHOLOGICAL BONE LOSS: Periodontal disease, associated with treatment of perio disease, smoking. LOCAL PLAQUE RETENTION: High muscle attachment, frenal pull, subgingival margins/BW violation, calculus. ORTHODONTIC MOVEMENTS: Excessive proclination, arch expansion. Bodily tooth movement labially through cortical plate → no bone formation in front of tooth → labial dehiscence created (Steiner 1981, Karring 1982). UNDERLYING BONY DEHISCENCE. → Bone destruction patterns
STEP 4: SELECT TECHNIQUE — Based on goal
Goal: INCREASE KERATINIZED TISSUE → FGG Free Gingival Graft. Best KT gain. Predictable. Rapid. Technique: Recipient site = split thickness incision at MGJ, vertical incisions M/D. Flap may be sutured to depth of vestibule. Donor site = palate, 2mm apical from teeth, graft 0.75-1.25mm thick. Place with sling sutures, no dead space. Healing (Gargiulo & Arocha 1967, Oliver 1968, Nobuto 1988): Phase 1 (0-3d): plasmatic circulation. Phase 2 (2-11d): revascularization — anastomosis between graft and recipient bed vessels, capillary proliferation. Phase 3 (11-42d): maturation — normal vascular system by d14. Advantages: Rapid, predictable, not as technically challenging, combinable with frenectomy. Disadvantages: Poor color match (looks like a patch), two surgical sites, post-op discomfort.
LIT Lang & Löe 1972: min 2mm KT needed. | Maynard & Wilson 1979: min 1mm attached + 1mm free. | Serino 1994: 3mm recession worsened 67%, 4mm worsened 98%. | Chambrone & Tatakis 2016: untreated facial recession with good OH highly likely to increase over time.
Goal: ROOT COVERAGE → CTG + CAF Connective Tissue Graft + Coronally Advanced Flap. Gold standard for esthetics. Technique: Recipient = split thickness incisions, horizontal sulcular incisions, intact IP tissue. Donor = palate (double incision with epithelium OR single incision without). CTG placed under the flap → can be completely covered (better esthetics, allows thin grafts to survive) or partially covered (maintains MGJ, allows keratinization). Flap coronally positioned + sutured to IP papilla + to graft. Advantages: Good color blend, can increase KT zone and thickness, predictable coverage in Miller I/II (RT1). Disadvantages: Requires adequate vestibular depth, two surgical sites, post-op discomfort, papilla on either side may be altered cosmetically.
LIT Langer & Langer 1985: described the CTG technique. | Wilderman & Wentz 1965: Pedicle graft healing — adaptation stage (0-4d, fibrin layer), proliferation (4-21d, CT invades fibrin, fibroblasts on root by d6-10), attachment (d27-28, thick collagen fibers insert in cementum), maturation (2-3mo, continuous collagen formation).
CAF Requirements (Bernimoulin 1981): Min tissue thickness 0.8mm. Shallow recession (<4mm). Miller Class I (RT1). KT attached >3mm. Minimal to no flap tension. Flap placed 2.5mm coronal to CEJ. Split-full-split design (3mm split zones M/D). → Combined flap = split-full-split
PEDICLE GRAFTS — Tissue moved from adjacent site
LATERALLY REPOSITIONED FLAP Split thickness flap from papilla D/M to recession site, slid over to cover. Pedicle (attached base) provides blood supply. Need adequate KT adjacent to recession.
LIT Grupe & Warren 1956. De Waal 1988.
DOUBLE PAPILLA FLAP Papilla from each side of recession tooth moved to facial/lingual aspect. Dual blood supply. Split thickness. No destruction of IP papilla on either side.
LIT Cohen DW, Ross SE 1968.
SEMILUNAR CORONALLY POSITIONED FLAP Apical semilunar incision 2mm from papilla (sufficient distance for vascularity). Partial thickness flap coronally advanced. Advantages: No vestibule shortening, good color, papilla unchanged cosmetically. Disadvantages: Requires adequate KT zone + thickness, does NOT increase KT, doesn't account for "hidden recession."
LIT Tarnow 1986. Allen & Miller 1989 (citric acid root conditioning).
ALLODERM (Acellular Dermal Matrix) Human dermis — matrix of collagen, elastin, vascular channels, protein that supports revascularization. Processed to remove epidermis and cells (no tissue rejection). MUST be completely covered by flap — if exposed → necrotizes + smells terrible. CTG can survive partial exposure (Alloderm cannot). Hydrate 15-20 min before placement. Prepare site 3mm apically to papilla tip, leave one papilla intact. Sling sutures for stability. Contraindication: Allergy to gentomycin. → Flap must cover completely
ALTERED PASSIVE ERUPTION — Coslet 1977 When gingival margin stays too far coronally during eruption → clinical appearance of "short teeth" / "gummy smile." This classification directly answers Essay 1 Q3 (when gingivectomy alone vs flap + bone). → Crown Lengthening: Coslet 1A/1B/2A/2B

14. Occlusal Trauma — Essay Topic 4

This is the most conceptually tricky essay. Does TFO cause attachment loss? (NO.) Is mobility disease? (NOT necessarily.) Does TFO need treatment? (DEPENDS.) → Bone destruction pathway 3

ESSAY 4 Occlusal Trauma — 7 sub-questions: Define TFO, diagnose primary vs secondary, relationship with/without perio disease, treatment for each type, is mobility disease?, mobility vs fremitus, does TFO cause attachment loss?
WHAT IS TRAUMA FROM OCCLUSION?
Occlusal Traumatism: A term used to describe pathologic or adaptive changes which develop in the periodontium as a result of undue force produced by the masticatory muscles. (AAP Glossary 2001) Any force applied to a tooth has the potential to produce a pathologic effect on the attachment apparatus. The pathologic lesion causes loss of alveolar and crestal lamina dura → widening of the PDL → appears clinically as increasing mobility over time.
TFO = the INJURY to the attachment apparatus from excessive force. Not the force itself — the damage the force causes.
CRITICAL DIAGNOSTIC CRITERION: You must have INCREASING mobility over time to diagnose occlusal trauma. Cracked teeth, fractures, worn teeth, abrasion ALONE are not enough. Mobility must be documented as INCREASING across appointments. What about wear? Pathologic tooth wear is NOT the same as occlusal trauma. A tooth can have extensive wear without mobility (force didn't exceed attachment apparatus threshold). Extent of dental wear may NOT be directly related to extent of the lesion in the attachment apparatus.
LIGHT FORCE vs HEAVY FORCE
LIGHT FORCE — Within adaptive range Force is constant but within periodontium's capacity to adapt. Occlusal traumatic lesion in attachment apparatus resolves over time. PDL widens permanently to compensate. Healing occurs but PDL remains widened. Clinically: Tooth has INCREASED mobility (wider PDL), but mobility is NOT INCREASING — it plateaued. The system adapted. If force is REMOVED: PDL returns to original width. Bone apposition at lamina dura. Mobility returns to pre-force baseline. Completely reversible.
HEAVY FORCE — Exceeds adaptive capacity Force exceeds the adaptive capacity of the attachment apparatus. The tooth can NEVER adapt. Mobility continues to INCREASE over time. PDL keeps widening. No healing plateau. This is pathologic — requires intervention.
PRIMARY vs SECONDARY OCCLUSAL TRAUMA
PRIMARY OCCLUSAL TRAUMA EXCESSIVE force applied to a periodontium with NORMAL amount of support. The bone and attachment are healthy — the force is just too damn much. Lesion is REVERSIBLE and REPAIRABLE. Treatment: Reduce the force. Occlusal equilibration (selective grinding to redistribute forces). Fix the bite. Once forces are acceptable → attachment apparatus heals, PDL returns to pre-force width, mobility reduces. Clinical presentation: Increasing mobility + migration + fremitus. Radiographic: widened PDL, possibly angular bone loss appearance (don't confuse with perio disease bone loss).
LIT Reinhart, Pao, Krejeci 1984: PDL stresses in initiation of occlusal traumatism.
SECONDARY OCCLUSAL TRAUMA NORMAL force applied to a periodontium WEAKENED by periodontal disease. The force is normal but the support is gone — like standing on a rotten floorboard. Lesion is usually NOT reversible or repairable. Caused by: Loss of adequate bone support (from perio disease), not excessive force. Treatment: SPLINTING. The splinting compensates for the lost support, distributing normal forces across multiple teeth. You MUST have attachment loss to have secondary occlusal trauma (by definition — it requires a weakened periodontium). BUT: You can still have primary occlusal trauma even if the tooth HAS attachment loss (if the force is also excessive).
THE BIG QUESTIONS — Essay gold
Does TFO cause attachment loss? NO. Occlusal trauma does NOT cause (initiate) attachment loss, pocketing, or recession. Effects of occlusal force on the periodontium are LIMITED to the attachment apparatus (PDL + bone). The supracrestal soft tissues (JE + CT attachment) are NOT damaged by excessive force. TFO does not initiate perio disease. → Bone destruction: TFO pathway
Is mobility DISEASE? Does it always need treatment? NO. A certain increase in mobility can be accepted, provided the mobile tooth does not disturb masticatory function or patient comfort. Increased mobility ≠ increasing mobility. If mobility is stable (increased but not increasing) → the tooth adapted → no treatment needed. Only treat mobility when it is INCREASING (pathologic) or when it interferes with function/comfort. Mobility grading: Physiologic = ≤0.2mm. Grade 1 = 0.2-1.0mm. Grade 2 = 1.0-2.0mm. Grade 3 = >2.0mm and/or vertical displacement.
Mobility vs Fremitus — Know the distinction Mobility: Side-to-side movement of the tooth when you push on it with instruments. Tests the PDL width/integrity. Fremitus: Vibration or movement of a tooth detected by placing a finger on the facial surface while the patient taps their teeth together in FUNCTION. Tests whether the tooth is receiving excessive force DURING occlusal function. A tooth can have mobility without fremitus (wide PDL but no occlusal interference) or fremitus without significant mobility (occlusal prematurity on a stable tooth).
TFO + Active Periodontal Disease = CO-DESTRUCTION A relatively HEAVY force combined with an ACTIVE periodontal infection can be co-destructive — exacerbating the destructive rate of the infection. This is the Glickman concept (Zone of Irritation vs Zone of Co-Destruction — unproven theory). Clinically, heavy force is often related to orthodontic treatment context. BUT: Animal studies show that occlusal trauma in a healthy but reduced periodontium (successfully treated perio case, no active inflammation) has NO different effect than TFO on a healthy periodontium with normal bone/attachment levels. Patients with bone loss but no significant marginal inflammation after definitive therapy respond to orthodontics normally.
TFO is only co-destructive when combined with ACTIVE infection. Once you treat the infection → the reduced periodontium is stable → TFO alone won't cause additional breakdown. This is crucial for ortho planning in perio patients.
→ TFO under bone destruction
Treatment Summary for Occlusal Trauma: A. Elimination of inflammation (treat the perio disease first — always). B. Elimination or control of occlusal etiology (equilibration, ortho, occlusal splint). C. Development of stable intra- and inter-arch relationships. Treatment may necessitate: occlusal equilibration, limited or complete orthodontics, prosthetics (splinting). Remember: etiologic agents or adverse environment does NOT guarantee an occlusal traumatic lesion — much depends on the nature of forces and the individual's resistance and repair capacity. Posterior Bite Collapse: A syndrome (not a disease entity) — missing posterior teeth → super-eruption → loss of contact → drifting → loss of posterior support → flaring of anterior teeth → attachment loss + additional occlusal trauma. This is the cascade you need to prevent with prosthetic rehabilitation.

15. NSPT/Surgical Therapy — Essay Topic 3

This essay walks through the entire treatment decision-making process. When is SRP indicated? When does surgery become necessary? What does success look like? → Treatment Pipeline → NSPT/SPT

ESSAY 3 Non-Surgical / Surgical Periodontal Therapy — 10 sub-questions including: SRP indications, definition, goals + evidence, findings that trigger surgery, rationale for adjunctive antimicrobials, success criteria, patient case scenario (75yo with 5-7mm PD), and assumptions we make about perio treatment.
Q1-3: SRP — When, What, Why
When is SRP indicated? Clinical findings: BOP at sites, PD >3mm with CAL, subgingival calculus. Radiographic findings: bone loss visible on PA radiographs. Diagnosis of periodontitis confirmed (not just gingivitis). SRP is the FIRST step for any periodontitis patient. It's both diagnostic (how does the patient respond?) and therapeutic. SRP differs from scaling: Scaling removes deposits. Root planing specifically instruments the root surface — removing contaminated cementum and creating a biologically compatible surface. Scaling is cleaning; root planing is preparing the foundation for healing. Goals + evidence of success: Reduce subgingival microbial load, remove endotoxin, reduce inflammation, allow tissue to shrink and reattach. Success is measured by PD reduction, CAL stability or gain, BOP resolution. | Lindhe 1984: Patients with high standard of home care maintained CAL + PD reductions more consistently than patients with poor plaque control over 5 years. → Full NSPT details
Q4: What triggers surgical therapy?
Persistent PD ≥5mm + BOP after Phase I (SRP + re-eval at 4-6 weeks) The rationale: Caffesse 1986 showed 65% inadequate debridement at ≥5mm. Lindhe showed critical PD of 4.2mm — above this, surgery provides better outcomes than SRP alone. "Closed" procedures have inherent limitations. Surgical therapy provides: direct access for debridement, ability to modify bone architecture, ability to reposition tissue for pocket elimination, ability to place regenerative materials. Objectives of Perio Surgery: 1) Gain access for effective removal of etiologic factors following SRP. 2) Reduce pocket depth, decreasing places bacteria adhere, making it more maintainable. → Pipeline: re-eval gate → Osseous surgery
Q5: Adjunctive Antimicrobials
Rationale: when local measures aren't sufficient Systemic: Amoxicillin + Metronidazole for aggressive/refractory forms. Azithromycin for aggressive periodontitis (concentrates in perio tissues). Periostat (sub-antimicrobial dose doxycycline — host modulation, inhibits collagenase). Local: Arestin, Atridox, Perio Chip, Actisite — for non-responsive sites after SRP. Placed subgingivally at specific sites ≥5mm + BOP that didn't respond to mechanical debridement alone. CHX (Chlorhexidine/Peridex): Gold standard oral antiseptic. BUT: cationic — forms salts with anionic detergents like SLS in toothpaste. Even 30min interval between SLS toothpaste and CHX rinsing significantly reduced antiplaque effect. Instruct patients: rinse 30+ min after brushing. Listerine: Essential oils. OTC alternative. Less effective than CHX but no staining. → LAAs and chemotherapeutics
Q6: What does SUCCESS look like?
Clinical outcomes that define successful therapy: PD reduction to ≤3-4mm. Resolution of BOP. Stable or improved CAL. No suppuration. Radiographic evidence of bone stability or fill. Patient able to maintain with effective home care. Smooth, hard root surfaces on re-evaluation. Patient enrolled in SPT q3mo with stable findings over time.
LIT Rosling 1976: With recall, patients maintained surgically reduced PD + gained attachment. | Nyman 1977: Without recall, PD rebounded regardless of technique. | Lindhe 1984: High OH patients maintained results over 5 years. | Isidor & Karring 1986: Professional cleaning q3mo compensated for variations in home care.
Q7: THE PATIENT SCENARIO
75yo healthy patient, 1 small carious lesion #28D, generalized BOP, generalized PD 5-7mm, minimal recession, gingival margin 1-2mm coronal to CEJ, 28 teeth, mobility ≤1, sporadic dental visits, occasional cleanings. Analysis: Generalized 5-7mm PD with minimal recession + margin coronal to CEJ means LOTS of attachment loss hidden by tissue that hasn't receded (deep true pockets, likely infrabony component interproximally). Generalized BOP = active inflammation everywhere. Mobility ≤1 = not severe attachment loss yet but getting there. Treatment plan: 1. Comprehensive exam + full mouth radiographic series. 2. OHI — aggressive patient education. 3. Full mouth SRP (D4341 all quads). 4. Re-eval 4-6 weeks after last quad. 5. Non-responsive sites → LAAs or surgical therapy. 6. D4910 SPT q3mo for life. Would recommendations differ at 30yo? YES — same clinical findings at 30 = much worse prognosis. Suggests aggressive/rapid progression (Stage III or IV, Grade C). Would consider systemic antibiotics adjunctively, more aggressive surgical approach, evaluate for genetic/systemic risk factors. The disease has progressed this far in HALF the time → rate of progression is the key difference. Assumptions we make when deciding treatment is indicated: That the patient will comply with home care. That the patient will return for SPT. That treatment will stabilize or improve the condition. That benefits outweigh risks. That the patient understands and accepts the treatment plan.
Q9-10: Pseudopocket vs Real + Attachment Terminology
Pseudopocket vs real pocket — in precise terms: Pseudopocket: gingival margin coronal to CEJ + PD >3mm BUT no CAL (probe doesn't extend past CEJ). Radiograph shows normal bone levels. Treatment: address cause of tissue excess (OH, gingivectomy, or monitor). True pocket: probe extends past CEJ = CAL confirmed. Radiograph shows bone loss. Suprabony if pocket bottom above bone crest, infrabony if below. Key: PD alone is NOT sufficient. Must locate gingival margin relative to CEJ. Must confirm with radiograph. Neither probing NOR radiograph alone is enough. → Full pocket type breakdown
Terminology (Essay Q10): a) Loss of attachment = distance from CEJ to base of pocket. Measured as recession + PD (if recession present) or PD minus distance of margin coronal to CEJ. Represents destruction of the connection between tooth and periodontium. b) Attachment apparatus = the structures that attach the tooth to bone: cementum, PDL fibers, alveolar bone. This is what TFO affects and what perio disease destroys. c) Attached gingiva = the band of keratinized tissue that is firmly bound to underlying bone/periosteum. Measured from sulcus base to MGJ (NOT from gingival margin — that includes free gingiva). Different from total KT width. → TFO affects attachment apparatus only → KT assessment
