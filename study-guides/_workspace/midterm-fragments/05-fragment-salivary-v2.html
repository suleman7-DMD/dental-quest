2. MALT Lymphoma Risk in Sjögren's 🔥 EXPLICIT · 🩺 BOARDS LOVE THIS

Prof said: "You have to know this. They love asking this question on the boards. This is a critical complication."
  • Lifetime risk: 5–10% of non-Hodgkin lymphoma
  • Specific type: Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue (MALT) lymphoma
  • 7–19× higher risk than general population (verbally emphasized)
  • Highest risk of lymphoma among ALL autoimmune diseases (verbally emphasized)

Red flags requiring urgent rheumatology/hematology referral (know all 4):

  1. Persistent unilateral gland enlargement
  2. Lymphadenopathy
  3. Purpura
  4. Constitutional symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss)

4. Radiation-Induced Hyposalivation — Caries Pattern 🔥 "BOARDS LOVE TO ASK"

Prof said: "You need to know the high risk caries pattern for patients who've had radiation... They love to ask about this on boards."

All 6 features are yellow-highlighted on slide:

  1. High caries risk pattern (parent bullet)
  2. Ipsilateral (same side as radiation)
  3. Cervical (location — starts at gumline)
  4. Facial to circumferential (progression pattern)
  5. Smooth surfaces of mandibular anterior dentition
  6. Maxillary molars (where parotid saliva normally washes)

Radiation thresholds (critical facts):

  • Parotid gland: irreversible damage at >25 Gy
  • Submandibular: irreversible damage at >39 Gy
  • Typical tumor dose: 65–70 Gy (always exceeds gland thresholds)

Management highlights: Aggressive fluoride protocol (daily 1.1% NaF gel), short recall intervals (3–4 months), CHX rinses (5-minute contact time), calcium phosphate saliva rinse/cream for remineralization.

🩺 Notecard #4: "Radiation caries: ipsilateral, cervical (facial→circumferential), mandib ant smooth surfaces, max molars. Parotid >25Gy, submandib >39Gy, tumor 65–70Gy."

10. Vaseline/Aquaphor vs. Lanolin HPA 🔥 "THE HILL I WILL DIE ON"

Prof said: "This is the hill I will die on. Stop giving your patients Vaseline and Aquaphor."

Avoid: Caffeine, Alcohol (beverages and mouthwash), Vaseline, Aquaphor

  • Vaseline/Aquaphor = fully occlusive → makes lips drier, worsens candidiasis, flammable

Over the Counter (OTC — recommended):

  • Hydration (+/- pineapple), Bedside humidifier, Sugar-free lemon candies
  • Xylimelts, Xerostrips, Biotene products
  • Lanolin HPA — purified anhydrous lanolin; safe for infants, lips; avoids allergenicity risk of regular lanolin; only contraindication = true wool allergy
🩺 Notecard #10: "Avoid: Vaseline/Aquaphor (occlusive, worsens candidiasis, flammable). Use: Lanolin HPA (Lansinoh)."

T2-1. Sjögren Overview — Demographics & Classic Presentation

  • Autoimmune destruction of salivary and lacrimal glands
  • Primary Sjögren: occurs alone (glands only)
  • Secondary Sjögren: occurs WITH other autoimmune disease (RA, SLE — the two named ones)
  • Demographics: middle-aged females, 9:1 female:male ratio, prevalence 0.5–1% of population
  • Age of diagnosis typically in 30s–40s (verbally stated — not on slide)
  • Classic presentation: Hyposalivation +/- xerostomia, Xerophthalmia (dry eyes), Parotid enlargement (bilateral), Middle-aged female, Autoimmune comorbidity
  • Must exclude: Head/neck radiation, Active hepatitis C, Sarcoidosis, IgG4-related disease, Recent anticholinergic use

T2-2. Hyposalivation Epidemiology + Top 3 Causes

Venn diagram key stats:

  • Hyposalivation alone: 40–60%
  • Xerostomia alone: 20–42%
  • Both together: 16–27%
  • Xerostomia without hyposalivation = nerve problem, not gland problem (patients lose 40–50% of flow before feeling dry)
Three teal-colored top causes of Hyposalivation (visually differentiated on slide):
  1. Anxiety
  2. Polypharmacy
  3. Dehydration
Prof said: "The three main questions you need to ask... are you anxious? Do you take many medications, and are you dehydrated?"

Less common causes: Poorly controlled DM, End-stage renal disease, Salivary gland damage (radiation, surgery)

⚡ Demote — uncommon (beyond student diagnostic scope): Parkinson disease, GVHD, IRAEs, Sjögren syndrome, Sarcoidosis, GPA, IgG4-related disease, Chronic hepatitis C, HIV.

T2-3. Salivary Gland Cell Types + Waterfall Mnemonic

Prof: "I think of it as the top of the waterfall, fast flow... I think about that lagoon at the bottom."
  • Parotid: "Serous glands" → Serous = thin, watery, fast flow (top of waterfall)
  • Submandibular: "Serous + Mucous glands" (mixed)
  • Sublingual: "Mucous glands" → Mucous = thick, syrupy, slow flow (lagoon at bottom)

Hyposalivation: Submandibular and sublingual glands are usually affected first. Water loss >> mucin loss → concentration → sticky/ropy/foamy saliva.

Key clinical signs of hyposalivation: sticky, ropy, foamy saliva; floor-of-mouth glands go first.

T2-4. Cevimeline and Bethanechol

Cevimeline (Evoxac):

  • Selective for M1 and M3 (fewer systemic side effects than pilocarpine)
  • Dose: 30 mg TID
  • Mnemonic: "E-I-E-I" (Old MacDonald) — "You'll never forget it now"

Bethanechol (Urecholine):

  • Third-line sialagogue (when pilocarpine + cevimeline fail)
  • Selective for M2 and M3
  • Dose: 25 mg TID
  • Origin: used for post-surgical urinary retention; found to increase salivation as side effect

T2-5. Challacombe Scale — 10-Point Dryness Classification

Named for: Steven Challacombe, King's College London, cornerstone of oral medicine (name likely testable).

Severity tiers (purple/lavender row banding on slide):

  • Mild (1–3) — lightest shading
  • Moderate (4–6) — medium shading
  • Severe (7–10) — darkest shading

The 10 clinical features:

  1. Mirror sticks to buccal mucosa
  2. Mirror sticks to tongue
  3. Saliva frothy
  4. No saliva pooling in floor of mouth
  5. Tongue shows generalised shortened papillae (mild depapillation)
  6. Altered gingival architecture (smooth)
  7. Glassy appearance of oral mucosa, especially palate
  8. Tongue lobulated/fissured
  9. Cervical caries (>2 teeth)
  10. Debris on palate or sticking to teeth
🩺 Notecard #14: "Challacombe: 1–3=mild, 4–6=moderate, 7–10=severe. 10 features (mirror, frothy, pooling, papillae, gingival, glassy palate, lobulated tongue, cerv caries, debris)"

T2-6. Chairside Assessment Structure

Three-step assessment framework:

  1. History → Medical History (radiation, autoimmune), Medications, Symptoms (duration, other body sites, difficulty swallowing)
  2. Exam → Challacombe Scale (10 features)
  3. Objective Testing (when indicated) → Unstimulated whole saliva flow, Stimulated flow, Consider referral for additional testing
❓ Knowledge Check answer (slide 16): "Measure salivary flow to distinguish xerostomia from hyposalivation"
65-year-old with dry mouth complaint but normal salivary pooling on exam → normal pooling + subjective dryness = likely xerostomia (nerve problem) → confirm with flow rate.

T2-7. Acute Bacterial Sialadenitis

Clinical signs:

  • Painful swollen gland (unilateral)
  • Fever
  • Purulence at duct orifice
  • Erythema
  • Firm, tender gland

Common organisms:

  • Staphylococcus aureus (most common)
  • Streptococcus species
  • Anaerobes

Management:

  • First-line antibiotics: Amoxicillin-clavulanate or clindamycin ("Cover Staph aureus")
  • Aggressive hydration
  • Culture if purulent discharge present
  • Imaging for obstruction (stone? mass?)
Urgent referral (ED/ENT/OMFS) if:
  • Systemic spread/sepsis
  • Immunocompromised patient
  • Trismus/severe swelling
  • Airway compromise concern
🩺 Notecard #15: "Sialadenitis: Staph aureus (MC), Strep spp, anaerobes. Tx: amox-clav or clindamycin. Urgent refer if: sepsis, immunocomp, trismus, airway."

T2-8. Mucocele Classic Presentation + Pathophysiology

Classic presentation:

  • Lower lip most common site (60–70%)
  • Fluctuant swelling
  • Bluish color (Tyndall effect — light scattering through thin mucosa into mucin pool)
  • History of trauma (lip biting), sharp canines
  • Painless

Pathophysiology: Trauma to minor salivary gland duct → mucin spillage into connective tissue → extravasation phenomenon

  • Extravasation type = mucin OUTSIDE duct (trauma/spillage)
  • Retention type = mucin INSIDE duct (obstruction) — can coexist with sialolith
  • Treatment is basically the same: excision
🩺 Prof pearl: Her own mucoceles from sharp canines → mother's dentist ground down canines + placed veneers → resolution.

T2-9. Sialolithiasis Management

Conservative first-line:

  • Hydration (2L/day)
  • Gland massage
  • Warm/moist heat compresses
  • OTC saliva products
  • NSAIDs or acetaminophen for pain
  • Muscarinic receptor agonist therapy (pilocarpine)
When to refer for sialoendoscopy:
  • Stone >2 mm in proximal duct
  • Failed conservative management after 1–2 weeks
  • Recurrent symptoms
  • Success rate: 85–92% gland preservation

Large/recurrent stones: Refer for sialoendoscopy → surgical removal if needed

T2-10. Tubarial Salivary Glands — "New Glands!"

Large font slide title: "New Glands!" (exclamation mark, visual emphasis)
  • Discovered 2021 in Netherlands: Valstar MH, et al. 2021. Radiother Oncol. 154:292–298.
  • Article: "The tubarial salivary glands: A potential new organ at risk for radiotherapy"
  • Location: nasopharynx / nasal turbinate area (torus tubarius region)
  • Function: contribute to posterior oral saliva flow
  • Clinical implication (testable): Spare tubarial glands during radiation planning to preserve salivary function
⚡ Demote — discovery concept testable; Valstar author/journal volume NOT testable.

T2-11. Hyposalivation — Sticky/Ropy/Foamy Saliva Signs

Slide header (large font, white-on-black): "Hyposalivation: submandibular and sublingual glands are usually affected first"

Mechanism:

  • "Water loss >> mucin loss" (>> confirmed in text dump)
  • Remaining mucins become concentrated in a smaller volume of fluid
  • Mucins become altered → saliva less lubricating, more stringy, more sticky
  • Result: sticky, ropy, foamy saliva = clinical hallmark of hyposalivation

T3-1. Why Dentists Should Care About Saliva
Section divider. Clinical context: dentists are primary detectors of dry mouth because patients rarely spontaneously report it. When saliva production drops, all 10 functions break down simultaneously — dental protection, antimicrobial defense, digestion, wound healing, and more. This is why "dry mouth" is clinically dangerous, not just uncomfortable.

T3-2. Practice Case — 77-year-old with Floor-of-Mouth Cancer

Patient: 77 y.o. male with DM type 2 (metformin, no insulin), CIDP, OSA, CAD, CVA, psoriatic arthritis, gout, BPH, anemia, glaucoma. CC: Tongue pain.

Prior histopathology: "Squamous mucosa with ulceration and granulation tissue. Negative for dysplasia." — From non-OMAX pathologist; language doesn't say "reactive" = red flag.

Case teaching (case MCQ fodder):

  1. "Negative for dysplasia" from non-specialist pathologist ≠ reassuring
  2. Steroids reduced pain → still red/white → second injection, no improvement
  3. Then: bleeding → spontaneous hemorrhage = new vessel formation = cancer until proven otherwise
  4. Re-biopsy by oral surgery → cancer confirmed (floor of mouth + alveolar ridge)

Rule: Failed steroids ×2 OR spontaneous bleeding → cancer until proven otherwise → re-biopsy + urgent referral.

T3-7. Mucocele Surgical Technique (verbal)

Prof described own technique: "like a C-section" — cut over swelling, blunt dissect, deliver intact.

Structures to avoid: labial artery (hemorrhage risk, "blood fountain"), labial nerve (numbness), labial vein (blue thing = vein, white thing = nerve).

Must excise feeding glands to prevent recurrence.

Dentist performs: pick out at least 5 clusters of minor salivary glands from lip.

Histopathologist looks for lymphocyte aggregates.
