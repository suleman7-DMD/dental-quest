Tier 3 Background — RAS Epidemiology & Pathophysiology
  • Most common oral ulcerative condition (~20% of population; range 5–66%). [Slide 4]
  • 80% experience first ulcer before age 30; 40% positive family history.
  • Mechanism: CD8+ T cells target nonkeratinized mucosa. [Slide 5]
  • Nutritional screen (order only when clinically suspected): B12, folic acid, iron/ferritin, Vit D, Zinc. Supplement ONLY AS NEEDED. [Slide 5]
  • Medication-induced RAS: NSAIDs, beta-blockers, bisphosphonates, nicorandil.
  • Systemic associations (Celiac, IBD, Behcet, cyclic neutropenia, PFAPA, HIV) — covered in separate systemic unit.
  • Silver nitrate cautery — AVOID (risk of massive necrosis and argyria).
  • Refractory cases: refer to oral medicine (thalidomide, colchicine, dapsone, apremilast — for specialist use).

If the lesion moves location over days, it cannot be cancer. This is the most clinically reassuring feature of geographic tongue. [Slide 12]
⚠️ TRAP 1 — White serpiginous border ≠ candidiasis. Candida scrapes off with gauze. Geographic tongue border does NOT scrape off and does NOT respond to antifungals. [Slide 12]

💊 BOARDS LOVE THIS SECTION — KNOW THESE DRUGS [Slide 14]
🎙️ Prof explicitly told students that boards love asking about drug-induced gingival hyperplasia and the specific drugs responsible — she recommended memorizing them completely.

🎙️ Prof described these three drug classes as ones to commit to memory completely — calling phenytoin out as the single most important drug in the list.
⏱️ ONSET: 1–3 months after starting the medication — classic exam timeline.
Distribution: begins at interdental papillae → spreads anteriorly and facially. Edentulous areas spared. [Slide 14]
🎙️ Prof gave this onset timeline as a direct exam target — 1-3 months and starting at the interdental papillae are the details she expects students to know.

Management Ladder [Slide 15]

STEP 1: METICULOUS PLAQUE CONTROL = #1 INTERVENTION
Must optimize oral hygiene BEFORE any other step. Surgery without prior hygiene = ~35% recurrence within 18 months.
⚠️ TRAP (F11): Do NOT go straight to gingivectomy. Surgery without correcting the causative drug and optimizing hygiene = the tissue grows right back.
  1. Plaque control (meticulous, daily)
  2. Antimicrobial rinse: chlorhexidine 0.12% (if CHX allergy → povidone-iodine/Betadine; shellfish allergy = iodine contraindicated)
  3. Coordinate with prescribing physician — suggest drug substitution (e.g., nifedipine → felodipine; CCB → ACE inhibitor/ARB). Never change unilaterally. Regression takes 6 months–1 year.
  4. Gingivectomy — LAST RESORT only after hygiene optimized + drug addressed
Tier 3 — DRGH Risk Factors & Drug Interactions
  • Risk factors: poor plaque control, male sex (3×), young age, higher drug dose, smoking. [Slide 14]
  • Cyclosporine + nifedipine = synergistic effect (transplant patients at highest risk for severe overgrowth).

✅ BENIGN + SELF-LIMITED — painful erythematous/whitish papules on fungiform papillae of tip/dorsolateral tongue. Resolves spontaneously in 2–15 days (mean ~7 days).

TIER 1

Oral Lichen Planus (OLP)

OLP is a chronic T-cell mediated AUTO-INFLAMMATORY disorder — NOT autoimmune. This distinction matters for patient counseling: patients with "autoimmune" diagnoses often become alarmed about life-altering systemic implications that do not apply here. [Slide 31]
🎙️ Prof stated this distinction beyond the slide content — "autoimmune" is the wrong classification; "auto-inflammatory" is correct on her exam.
Prof's Position on Malignant Potential (Category G — likely exam target): Standard teaching (Neville) classifies OLP as an oral potentially malignant disorder (OPMD). Prof's updated position: OLP may NOT be a true OPMD. Cancers attributed to OLP transformation were likely dysplasia that was misdiagnosed as OLP from the start. Reference: Wu (2019), Head and Neck Pathology (Harvard). [Slides 31–32]
On this professor's exam: "OLP is OPMD" = nuanced/controversial answer, not a clear yes.
📍 PATHOGNOMONIC LOCATION: Bilateral posterior buccal mucosa + ventral tongue with Wickham striae
Bilateral = reassuring for OLP. Unilateral or atypical site = BIOPSY NOW. [Slide 31]
🎙️ Prof noted that the pathognomonic bilateral location is a key exam feature — she expects students to use bilaterality as a reassuring sign and flag unilateral presentations for biopsy.
📖 Wickham Striae = the lacy white reticulations of OLP. These white lacy lines define the reticular form and are the clinical/histopathologic signature finding of OLP. [Slide 31]

Mucous Membrane Pemphigoid (MMP)

🧠 Hades Mnemonic — pemphigoiD lives DEEP
"Hades lives deep in the underworld. PemphigoiD has a D — think DEEP. The cleavage is at the basement membrane zone, down below the epithelium." [Slide 37]
PemphigoiD
= DEEP (BMZ)
Like Hades — deep underground
PemphiGus
= HIGH UP (intraepithelial)
Like Pegasus — soaring above
MMP = Autoimmune SUBEPITHELIAL Blistering Disease [Slide 36]
Autoantibodies target BMZ proteins: BP180 · BP230 · type VII collagen · laminin 332
🦷 Most Common Oral Presentation = DESQUAMATIVE GINGIVITIS
Oral cavity involved in ~85% of MMP. Erythematous, eroded, peeling gingiva. [Slide 36]
👁️ REFER ALL MMP → OPHTHALMOLOGY — EVEN IF ASYMPTOMATIC
Ocular involvement: 61–70% of patients. Early symptoms: burning · dryness · foreign body sensation.
Conjunctival scarring (symblepharon) = IRREVERSIBLE → BLINDNESS if missed. [Slide 39]
🧠 Pemphigoid has two i's in the word → "two eyes." This disease involves the eyes. [Slide 37]
🎙️ Prof described the ophthalmology referral rule as a "do not miss" — 60–70% of patients have ocular involvement and the scarring is irreversible. She expects students to remember ALL patients, even asymptomatic ones.
Anti-Laminin 332 (+) → ORDER CANCER SCREEN IMMEDIATELY [Slide 38]
If DIF is positive, send serology for anti-laminin 332. 25% of anti-laminin 332-positive patients ALREADY have an occult adenocarcinoma. This is a life-saving diagnostic step.
🎙️ Prof said "already have it" twice for emphasis — anti-laminin 332 should immediately trigger a malignancy workup.
Nikolsky Sign = NEGATIVE in MMP — subepithelial blister is tense; harder to elicit. Contrast with PV (positive). [Slide 37]
🔬 BIOPSY PROTOCOL — 2 SPECIMENS REQUIRED [Slide 38]
Biopsy perilesional tissue (normal-appearing tissue adjacent to blister).
  1. H&E histology → 10% formalin
  2. DIF immunofluorescence → Michel's solution (formalin DESTROYS immunoglobulins needed for DIF)
⚠️ TRAP (F15): Putting all tissue in formalin = destroys DIF = cannot diagnose. Two vials required.
MMP DIF Pattern: Linear IgG, IgA, and/or C3 at basement membrane zone. [Slide 38]
Contrast: PV = "chicken wire" intercellular pattern. Same antibodies — different LOCATION.
🎙️ Prof said boards love to ask about IgA, IgG, and C3 in the context of immunobullous diseases — the key is that the location differs: linear at BMZ = MMP, intercellular = PV.
🍣 "Don't Lose Your Sushi" — MMP epithelium is extremely fragile and detaches easily. Warn assistant: NO close suctioning near biopsy site. Epithelium will flap off into the suction. [Slide 38]

TIER 1

Pemphigus Vulgaris (PV)

🦷 >50% PRESENT WITH ORAL LESIONS FIRST — oral lesions precede skin involvement by months to over a year. You may be the FIRST clinician to see this potentially fatal disease. Early diagnosis = lives saved. [Slide 41]
🎙️ Prof said "oral lesions are first to show, last to go" — the dentist's position to catch this disease early is a point of professional pride and clinical responsibility she emphasized strongly.
Nikolsky Sign = POSITIVE in PV — intraepithelial cleavage; lateral pressure on normal mucosa creates blister easily. [Slide 41]
🧠 Pegasus Mnemonic:
"PemPHIGus sounds like Pegasus. Pegasus FLIES BETWEEN THE CLOUDS. The cells are the clouds. Acantholysis = cells falling apart = space between clouds. Cleavage is INTRAEPITHELIAL (suprabasal) — UP HIGH like Pegasus." [Slides 42–43]
PV Pathomechanism: Autoantibodies attack desmoglein proteins (Dsg3, Dsg1) → epithelial cells lose cohesion = ACANTHOLYSIS (cells fall apart). Intraepithelial/suprabasal cleavage — above the basement membrane. [Slide 41]

DSG Antibody–Phenotype Table [Slides 43–44]

Antibodies PresentPhenotypeWhy

Anti-DSG3 + Anti-DSG1Mucocutaneous PV — oral + skinBoth targets attacked

⚠️ TRAP (F13): DSG3 = oral; DSG1 = skin; both = oral + skin. Don't blur these. [Slide 43]
🔬 BIOPSY BEFORE ANY TREATMENT — CRITICAL RULE [Slide 43]
Topical steroids suppress the autoantibodies needed for DIF diagnosis. Starting treatment first will obscure results.
  1. Take PERILESIONAL biopsy (2 specimens: formalin H&E + Michel's DIF)
  2. Begin topical steroids on non-biopsy areas immediately
  3. Wait 5–7 days before applying steroids to the biopsy site (delayed healing)
🎙️ Prof stated that biopsying early and before treatment is how lives get saved — the faster the diagnosis, the easier treatment is.
PV DIF Pattern: "Chicken wire" intercellular IgG/C3 deposition between keratinocytes throughout the spinous layer — pathognomonic for PV. [Slide 43]

TIER 1

Erythema Multiforme (EM) / RIME

EM/RIME and SJS/TEN are SEPARATE DISEASES — NOT a spectrum. [Slides 47, 50]
Old teaching (EM → SJS → TEN = severity continuum) = WRONG per this professor.
Different causes → different prognoses → different management. RIME is the preferred modern term.
🎙️ Prof called the "spectrum" concept "old, old thought process" and explicitly told students to abandon it — on her exam, these are separate entities with different etiologies.
THE 90% RULE — Core Distinguisher [Slides 47, 50]
✅ INFECTION (90%) → EM/RIME → good prognosis, self-limiting
🚨 DRUG (90%) → SJS/TEN → potentially FATAL
🎙️ Prof called this the central clinical logic of the entire EM/SJS section — knowing whether it's infection or drug triggered determines everything about prognosis and urgency.

Tier 3 — PNP Phenotypes, Diagnostics & Supportive Care
  • Five mucocutaneous phenotypes: Pemphigus-like, Pemphigoid-like, EM-like, GVHD-like, LP-like. [Slide 54]

  • Rituximab less effective for PNP than for regular PV.
  • Better prognosis: Castleman disease (surgically removable), lichenoid pattern, oral-only involvement.

Flashcard Deck — 15 Must-Know Q&A Pairs

Click each card to reveal the answer. All T1 items from the handoff.

What is the most common oral ulcerative condition?
Recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) — affects approximately 20% of the population. [Slide 4]
What clinical feature of RAS earns "easy points" on the exam?
The erythematous halo surrounding the yellow-white fibrinopurulent membrane. If you see that red halo in a photo → put RAS on your differential. [Slide 4]
New-onset RAS in a patient over 40 with no childhood ulcer history requires what?
Systemic workup — evaluate for HIV, celiac disease, IBD, hematologic malignancy. Always ask: "Did you have these as a child?" No = mandatory workup. [Slide 4]
Which topical corticosteroid does the professor say "commit to memory" and "will torture you about"?
Dexamethasone elixir 0.5 mg/5 mL — soak 5 minutes (minimum 2 min), up to QID. The universal first-line oral medicine rinse. Every dentist can and should write this prescription. [Slides 6–7]
Rank these three corticosteroids from most to least potent: triamcinolone paste, clobetasol, fluocinonide.
Clobetasol propionate 0.05% (Group 1 — super-high) > Fluocinonide 0.05% (Group 2 — high) > Triamcinolone acetonide 0.1% paste (Group 4 — medium/WEAKEST). Trap: "dental" in triamcinolone's name does NOT make it the best choice. [Slide 7]
What are the 3 D's of geographic tongue?
Depapillation (loss of filiform papillae → smooth red patches) + Demarcated (clear circumferential serpiginous white border) + Dynamic (migrates/changes location over days–weeks). [Slide 11]
What is the specific vocabulary word for geographic tongue's white serpentine border?
Serpiginous — from "serpent" (snake-shaped winding border). Prof had the entire class say this word aloud. Key exam vocabulary. [Slides 11–12]
What are the three drug classes that cause gingival hyperplasia, and which drug is the most important?
Anticonvulsants (Phenytoin = #1 most important, ~50% of users) + Immunosuppressants (Cyclosporine, 25–70%) + Calcium Channel Blockers (Nifedipine/Amlodipine, 6–25%). Boards love this section. [Slide 14]
What is the FIRST and MOST IMPORTANT intervention for drug-induced gingival hyperplasia?
Meticulous plaque control — before ANY pharmacologic or surgical treatment. Surgery without prior hygiene optimization = ~35% recurrence within 18 months. [Slide 15]
What is the pathognomonic location of oral lichen planus?
Bilateral posterior buccal mucosa + ventral tongue with Wickham striae (the lacy white reticulations). Bilateral = reassuring for OLP. Unilateral or atypical = BIOPSY. [Slide 31]
Is OLP autoimmune or auto-inflammatory per this professor?
AUTO-INFLAMMATORY (NOT autoimmune). Chronic T-cell mediated auto-inflammatory disorder. This distinction is important for patient counseling — autoimmune implies systemic life-altering disease that does not apply here. Prof's updated position. [Slide 31]
Nikolsky sign: positive in which disease, negative in which disease?
Positive = Pemphigus Vulgaris (intraepithelial cleavage — cells fall apart easily with lateral pressure). Negative = MMP (subepithelial cleavage — tense blister, harder to elicit). Mnemonic: Pegasus (PV) flies HIGH; Hades (pemphigoiD) lives DEEP. [Slides 37, 41]
MMP: what percentage have ocular involvement, and what is the referral rule?
60–70% have ocular involvement. Rule: Refer ALL MMP patients to ophthalmology — EVEN IF ASYMPTOMATIC. Early symptoms: burning, dryness, foreign body sensation. Conjunctival scarring (symblepharon) is irreversible → blindness. [Slide 39]
EM/RIME is triggered by what 90% of the time? SJS/TEN by what 90% of the time?
EM/RIME = Infection 90% (HSV ~70% for EM; Mycoplasma for RIME) → good prognosis. SJS/TEN = Drug ~85–90% → potentially fatal. Single most important distinguishing principle of the section. Allopurinol = #1 drug to remember for SJS/TEN. [Slides 47, 50]
PNP/PAMS: what is ALWAYS present, and what are the two hallmark complications?
ALWAYS an underlying neoplasm (known or occult) — trigger cancer workup. Hallmarks: (1) Intractable stomatitis — severe oral lesions that do not respond to standard therapy. (2) Bronchiolitis obliterans — autoantibodies attack respiratory epithelium = major cause of death. NHL ~40% is the most common associated malignancy. [Slides 54–55]
