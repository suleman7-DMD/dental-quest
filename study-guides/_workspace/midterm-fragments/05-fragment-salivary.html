Section 01

Why Saliva Matters

Saliva does far more than keep your mouth wet. It is a multi-functional fluid that protects teeth, fights infection, aids digestion, and even serves as a diagnostic tool.

Image: TBD — Salivary gland anatomy overview with labeled glands
Before we start

The 10 Functions of Saliva

When saliva production drops, all of these break down. This is why hyposalivation is a medical emergency for your teeth.

Think of saliva as your mouth's Swiss Army knife. It doesn't just do one thing — it does ten. When a patient loses salivary function (from medications, radiation, or autoimmune disease), the cascade of problems hits every single one of these domains simultaneously. That is why a "dry mouth" is never just uncomfortable — it is clinically dangerous.

Dental Protection

  • Remineralization — saliva delivers calcium, phosphate, and fluoride back into enamel
  • pH buffering — neutralizes acid from bacteria and diet
  • Pellicle formation — protein layer shields enamel surface

Lubrication & Mechanical Protection

  • Oral tissue lubrication (prevents friction injuries)
  • Prevention of mucosal abrasion
  • Upper GI tract lubrication

Antimicrobial & Immune Defense

  • Antibacterial activity (lysozyme, lactoferrin)
  • Antiviral and antifungal properties
  • Secretory IgA immunoglobulin secretion
  • Immune cell presence
  • Microbiome modulation

Digestion

  • Food bolus formation (chewing + swallowing)
  • Carbohydrate digestion (salivary amylase)
  • Lipid digestion (lingual lipase)
  • Esophageal acid neutralization

Sensory & Communication

  • Taste perception — saliva is the solvent for tastants (food molecules must dissolve in saliva to reach taste buds)
  • Taste sensitivity enhancement
  • Speech articulation — lubrication needed for tongue movement

Other Roles

  • Wound healing — growth factors in saliva (why oral wounds heal faster than skin)
  • Hormone synthesis
  • Diagnostic biomarker reservoir — saliva can be tested for drugs, hormones, antibodies, DNA
  • Cleansing & clearance — washes away food debris and bacteria

Stoopler ET, et al. 2024. JAMA. 331(12):1045–1054.

Major Salivary Gland Anatomy

Three paired major glands + hundreds of minor glands throughout the oral mucosa

There are two types of salivary secretion: serous (thin, watery, fast-flowing — like a waterfall) and mucous (thick, syrupy, slow-flowing — like honey). Each gland produces a different ratio of these, which determines the character of its saliva.

Gland Secretion Type Key Facts
Parotid Serous (purely watery) Largest gland. In front of ear/along jaw. Drains via Stensen duct opposite upper 2nd molar. Produces thin, enzyme-rich saliva.
Submandibular Mixed (serous + mucous) Below jaw. Drains via Wharton duct under tongue. Produces ~70% of resting saliva. Most prone to stones (sialolithiasis).
Sublingual Mucous (predominantly) Floor of mouth. Multiple small ducts (ducts of Rivinus). Produces thick, mucin-rich saliva.

New Discovery: Tubarial Glands

In 2021, researchers discovered a 4th pair of major salivary glands — the tubarial glands — located in the nasopharynx near the Eustachian tube openings. These were identified using PSMA PET-CT scans and are clinically relevant because they may be damaged during radiation therapy for head and neck cancers, contributing to post-radiation dry mouth.

Valstar MH, et al. 2021. Radiother Oncol. 154:292–298.

Section 02

Hyposalivation & Xerostomia

These are NOT the same thing. This distinction is one of the most commonly tested concepts in oral medicine.

Image: TBD — Venn diagram: hyposalivation 40–60% vs xerostomia 20–42%, overlap 16–27%

Terminology: Hyposalivation vs. Xerostomia

One is measurable. The other is subjective. Only one causes dental damage.

Hyposalivation

Objective decrease in salivary output — this means you can measure it with a test.

  • Unstimulated salivary flow rate ≤0.1 mL/min
  • Stimulated salivary flow rate ≤0.5–0.7 mL/min
  • Prevalence: 40–60% of population

Xerostomia

Subjective sensation of dryness — a symptom, not a measurement. The patient feels dry.

  • Does NOT require an objective decrease in salivary flow
  • Can occur even with normal saliva production
  • Noted at 40–50% of normal salivary flow
  • Prevalence: 20–42% of population

Critical concept: Only hyposalivation increases dental and medical risk. Xerostomia alone (without measurably reduced saliva) does not cause caries, candidiasis, or other oral complications. But the patient still suffers from the sensation and deserves treatment for comfort.

Analogy: Hyposalivation is like your faucet actually running dry (you can measure zero water coming out). Xerostomia is like feeling thirsty even though your faucet works fine. Both are real complaints, but only the broken faucet causes actual water damage to your house (teeth).

The Overlap

These two conditions overlap 16–27% of the time. That means many patients have one without the other:

  • A patient can have hyposalivation WITHOUT xerostomia — their saliva is objectively low but they haven't noticed
  • A patient can have xerostomia WITHOUT hyposalivation — they feel dry but their flow rate is normal (may be due to medications, burning mouth syndrome, oral dysesthesia, or trigeminal neuropathy)

Morita I, et al. 2023. PLoS One. 18(3):e0282740. · Islas-Granillo H, et al. 2017. Sci Rep. 7:40686. · Diep MT, et al. 2021. Eur J Oral Sci. 129(1):e12757. · Rojas-Ramirez MV, et al. 2023. OSOMOPOR. 136(2):154–161.

Why Dry Mouths Have Thick, Ropey Saliva

Submandibular and sublingual glands are usually affected first in hyposalivation

When salivary output drops, you lose mostly water, not mucin (the protein that makes saliva thick). So the remaining saliva becomes concentrated with mucins — making it stringy, ropey, and sticky instead of thin and watery. The submandibular and sublingual glands (which produce most of the mucous component) are typically affected first, meaning the serous (watery) output from the parotid becomes the dominant remaining flow. But overall volume is so low that the thick, mucin-heavy residue is what you see clinically.

Three reasons why dry mouths look the way they do:

  • Water loss >> mucin loss — the watery component evaporates/decreases faster than the mucin proteins
  • Remaining mucins become concentrated in a smaller volume of fluid — like reducing a sauce, it gets thicker
  • Mucins become altered — saliva isn't as lubricating, is more stringy, and more sticky

Consequences of Hyposalivation

The clinical sequelae that make hyposalivation a dental emergency

Xerostomia alone does not cause these complications — hyposalivation does. This is a board-testable distinction. If a patient feels dry but has normal flow, they won't develop radiation-pattern caries.

  • Root/cervical caries — characteristic location pattern (detailed in Radiation section below)
  • Recurrent decay — loss of buffering + remineralization = rapid breakdown of previous restorations
  • Candidiasis — especially erythematous candidiasis (red, not white). Saliva normally suppresses fungal overgrowth via antimicrobial proteins and mechanical flushing
  • Dysphagia — difficulty swallowing because saliva is needed to form a food bolus and lubricate the esophagus
  • Mucosal trauma — dry tissue tears and ulcerates more easily
  • Difficulty wearing dentures — saliva creates the thin film that helps dentures adhere to tissue via suction

Stoopler ET, et al. 2024. JAMA. 331(12):1045–1054. · Plemons JM, et al. 2014. J Am Dent Assoc. 145(8):867–873.

Chairside Saliva Assessment

How to evaluate a patient who reports dry mouth — a systematic three-step approach.

Image: TBD — Challacombe Scale clinical photographs (mild/moderate/severe dryness features)

The Three-Step Evaluation

Step 1: History

  • Medical history
    • Radiation history (head & neck?)
    • Autoimmune history (Sjögren, RA, SLE, thyroid disease?)
  • Medications — complete list, including OTC and supplements. Count total number (polypharmacy threshold: >3)
  • Radiation history — dose, field, when?
  • Symptoms
    • Duration — how long has this been happening?
    • Other body sites — dry eyes? dry nose? dry throat?
    • Difficulty swallowing — sips of water with meals? choking?

Step 2: Clinical Exam

Use the Challacombe Scale — a standardized scoring system with 10 clinical features to classify dryness severity:

Step 3: Objective Testing (When Indicated)

  • Unstimulated whole saliva flow — patient drools into a graduated tube for 5–15 min. Normal: ≥0.1 mL/min
  • Stimulated flow — patient chews paraffin wax or uses citric acid, then collects saliva. Normal: ≥0.7 mL/min
  • Consider referral for additional testing if autoimmune etiology suspected
A 65-year-old patient has a concern of dry mouth. On exam, you see normal salivary pooling. What is your next step?
  • Prescribe pilocarpine immediately
  • Tell the patient everything is fine and no workup is needed
  • Measure salivary flow to distinguish xerostomia from hyposalivation
  • Refer to Rheumatology for Sjögren workup
Correct: Measure salivary flow. Normal pooling on exam suggests salivary output may be adequate — but the patient's subjective complaint is real. You need objective flow rate measurement to determine if this is true hyposalivation (which carries dental risk) or xerostomia without reduced flow (which may be neurological/medication-related and managed differently). Don't dismiss the patient, but don't jump to prescribing sialagogues or specialist referral without confirming the diagnosis first.
Section 04

Causes of Hyposalivation

Medications and radiation are the two biggest culprits. Know the risk tiers and the specific caries patterns.

Medication-Induced Hyposalivation

The most common cause of dry mouth in your patients

Very High Risk

  • Urological anticholinergics (bladder meds)
    • Oxybutynin OR 18.9
    • Tolterodine OR 4.8

High Risk

  • Antidepressants OR 4.7
  • Psycholeptics OR 2.6
  • Anticholinergics (all classes)
  • Antihypertensives
  • Antipsychotics
  • Sedatives

Board Pearl: Polypharmacy

Polypharmacy increases dry mouth risk independent of the specific drug. It's not just about which medications — it's about how many.

Use of >3 medications/day increases dry mouth risk with an odds ratio of OR 2.9

This means: even if none of the individual medications are "high risk" for dry mouth, combining 4+ drugs together creates additive anticholinergic burden that suppresses salivary output. Always count total meds.

Stoopler ET, et al. 2024. JAMA. 331(12):1045–1054. · Tan ECK, et al. 2017. J Am Geriatr Soc. 65(1):76–84.

Radiation-Induced Hyposalivation

Head & neck radiation causes permanent, dose-dependent gland destruction

Key Features

  • Standard head & neck radiation dose: 65–70 Gy
  • Dose-dependent gland destruction — higher dose = more damage, eventually irreversible
  • Tolerance thresholds for irreversible changes:
    • Parotid: >25 Gy
    • Submandibular: >39 Gy

Radiation caries has a characteristic pattern (this is highly testable):

  • Ipsilateral — on the same side as the radiation field
  • Cervical — at the neck of the tooth (CEJ), facial to circumferential
  • Smooth surfaces of mandibular anterior dentition — these surfaces are normally caries-resistant, so decay here is a red flag
  • Maxillary molars

Normally, the smooth labial surfaces of your lower front teeth never get cavities because saliva bathes them constantly from the sublingual glands right underneath. When radiation destroys those glands, these normally-protected surfaces become sitting ducks. That is why caries on the facial smooth surface of mandibular anteriors is a classic red flag for radiation-induced hyposalivation.

Image: TBD — Radiation caries: (A) cervical/root caries CEJ-level decay; (B) mandibular anterior smooth surface caries; (C) widespread cervical caries panoramic view.

Management of Radiation-Induced Hyposalivation

Aggressive Fluoride Protocol

  • Daily 1.1% sodium fluoride (NaF) gel or stannous fluoride (SnF2) gel
  • Applied via brush-on technique or custom dental trays
  • Daily 1.1% NaF dentifrice (5000 ppm fluoride) — e.g., PreviDent 5000
  • Fluoride varnish application 3× per year (alternative or adjunct)
  • Calcium phosphate artificial saliva rinse/cream/gel for remineralization

Recall & Caries Control

  • Short recall intervals: every 3–4 months
  • Brushing 2–4× daily
  • Chlorhexidine (CHX) rinses

Plemons JM, et al. 2014. J Am Dent Assoc. 145(8):867–873. · Mariette X, Criswell LA. 2018. N Engl J Med. 378(10):931–939.

Section 05

Treatment of Hyposalivation & Xerostomia

A tiered approach: things to avoid, OTC options, and prescription sialagogues with their pharmacology.

Image: TBD — Sialagogue mechanism: muscarinic receptor (M3) activation pathway diagram

Non-Pharmacologic Management

Avoid

  • Caffeine — mild diuretic, can worsen dehydration
  • Alcohol — both beverages and alcohol-containing mouthwash (e.g., original Listerine). Alcohol is drying.
  • Vaseline / Aquaphor on lips — petroleum-based products seal moisture OUT, not in. Use lanolin instead.

Over-the-Counter Options

  • Hydration (+/- pineapple — bromelain may stimulate saliva)
  • Bedside humidifier — especially for nighttime mouth breathing
  • Sugar-free lemon candies — citric acid stimulates residual gland function
  • XyliMelts — xylitol adhesive discs that stick to gums/teeth
  • Xerostrips — dissolving oral strips
  • Biotene products — rinse, spray, gel, toothpaste (enzyme-based saliva substitutes)
  • Mouth Kote — moisturizing spray
  • Lanolin HPA (Lansinoh) — for dry/cracked lips. Better than petroleum because it lets moisture pass through.

Pharmacologic Treatment: Sialagogues

Prescription drugs that stimulate salivary glands to produce more saliva by activating muscarinic receptors

What are muscarinic receptors? They are a type of receptor for the neurotransmitter acetylcholine, found throughout the body. There are 5 subtypes (M1–M5). The one we care most about is M3, which is the receptor on salivary glands, lacrimal glands, sweat glands, and smooth muscle. When M3 is activated, it tells the gland to secrete. Sialagogues are drugs that activate M3 to make salivary glands produce more saliva.

Sialagogue contraindications (because muscarinic activation affects more than just salivary glands): Asthma (M3 causes bronchoconstriction), narrow-angle glaucoma (M3 increases intraocular pressure), cardiac disease (M2 slows heart rate).

The Three Sialagogues

Drug Dose Receptor Selectivity Key Detail
Pilocarpine 5 mg TID–6x/day Non-selective (hits all M1–M5) but highest affinity for M3 Most commonly prescribed sialagogue. More side effects because it is non-selective (sweating, GI upset, flushing).
Cevimeline 30 mg TID Selective for M1 and M3 More targeted = fewer side effects than pilocarpine. Does not hit M2 (heart) as much.
Bethanechol 25 mg TID Selective for M2 and M3 Less commonly used for dry mouth. Hits M2 (cardiac effects).

Topical Alternative

2 drops of pilocarpine 4% ophthalmic solution in 1 teaspoon of water, hold in mouth for 5 minutes, then spit out. This provides local stimulation with fewer systemic side effects.

Muscarinic Receptor Reference Table

You don't need to memorize every detail of this table, but understand the pattern: M3 is the "secretion + smooth muscle" receptor. When sialagogues activate M3, the salivary glands secrete — but so do sweat glands (sweating side effect), bronchial smooth muscle contracts (why it's contraindicated in asthma), and GI motility increases (diarrhea side effect).

Receptor Primary Locations Normal Physiologic Effect
M1 CNS (cortex, hippocampus), autonomic ganglia, gastric parietal cells Cognitive modulation, facilitates autonomic transmission, ↑ gastric acid secretion
M2 Heart (SA node, AV node, atria) ↓ Heart rate, ↓ conduction velocity
M3 Salivary glands, lacrimal glands, sweat glands, bronchial smooth muscle, GI smooth muscle, bladder detrusor muscle, vascular endothelium ↑ Exocrine secretions (saliva, tears, sweat), bronchoconstriction, ↑ GI motility, bladder contraction, endothelial NO release (vasodilation)
M4 CNS (less well defined clinically) Modulates dopamine pathways
M5 CNS, cerebral vasculature Cerebral vasodilation (via NO)

Stoopler ET, et al. 2024. JAMA. 331(12):1045–1054. · Plemons JM, et al. 2014. J Am Dent Assoc. 145(8):867–873.

Section 06

Sjögren Syndrome

Autoimmune destruction of salivary and lacrimal glands. The quintessential "dry mouth + dry eyes" disease with a dangerous complication: lymphoma.

Image: TBD — Bilateral parotid swelling in Sjögren patient; oral purpura; labial salivary gland biopsy (focal lymphocytic sialadenitis)

Sjögren Overview

Sjögren syndrome is an autoimmune disease where your immune system attacks and destroys your own moisture-producing glands — primarily salivary and lacrimal (tear) glands. Think of it as your body's immune system systematically dismantling its own plumbing. The result: dry mouth, dry eyes, and a significantly elevated risk of lymphoma.

Definition & Classification

Autoimmune destruction of salivary and lacrimal glands

Primary Sjögren Syndrome

Occurs alone — no other autoimmune disease present

Secondary Sjögren Syndrome

Occurs alongside another autoimmune disease (most commonly RA or SLE)

Epidemiology

  • Middle-aged females
  • Female:male ratio = 9:1
  • Prevalence: 0.5–1% of population

Classic Presentation

  • Hyposalivation +/- xerostomia
  • Xerophthalmia (dry eyes)
  • Parotid enlargement (bilateral)
  • Middle-aged female
  • Autoimmune comorbidity (RA, thyroid disease, etc.)

Must Exclude Before Diagnosing

  • History of head/neck radiation
  • Active hepatitis C infection
  • Sarcoidosis
  • IgG4-related disease
  • Recent anticholinergic use (can cause false positive findings)

Zero DT, et al. 2016. J Am Dent Assoc. 147(4):295–305. · Goldblatt F, O'Neill SG. 2013. Lancet. 382(9894):797–808.

Diagnostic Workup: 2016 ACR-EULAR Criteria

Current classification requires a score ≥4 points

Know this scoring system for the exam. The two "3-point" items (biopsy and anti-SSA) are the heavy hitters. Either one alone gets you 75% of the way to diagnosis.

Criterion Points Details
Labial salivary gland biopsy with focal lymphocytic sialadenitis (focus score ≥1) 3 points Also correlates with increased lymphoma risk
Anti-SSA (Ro) positive 3 points The most important serological marker
Ocular staining score ≥5 (or Schirmer test ≤5 mm/5 min) 1 point Measures tear production / corneal damage
Unstimulated whole saliva flow ≤0.1 mL/min 1 point Objective hyposalivation
Anti-SSB (La) positive 1 point Less specific than anti-SSA; often co-occurs

What to do as a dentist when you suspect Sjögren:

  • Order Anti-SSA (Ro) and Anti-SSB (La) antibodies
  • Also order ANA (antinuclear antibody) and RF (rheumatoid factor)
  • Refer to Oral Medicine or OMFS for minor salivary gland biopsy

Early detection matters: preserving gland function early (DMARDs, clinical trials) is better than managing the consequences of complete gland destruction later.

Zero DT, et al. 2016. J Am Dent Assoc. 147(4):295–305. · Mariette X, Criswell LA. 2018. N Engl J Med. 378(10):931–939. · Goldblatt F, O'Neill SG. 2013. Lancet. 382(9894):797–808.

A 52-year-old woman with objectively dry mouth and dry eyes has negative anti-SSA and anti-SSB. Can she have Sjögren syndrome?
  • Yes — up to 30% of patients with positive biopsy/Schirmer may be seronegative for antibodies
  • No — anti-SSA is required for the diagnosis
  • No — if both antibodies are negative, Sjögren is definitively ruled out
  • Only if she has rheumatoid arthritis
Correct: Yes — seronegative Sjögren exists. Up to 30% of patients may have positive biopsy and/or Schirmer test findings despite negative serology. A negative antibody result does not rule out the disease. Management: increase recall frequency, start high-potency fluoride therapy, counsel on caries risk and OHI, refer to Oral Medicine (for minor salivary gland biopsy, ocular staining or Schirmer test) and Rheumatology, consider sialagogue therapy, OTC saliva products, and encourage hydration.

Lymphoma Risk in Sjögren Syndrome

The most dangerous complication — and the reason we monitor these patients closely

Critical complication:

  • Lifetime risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma: 5–10%
  • Specifically: Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue (MALT) lymphoma
  • Highest risk of lymphoma among all autoimmune diseases
  • 7–19× higher risk than general population

Red Flags Requiring Urgent Rheumatology/Hematology Referral

  • Persistent unilateral gland enlargement — bilateral is typical Sjögren; unilateral suggests neoplasm
  • Lymphadenopathy
  • Purpura (purple skin spots from bleeding into skin)
  • Constitutional symptoms — fever, night sweats, weight loss (the classic "B symptoms" of lymphoma)

Key distinction: Bilateral parotid enlargement = typical Sjögren (autoimmune inflammation). Unilateral persistent enlargement = worry about lymphoma or other neoplasm. This is a critical exam distinction.

Obstructive & Infectious Conditions

Salivary stones and bacterial infections — mechanical blockages and their consequences.

Image: TBD — Sialolithiasis imaging: panoramic radiograph showing submandibular sialolith; occlusal film floor of mouth stone

Sialolithiasis (Salivary Stones)

Calcified masses that block salivary ducts, causing pain and swelling during meals

Classic Pattern

Pathognomonic finding: meal-time pain and swelling. The gland swells because it is producing saliva in response to eating but the stone blocks the duct, so the saliva backs up. The swelling goes down between meals when the gland stops producing actively.

  • Submandibular gland is most common — 80–90% of all salivary stones
  • Unilateral presentation (one side)

Why Is the Submandibular Gland Most Commonly Affected?

Three anatomical reasons conspire against the submandibular gland:

  • Longer, more tortuous duct — Wharton's duct is longer and has more twists than Stensen's duct (parotid), creating more opportunity for stones to form and get stuck
  • Saliva flows against gravity — the gland is below the floor of the mouth and the duct opening is above, so saliva must travel uphill. Stagnation promotes stone formation.
  • More mucous, calcium-rich saliva — the submandibular gland produces mixed (serous + mucous) saliva. The mucous component is thicker and contains more calcium, which precipitates into stones.

Imaging Options

Modality When to Use
Occlusal radiograph First-line for submandibular stones (can often see radiopaque stones in floor of mouth)
Panoramic radiograph Good overview; may show large stones
Ultrasound (via referral) Excellent for non-radiopaque stones that don't show up on X-rays
CT If needed for surgical planning

Not all stones are radiopaque — if your X-ray is negative but you still suspect a stone (classic meal-time symptoms), order ultrasound or CT. A negative radiograph does not rule out sialolithiasis.

Image: TBD — Panoramic radiograph: edentulous patient with radiopaque sialolith in right submandibular region.

Management

Conservative (First-Line)

  • Hydration (2L/day)
  • Gland massage — milk the gland toward the duct opening
  • Warm/moist heat compresses
  • OTC saliva products
  • NSAIDs or acetaminophen for pain
  • Muscarinic receptor agonist therapy (sialagogues — increase flow to flush the stone)

When to Refer for Sialendoscopy

  • Stone >2 mm in proximal duct
  • Failed conservative management after 1–2 weeks
  • Recurrent symptoms
  • Success rate: 85–92% gland preservation

Large / Recurrent Stones

  • Refer for sialendoscopy
  • Surgical removal if needed

Acute Bacterial Sialadenitis

Bacterial infection of a salivary gland — typically secondary to obstruction or dehydration

When saliva stagnates (from a stone, dehydration, or post-surgical state), bacteria can ascend the duct and infect the gland. The classic scenario: a debilitated, dehydrated post-surgical patient develops a painful, swollen parotid gland. Or a patient with a known stone develops secondary infection.

Clinical Signs

  • Painful swollen gland (unilateral)
  • Fever
  • Purulence at duct orifice — if you milk the gland and pus comes out of the duct opening, that is diagnostic
  • Erythema (redness of overlying skin)
  • Firm, tender gland on palpation

Common Organisms

  • Staphylococcus aureus — most common
  • Streptococcus species
  • Anaerobes
Image: TBD — Acute sialadenitis: massive unilateral parotid/submandibular swelling with visible skin erythema and tension.

Management

Treatment

  • Antibiotics
    • First-line: Amoxicillin-clavulanate or Clindamycin
    • Must cover Staph aureus
  • Aggressive hydration
  • Culture if purulent discharge present
  • Imaging to rule out obstruction (stone? mass?)

Urgent Referral (ED/ENT/OMFS) If:

  • Systemic spread / sepsis
  • Immunocompromised patient
  • Trismus / severe swelling
  • Airway compromise concern

Mucocele

A common benign lesion caused by trauma to minor salivary gland ducts. Know where they occur and why.

Image: TBD — Mucocele on lower lip: dome-shaped, bluish, fluctuant. Surgical excision sequence.

Mucocele

Mucus retention/extravasation phenomenon of minor salivary glands

Classic Presentation

  • Lower lip — most common site: 60–70% of cases
  • Fluctuant swelling — soft, compressible, dome-shaped
  • Bluish color — mucus showing through the thin overlying mucosa
  • History of trauma — lip biting is the classic cause (sharp canine teeth biting the lower lip)
  • Painless
  • May rupture and reform cyclically

Pathophysiology

You bite your lip, and the force severs a tiny minor salivary gland duct. The duct keeps trying to pump mucus, but now it has nowhere to go. The mucus spills out into the surrounding connective tissue, forming a pool surrounded by inflamed tissue (granulation wall). This is NOT a true cyst — it has no epithelial lining. It is technically a "mucus extravasation phenomenon."

Extravasation Type (Most Common)

  • Trauma severs the duct
  • Mucus spills into connective tissue
  • Surrounded by granulation wall (inflammatory reaction)
  • No epithelial lining — therefore NOT a true cyst
  • Common in younger patients

Retention Type (Less Common)

  • Duct becomes partially blocked (not severed)
  • Mucus backs up within the duct itself
  • Duct dilates and forms a mucus-filled sac
  • HAS an epithelial lining (the duct wall) — IS a true cyst
  • More common in older adults

Image: TBD — Diagram: Extravasation type (severed duct, mucin pooling freely, granulation wall, no epithelial lining) vs. Retention type (obstructed intact duct, dilated with mucin, epithelial duct wall maintained).

Why lower lip? The lower lip is the most commonly traumatized mucosal surface in the mouth. It sits right over the lower incisor/canine teeth, which act as a biting surface during accidental lip biting, habitual chewing, or trauma. The lower lip is also packed with minor salivary glands whose tiny ducts are vulnerable to rupture.

Treatment

  • Surgical excision — remove the mucocele along with the associated minor salivary gland to prevent recurrence
  • Must remove the feeding gland, not just drain the mucus — otherwise it will refill
  • If the mucocele is very large (on the floor of the mouth) it is called a ranula — these may require marsupialization or removal of the sublingual gland

Key considerations for this patient: (1) He has glaucoma — narrow-angle glaucoma is a contraindication to sialagogues. If his dry mouth contributes to tongue ulceration, you cannot simply prescribe pilocarpine. (2) He has extreme polypharmacy (>10 medications) — his dry mouth is almost certainly medication-induced. (3) The histopath shows ulceration + granulation tissue, negative for dysplasia — this is reassuring but doesn't eliminate the need for follow-up. (4) Clotrimazole (Mycelex) has sugar — not appropriate for diabetic patients (from quiz review slide).
