Emicizumab & Hemophilia A

Housekeeping from previous lecture
Plain English — What Is Hemophilia A?

Your blood clots through a cascade — a chain of proteins (called "factors") that activate each other like dominoes. Hemophilia A means you're missing Factor VIII (factor 8). Factor VIII normally acts as a "bridge" that helps Factor IXa (9a) activate Factor X (10) in the intrinsic pathway of the cascade. Without that bridge, the dominoes stop falling and your blood can't clot properly.

Emicizumab (Hemlibra)

  • Only useful for Hemophilia A (Factor VIII deficiency) — does nothing for Hemophilia B (Factor IX deficiency)
  • It's a bispecific antibody — a Y-shaped antibody where each arm grabs a different target:
    • One arm grabs activated Factor IX (IXa)
    • The other arm grabs Factor X
    • By physically holding them together, it mimics the cofactor function of activated Factor VIII
Clinical Pearl

Why doesn't it work for Hemophilia B? Because Hemophilia B is missing Factor IX itself. If there's no Factor IX for one arm of the antibody to grab, the bridge can't form. Emicizumab replaces Factor VIII's function — it does NOT replace Factor IX.

Part I

Dental Management of Oncologic Patients

Pre-Transplant Dental Clearance = Infection Control

Why this matters for your oncology patients
Plain English — Why Clearance?

When a patient is about to undergo bone marrow transplant, organ transplant, or start chemo/radiation, their immune system will be destroyed or severely suppressed. Any dental infection — even a completely asymptomatic one — becomes potentially lethal because they won't have white blood cells to fight it. Think of it as "clearing the minefield before the army loses its armor."

  • Odontogenic infections can be life-threatening during profound immunosuppression
  • Only 0.57% develop odontogenic complications with appropriate clearance — much higher without
  • Asymptomatic disease still matters — non-vital teeth and untreated periapical pathology must be eliminated
Key Concept

Even if a tooth looks fine on exam and the patient has zero symptoms, a dead tooth with periapical radiolucency is a ticking time bomb in an immunosuppressed patient. It MUST be dealt with before treatment starts.

Mota & Alves (2025). Clin Oral Investig, 29, 576. Hansen et al. (2021). Support Care Cancer, 29(4), 2231–2238.

The Golden Rule

NEVER delay oncologic treatment for dental work unless the tumor is slow-growing AND oncology agrees. The cancer will kill the patient faster than dental disease will.

Urgent Patients

Sometimes the cancer can't wait for ideal dental treatment. If someone needs their transplant NOW, prioritize infection control even if the treatment plan isn't ideal. Extract questionable teeth rather than attempting complex restorative work. Infection control > perfect dentistry.

Tomblyn et al. (2009). Biol Blood Marrow Transplant, 15(10), 1143–1238.

Treatment Types: Healing Windows & Risk Periods

Plain English — Why Do These Differ?

Each cancer treatment destroys your immune system differently and for different durations. HSCT (bone marrow transplant) wipes out the entire immune system and regrows it — the "reboot" takes ~100 days. Standard chemo temporarily drops your counts between cycles but they recover. Radiation damages bone blood supply permanently in the treatment field. Bone-modifying agents (bisphosphonates/denosumab) shut down bone remodeling lifelong. Each requires a different dental strategy.

Key Terminology Explained
  • ANC = Absolute Neutrophil Count. Neutrophils are your infection-fighting white blood cells. Below 1000 = neutropenic = severely immunocompromised.
  • ORN = Osteoradionecrosis. Radiation permanently damages blood vessels in bone. Extraction after radiation can create a wound that never heals because blood supply is destroyed. This risk is lifelong.
  • MRONJ = Medication-Related Osteonecrosis of the Jaw. Bisphosphonates/denosumab shut down osteoclasts (cells that remodel bone). Extracting a tooth in bone that can't remodel = exposed, dead bone.
  • Engraftment = When transplanted bone marrow stem cells start producing new blood cells. Before this happens (~Day +14–28), the patient has essentially zero immune system.
  • Conditioning = The chemo/radiation given right before transplant to destroy the patient's existing bone marrow and make room for the donor cells.

ADA Caries Classification System (ICDAS)

Why This Matters Here

Oncology dental clearance decisions depend on the depth of caries. A D1 (enamel-only) lesion gets conservative fluoride treatment. A D4 (pulpal involvement) gets extracted. You need to classify accurately because the wrong call could either leave infection behind (under-treating) or delay cancer treatment with unnecessary procedures (over-treating).

Why Coronectomy Over Extraction Post-RT and On BMA?

Coronectomy = removing the crown of the tooth but intentionally leaving the roots in the bone. Why? Because extracting creates an open wound in bone that needs to heal. In irradiated bone (ORN risk) or bisphosphonate-treated bone (MRONJ risk), that wound may never heal because the bone's blood supply or remodeling capacity is permanently impaired. By leaving roots in place, you avoid creating the wound while still removing the source of pain and caries.

SDF = Silver Diamine Fluoride. Arrests active caries without drilling. The silver kills bacteria, the fluoride remineralizes. Turns the lesion black (cosmetic downside) but stops progression — perfect when you need to control caries fast and invasive treatment is risky.

ITR = Interim Therapeutic Restoration. A temporary filling (usually glass ionomer) placed without full cavity preparation. Quick, minimally invasive, releases fluoride. Buys time until the patient can receive definitive treatment.

Raber-Durlacher JE et al. (2013). Support Care Cancer. 21(11):3053–3060. Peterson DE et al. (2024). J Clin Oncol. 42(11):1267–1291.

Key Concepts Explained
  • Day +100: After HSCT, this is roughly when the new bone marrow (engraftment) is established and producing enough immune cells. Before this, the patient is profoundly immunosuppressed.
  • Pentoxifylline + Tocopherol (PENTOCLO protocol): Pentoxifylline improves blood flow in small vessels; tocopherol (vitamin E) reduces oxidative damage. Together they help irradiated bone heal better. This is the standard of care for extractions in irradiated fields.
  • Why lifelong fluoride after H&N RT? Radiation damages salivary glands → permanent xerostomia → saliva's buffering and remineralization capacity is destroyed → rampant "radiation caries" develops rapidly. Lifelong prescription fluoride prevents this.
  • Why is BMA elective surgery contraindicated? Bisphosphonates bind to bone and have half-lives of years to decades. Even after stopping the drug, the bone can't remodel normally. Any surgical wound in jaw bone risks MRONJ — exposed necrotic bone that doesn't heal.

Lupus Erythematosus

Tier 1 — High Priority
What Is It
Chronic autoimmune disease where the body attacks its own tissues
Demographics
90% female, typically ages 15–44
Key Oral Finding
Ulcers on hard palate/buccal mucosa; discoid lesions with radiating striae
Most Specific Antibody
Anti-Sm (Smith) — 10–30% of patients but highly specific for lupus
Plain English — What Is Lupus?

Normally your immune system makes antibodies that attack foreign things — bacteria, viruses, etc. In lupus, the immune system gets confused and makes autoantibodies that attack your own cells and tissues. These autoantibodies target the nucleus of your cells (that's why the screening test is called ANA — Anti-Nuclear Antibody). Since every organ has cells with nuclei, lupus can attack virtually anything — skin, joints, kidneys, heart, lungs, brain, blood.

Two Forms

Systemic Lupus (SLE)
  • Multi-organ involvement: skin, joints, kidneys, lungs, heart, CNS, blood
  • Can be life-threatening
  • Requires systemic treatment
Discoid Lupus (DLE)
  • Cutaneous (skin) form only
  • Characteristic discoid (disc-shaped) skin lesions
  • Does not involve internal organs

Systemic Manifestations — Why You Care as a Dentist

  • Hematologic: Thrombocytopenia (low platelets → bleeding risk), neutropenia (low WBCs → infection risk), hemolytic anemia
  • Renal: Lupus nephritis in ~40% → 10% progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) → affects drug dosing, may be on dialysis
  • Cardiac: Pericarditis (~25%), valve disease
  • Pulmonary: Pleuritis (16%), interstitial lung disease
  • Neuropsychiatric: Involvement in ~50% — seizures, psychosis, cognitive dysfunction
  • Medications: Immunosuppressants, steroids, anticoagulants — all affect dental management
Before Any Procedure on a Lupus Patient

Know their: platelet count (bleeding risk), ANC (infection risk), renal function (drug metabolism), anticoagulation status (bleeding risk). Don't just look in the mouth — check the medical history thoroughly.

Oral Manifestations

  • Oral Ulcers:
    • Can be painless or painful
    • Classically on hard palate and buccal mucosa
    • Part of the SLE classification criteria (worth 2 points)
  • Discoid Lesions:
    • Erythema (redness) + radiating white striae
    • This is where it gets confusing — these look similar to oral lichen planus (OLP)
  • Hyposalivation:
    • Secondary Sjögren overlap is common — lupus and Sjögren's often occur together
    • Leads to dry mouth, increased caries risk
  • Increased caries and periodontal disease — secondary to altered salivary flow
  • Lichenoid drug reactions from hydroxychloroquine and NSAIDs — look like lichen planus but caused by the medication

How to Tell Oral Lupus from Oral Lichen Planus (OLP)

High-Yield Comparison — Commonly Tested

Both lupus and lichen planus can cause white striae and erythema in the mouth. Here's how to tell them apart:

Feature Oral Lupus Oral Lichen Planus
Striae Pattern Radiating from center (like sun rays) Reticular/lacy (Wickham striae — interlacing network)
Center of Lesion Erythema or ulceration (the center is damaged) Usually intact (center preserved)
DIF (Direct Immunofluorescence) Granular IgG/IgM/C3 — "Lupus Band" Fibrinogen at DEJ
What Is DIF and the "Lupus Band"?

DIF (Direct Immunofluorescence) is a lab technique where you take a biopsy, stain it with fluorescent antibodies, and look at it under a special microscope. The fluorescent antibodies stick to immune complexes in the tissue and glow under UV light.

In lupus, you see a continuous granular band of IgG, IgM, and C3 (antibodies and complement proteins) deposited along the basement membrane zone. This glowing band is called the "Lupus Band" — it's the immune system's attack on the tissue made visible.

In lichen planus, you see fibrinogen (a clotting protein) deposited at the DEJ (dentinoenamel junction area / basement membrane). Different protein, different pattern — that's how you distinguish them.

Workup & Diagnosis

Biopsy
  • H&E (standard histology) + DIF (immunofluorescence) — you need both
  • DIF shows the characteristic granular lupus band
If You Suspect New Lupus Diagnosis — Refer to Rheumatology
  • ANA (Antinuclear Antibody): Best screening test — very sensitive but not specific. A positive ANA doesn't mean lupus, but a negative ANA essentially rules it out.
  • Anti-dsDNA Antibody: 60–80% of active lupus patients; specific for lupus. Levels correlate with disease activity — they go up during flares.
  • Anti-Sm (Smith) Antibody: Only in 10–30% of patients, but Most Specific for lupus. If it's positive, it's almost certainly lupus.
  • CBC, CMP, complement levels (C3, C4 — consumed in active lupus)
Memory Trick: ANA vs. Anti-dsDNA vs. Anti-Sm
  • ANA = casts a wide net (sensitive, not specific) — catches lupus but also catches other autoimmune diseases
  • Anti-dsDNA = medium net (more specific, correlates with disease activity/flares)
  • Anti-Sm = laser-guided (most specific, but misses 70–90% of patients because it's only in 10–30%)
SLE Classification Criteria (2019 EULAR/ACR)

Entry criterion: ANA titer ≥1:80 on HEp-2 cells (ever). If absent → do not classify as SLE.

If ANA positive → apply additive criteria across domains. Need ≥10 points + at least one clinical criterion to classify as SLE.

Management of Oral Lupus Lesions

Treatment Ladder
  • Topical (1st line):
    • Generalized involvement: Dexamethasone rinse
    • Focal or multifocal: Fluocinonide 0.05% or Tacrolimus 0.1%
  • Intralesional (for stubborn lesions): Triamcinolone 40 mg/mL
  • Systemic: Hydroxychloroquine (this is the cornerstone systemic therapy for lupus — originally an antimalarial drug)
  • All patients: Smoking cessation + sun protection
Why Sun Protection?

UV light (both natural and artificial UVB) triggers lupus flares. UV radiation causes skin cell damage → exposes nuclear antigens → stimulates the autoimmune response. This can trigger both skin (cutaneous lupus) and systemic flares. Every lupus patient should avoid prolonged sun exposure and use high-SPF sunscreen.

Siegel CH, Sammaritano LR. (2024). JAMA. 332(4):326–337. He J et al. (2025). J Autoimmun. 153:103393. Jessop S et al. (2017). Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2017(5):CD002954.

Sarcoidosis

Tier 1 — High Priority
What Is It
Multisystem granulomatous disease of unknown cause
Hallmark
Noncaseating granulomas (no central necrosis)
Demographics
Peak onset 25–35; higher prevalence in Black patients
Key Lab
ACE (angiotensin converting enzyme) elevated in ~60%
Key Imaging
Bilateral hilar adenopathy on CXR — highly specific
Oral Significance
Oral lesions may be the FIRST sign of systemic sarcoidosis
Plain English — What Is Sarcoidosis?

Sarcoidosis is a disease where your immune system forms tiny clusters of inflammatory cells called granulomas in various organs — nobody knows exactly why. It's like your immune system is fighting an invisible enemy and building little "fortresses" (granulomas) throughout your body. The lungs are involved in >90% of cases, but it can hit virtually any organ. The granulomas are noncaseating — meaning they don't have the cheesy central dead zone you see in tuberculosis. About half of patients get better on their own; about a third develop chronic disease.

  • Lungs involved in >90% of cases; can affect virtually any organ
  • ~50% have spontaneous remission; ~1/3 develop chronic disease
  • Cardiac/neurologic involvement → increased mortality

Systemic Manifestations

  • Pulmonary (>90%): Cough, dyspnea, bilateral hilar adenopathy
  • Skin: Lupus pernio (violaceous plaques on nose/cheeks/ears), erythema nodosum (tender red nodules on shins), papules/plaques
  • Eyes (20–25%): Uveitis, sicca symptoms (dry eyes — similar to Sjögren's overlap)
  • Cardiac (10–20%): Arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy
  • Neurologic (10–25%): Cranial nerve palsies, neuropathy
  • Medications: Steroids, methotrexate, biologics — affects dental management

Oral Manifestations

Critical Point

Oral lesions may be the first sign of systemic sarcoidosis — if you see these findings, always screen for pulmonary involvement. You could be the first provider to catch this disease.

  • Soft tissue: Nodules, ulcers, swelling of tongue, lips, gingiva, palate
  • Bone: Alveolar bone loss, tooth mobility (granulomas can form in bone)
  • Salivary glands: Parotid swelling, hyposalivation
  • Gingiva: Gingivitis, gingival enlargement
What Does Oral Sarcoidosis Look Like?

The lecture showed clinical photos of: swollen, granular-textured lip involvement, gingival erythema and enlargement, and raised tongue nodules/papules. The lesions are firm and may be the same color as surrounding mucosa or slightly erythematous. They can mimic many other conditions, which is why biopsy is essential.

Workup

  • Biopsy: Shows noncaseating granulomas. Must exclude TB and fungal infection (both can also cause granulomas, but they're caseating/necrotizing)
  • Chest X-ray / CT: Bilateral hilar adenopathy — highly specific for sarcoidosis
  • Labs:
    • ACE (Angiotensin Converting Enzyme): Elevated in ~60% of sarcoidosis patients. The granulomas themselves produce ACE.
    • CBC, CMP, calcium (sarcoid granulomas can produce excess vitamin D → hypercalcemia)
  • Refer to pulmonology/rheumatology for systemic workup
  • Before dental procedures: Know if on immunosuppressants; check CBC if on methotrexate

Treatment

Treatment Approach
  • Observation for asymptomatic oral lesions (remember: ~50% remit spontaneously)
  • Corticosteroids:
    • Systemic prednisone 20–40 mg/day
    • Topicals are not proven to be successful for sarcoidosis (unlike lupus)
  • Steroid-sparing agents:
    • Methotrexate
    • Hydroxychloroquine (especially for cutaneous/oral disease)
  • Surgery: For localized, symptomatic lesions only

Granulation Tissue vs. Granulomas

Commonly confused terminology
Why This Section Exists

Students constantly confuse these terms. They sound similar but are completely different things. And a pyogenic granuloma is NOT a granuloma — it's a misnomer. Let's clear this up.

Granulation Tissue
  • A clinical term (what you see with your eyes), not histologic
  • Describes the grainy appearance of healing tissue
  • Classically found in extraction sockets during normal healing
  • It's a GOOD thing — it means the wound is healing
  • Made of new blood vessels + fibroblasts + inflammatory cells
Granulomas
  • A histologic finding (what you see under the microscope)
  • Clusters of macrophages and giant cells in chronic inflammation
  • Pyogenic granuloma is NOT a granuloma — it's a vascular proliferation (misnomer)
  • Indicates the immune system is trying to wall off something it can't destroy

Types of Granulomatous Disease

Type Key Feature Think…
Caseating (necrotizing) Central necrosis (cheesy dead center) TB, syphilis, deep fungal infection, GPA
Non-caseating No necrosis ("naked granulomas" — few surrounding lymphocytes) Sarcoidosis, Crohn disease, OFG (orofacial granulomatosis)
Suppurative Neutrophils / abscess within granuloma Bartonella (cat scratch), fungi
Foreign body Foreign material visible within granuloma Dermal filler, food particles, suture material, injuries
Board-Friendly Summary

Caseating = infectious (TB, syphilis, fungi, GPA). Think "cheese = disease."
Non-caseating = sarcoidosis (or Crohn). The immune system is fighting but there's no pathogen to kill, so no necrosis.
Suppurative = abscess + granuloma together. Bartonella is the classic cause.
Foreign body = you can see the culprit under the microscope.

Politis C et al. (2016). Front Physiol. 7:507.

Vitamin Deficiencies & Toxicities

Tier 1 — High Priority
Why This Matters for Dentists

Vitamin deficiencies cause characteristic oral findings that may be the first clinical sign of deficiency. As a dentist, you may be the first provider to notice glossitis, angular cheilitis, or gingival bleeding that signals a systemic nutritional problem. You're not diagnosing the vitamin deficiency — you're recognizing the oral signs and referring for workup.

Who Gets Vitamin Deficiencies?
  • Common in: People with alcohol use disorder (AUD), older adults, malabsorption syndromes, post-bariatric surgery, restrictive diets
  • High-risk populations for restrictive diets: Autism spectrum disorder, ARFID, OCD, schizophrenia, depression, institutionalized, incarcerated, unhoused, refugee populations
Two-Way Street

B12, iron, vitamin C → deficiency causes oral manifestations.
Vitamin A, B6, D, zinc, B12 → toxicity from over-supplementation causes problems too. Unsupervised supplementation is increasingly common and dangerous.

Vitamin Deficiencies

Deficiency Systemic Manifestations Oral Manifestations
Vitamin B12
  • Megaloblastic anemia (big immature RBCs)
  • Neurologic: paresthesias, ataxia, cognitive decline
  • Glossitis — atrophic or "Beefy Red" tongue
  • Angular cheilitis
  • Burning sensation
  • RAS (recurrent aphthous stomatitis)

  • Microcytic anemia (small pale RBCs)
  • Fatigue
  • Koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails)
  • Pica (craving non-food items like ice)
  • Atrophic glossitis
  • Angular cheilitis
  • Mucosal pallor
  • Plummer-Vinson syndrome
Vitamin C (Scurvy)
  • Fatigue
  • Perifollicular petechiae (bleeding around hair follicles)
  • Corkscrew hairs
  • Poor wound healing
  • Scorbutic gingivitis:
  • Pathognomonic Edematous, hemorrhagic, violaceous (purple) gingiva
  • Periodontal destruction with tooth mobility
Key Concepts Explained
  • "Beefy red" tongue (B12): The tongue loses its papillae (atrophy) and becomes smooth and intensely red. This is because B12 is needed for normal epithelial cell turnover. Without it, the rapidly dividing lingual papillae can't regenerate.
  • Plummer-Vinson syndrome (Iron): A triad of iron deficiency anemia + dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) + esophageal webs. The esophageal webs are thin mucosal membranes that partially obstruct the esophagus. Important because it increases the risk of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Why does scurvy destroy gingiva? Vitamin C is essential for collagen synthesis (specifically, it's a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase, the enzymes that crosslink collagen fibers). Without collagen crosslinking, connective tissue falls apart. The gingiva is highly vascular connective tissue → it swells, bleeds, and turns purple. Teeth lose periodontal support and become mobile.
  • Megaloblastic vs. microcytic anemia: B12 deficiency makes cells that are too big (mega = big) because they can't divide properly. Iron deficiency makes cells that are too small (micro = small) because there's not enough hemoglobin to fill them.

Excess Source Systemic Manifestations Oral Manifestations
Vitamin A Supplements; excessive liver intake Hepatotoxicity; increased ICP (intracranial pressure); teratogenicity Cheilitis, lip fissures; mucosal dryness and/or desquamation

Clinical Pearl — Zinc & Copper

Zinc and copper compete for the same absorption pathway in the gut. Taking too much zinc blocks copper absorption, causing copper deficiency. Copper deficiency then causes anemia, neutropenia, and neuropathy — mimicking B12 deficiency. Always ask patients about supplement use.

Clinical Approach

Workup

If deficiency suspected:

  • CBC with differential
  • Reticulocyte count
  • B12 level
  • Folate level
  • Iron studies (ferritin, total iron binding capacity)
  • Vitamin C level

If toxicity suspected:

  • Vitamin A level
  • B6 (plasma pyridoxal 5'-phosphate / PLP)
  • 25(OH)D
  • Calcium
  • Zinc
  • Copper
Treatment

Deficiency:

  • B12: 1000 μg IM/SQ (preferred if malabsorption) or oral high-dose if adherence is high
  • Iron: Ferrous sulfate 325 mg daily; IV if intolerant
  • Vitamin C: 500–1000 mg daily — rapid improvement within days
  • Dietary modification: ketchup, pizza, potatoes are equivalent in vitamin C to an orange or tangerine!

Toxicity:

  • Stop the offending supplement
  • Most symptoms are reversible
  • Exception: B6 neuropathy may persist even after stopping

Amyloidosis

Tier 2 — Moderate Priority
What Is It
Extracellular deposition of misfolded protein fibrils in tissues
Key Oral Finding
Macroglossia (enlarged tongue) in ~15–20% of AL type
Pathognomonic Sign
Periorbital purpura ("raccoon eyes")
Diagnostic Stain
Congo red → apple-green birefringence under polarized light
Plain English — What Is Amyloidosis?

Proteins normally fold into precise 3D shapes to function. In amyloidosis, certain proteins misfold into abnormal, insoluble fibrils that deposit in tissues throughout the body. Think of it like plaque building up in pipes — the deposits physically interfere with organ function. The type of amyloidosis depends on which protein is misfolding.

Types Relevant to Oral Medicine

Type Protein Source Key Association Where It Deposits
AL (Primary)
Most Common		 Immunoglobulin light chains from bone marrow plasma cell disorders Multiple myeloma Tongue, heart, kidneys, nerves, liver. Has oral manifestations. Clotting Factor X is adsorbed (bound) to amyloid fibrils → bleeding risk.
AA (Secondary) Serum amyloid A protein from chronic inflammation Rheumatoid arthritis, IBD, chronic infections Kidneys, spleen, liver, thyroid, heart
Aβ2M (Dialysis) β2-macroglobulin that can't be cleared by diseased kidneys Long-term dialysis patients Bones, joints, tendons; rarely deposits in TMJ
ATTR (Transthyretin) Transthyretin protein (hereditary or wild-type) Cardiac amyloidosis Primarily heart
Why AL Amyloidosis Matters Most for Oral Medicine

Macroglossia (enlarged tongue) occurs in ~15–20% of AL amyloidosis and may be the first clinical sign. An oral biopsy can diagnose systemic amyloidosis even without other oral symptoms. You could save a life by recognizing this.

Manifestations

Why the "Raccoon Eyes"?

Amyloid deposits weaken blood vessel walls. Minor trauma (even something as gentle as rubbing your eyes or bearing down during a bowel movement) causes the fragile vessels around the eyes to rupture, producing the characteristic bilateral periorbital bruising. This is pathognomonic — meaning if you see it, amyloidosis should be at the top of your differential.

Diagnosis & Clinical Management

Diagnosis
  • Incisional biopsy or labial salivary gland biopsy
    • Can diagnose systemic amyloidosis even without oral symptoms
    • Sensitivity: 58–86%
  • Pathognomonic Congo red stain → apple-green birefringence under polarized light
    • Congo red dye binds to amyloid fibrils. Under regular light, it appears red-orange. Under polarized light, amyloid shows apple-green birefringence (it glows green). This is the gold standard diagnostic finding.
  • If AL amyloidosis suspected: serum/urine protein electrophoresis (SPEP/SUEP), free light chains, bone marrow biopsy
Before Oral Procedures
  • Bleeding risk: Factor X deficiency (adsorbed to amyloid) → check PT/PTT, platelet count
  • Cardiac status: Restrictive cardiomyopathy → may need cardiac consultation
  • Renal function: Nephrotic syndrome → affects drug dosing
Treatment
  • Managed by heme/onc (hematology/oncology)
  • AL amyloidosis: Chemotherapy (daratumumab-based regimens), autoHSCT (autologous stem cell transplant)
  • Prognosis: Median survival 6 months if untreated with cardiac involvement — this is an aggressive disease

Peutz-Jeghers Syndrome (PJS)

Tier 2 — Moderate Priority
Genetics
STK11/LKB1 (tumor suppressor) gene mutation
Classic Triad
Mucocutaneous pigmentation + GI polyps + increased cancer risk
Cancer Risk
93% lifetime cancer risk (GI, breast, pancreas, ovary, lung)
Oral Significance
Perioral melanotic macules may be the FIRST sign, appearing in childhood
Plain English — What Is Peutz-Jeghers?

PJS is caused by a mutation in STK11/LKB1, a tumor suppressor gene. When this gene is broken, you get two problems: (1) hamartomatous polyps grow throughout the GI tract (hamartomas are benign overgrowths of normal tissue), and (2) cancer risk skyrockets to 93% lifetime. The oral finding that tips you off is a pattern of dark spots (melanotic macules) around the lips and inside the mouth, often appearing in early childhood, years before any GI symptoms develop.

Findings

Systemic Manifestations Oral Manifestations
GI hamartomatous polyps Perioral melanotic macules — lips, vermilion border
Intussusception, GI obstruction, bleeding Intraoral pigmentation — buccal mucosa, gingiva, palate
93% lifetime cancer risk (GI, breast, pancreas, ovary, lung) Macules are dark blue-brown, 1–5 mm
Polyps appear in childhood/adolescence Pigmentation appears in infancy/early childhood
Lip lesions may fade after puberty; intraoral lesions persist

Diagnosis & Differential

Diagnostic Features (Need any one)
  • ≥2 characteristic mucocutaneous pigmented macules
  • ≥2 PJS-type hamartomatous polyps in GI tract
  • Family history of PJS
DDx for Perioral Pigmentation — This Comes Up on Exams
Condition How to Distinguish from PJS
Racial/physiologic pigmentation Diffuse, symmetric, involves attached gingiva. No polyps, no cancer risk.
Addison disease Generalized hyperpigmentation (skin folds, mucosa). Systemic symptoms: fatigue, weight loss, hypotension. Due to excess ACTH stimulating melanocytes.
Laugier-Hunziker syndrome Benign. Similar perioral + nail pigmentation but no polyps and no cancer risk. Diagnosis of exclusion.
Melanotic macule (oral) Usually solitary, on lower lip. Benign. PJS has multiple macules.

Management

Cancer Surveillance (This is Why Recognition Matters)
  • Upper/lower endoscopy starting age 8–10, then every 2–3 years
  • Breast MRI, pancreatic surveillance, gynecologic screening
Oral Medicine Role
  • Recognize the pattern of perioral + intraoral melanotic macules
  • Ask about GI symptoms: abdominal pain, bleeding, obstruction
  • Refer to genetics/GI
  • No treatment needed for oral lesions — they're cosmetic only (laser if patient desires)
Why This Matters

A child comes in for a dental visit with dark spots around their lips and on their buccal mucosa. The parents haven't noticed anything unusual. You recognize the pattern as possible PJS, ask about GI symptoms, and refer. That referral triggers surveillance that catches a GI cancer early. This is oral medicine at its most impactful — you're not treating the spots, you're saving a life.

Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA)

Tier 2 — Moderate Priority · Formerly "Wegener's Granulomatosis"
What Is It
ANCA-associated small vessel vasculitis
Classic Triad
Upper respiratory + Lower respiratory + Kidneys (glomerulonephritis)
Pathognomonic Oral Sign
Strawberry gingivitis
Key Antibody
PR3-ANCA (c-ANCA) positive in >85% with systemic involvement
Prognosis
Untreated = fatal. Early immunosuppression dramatically improves outcomes.
Oral Prevalence
Oral manifestations in 6–13%; first sign in only 2%
Plain English — What Is GPA?

GPA is a disease where the immune system attacks small blood vessels (vasculitis) throughout the body. ANCA stands for Anti-Neutrophil Cytoplasmic Antibody — the immune system makes antibodies against proteins inside neutrophils (your own white blood cells). When these antibodies activate neutrophils inappropriately, the neutrophils attack the walls of small blood vessels, causing inflammation and tissue destruction. The disease classically targets three areas: the sinuses/nose (upper respiratory), the lungs (lower respiratory), and the kidneys (glomerulonephritis). Without treatment, GPA is fatal.

Critical Point

Untreated GPA = fatal. Early immunosuppression dramatically improves prognosis. Recognition is critical even though oral manifestations are the first sign in only 2% of patients.

Findings

Systemic Manifestations Oral Manifestations
Sinusitis — chronic, destructive; saddle nose deformity Pathognomonic Strawberry gingivitis
  • Erythematous to violaceous
  • Hyperplastic (swollen, enlarged)
  • Petechial (tiny red dots)
Pulmonary nodules/cavities → hemoptysis Mucosal ulceration — nonspecific, rapidly progressive
Glomerulonephritis — hematuria, renal failure Alveolar bone necrosis, tooth loss
Subglottic stenosis — stridor (high-pitched whistling) Palatal perforation, oro-antral communication
Otitis media — conductive hearing loss
What Does "Strawberry Gingivitis" Look Like?

The lecture showed a dramatic clinical photo: the gingiva is bright red to purple (erythematous to violaceous), swollen/overgrown (hyperplastic), and covered in tiny red dots (petechiae) — giving it a texture that resembles the surface of a strawberry. This appearance is pathognomonic for GPA. If you see gingiva that looks like a strawberry, GPA should be at the top of your differential — even though it's rare (6–13% of GPA patients).

What Is a Saddle Nose Deformity?

GPA causes chronic destructive sinusitis that can erode the nasal septum cartilage. When the septum collapses, the bridge of the nose sinks inward, creating a "saddled" or concave profile. The lecture also showed a photo of palatal perforation from GPA — the destructive inflammation literally eats through the hard palate, creating a communication between the mouth and the nasal/sinus cavity.

Diagnosis & Management

Diagnosis
  • Gingival biopsy rarely shows classic necrotizing granulomatous vasculitis — the histology from gingiva is often nonspecific. You need other tissue.
  • PR3-ANCA (c-ANCA): highly specific for GPA. The "c" stands for cytoplasmic pattern on immunofluorescence.
  • Urinalysis: hematuria, proteinuria (kidney involvement)
  • Chest imaging: pulmonary nodules/cavities
  • Biopsy: Nasal or lung tissue is more diagnostic than gingiva
Treatment (Managed by Rheumatology)
Treatment Protocol
  • Induction: Cyclophosphamide or rituximab + high-dose corticosteroids
  • Maintenance: Rituximab, azathioprine, or methotrexate
Oral Medicine Role
  • Recognize strawberry gingivitis — your awareness could lead to a life-saving diagnosis
  • Coordinate care: patients will be immunosuppressed; check CBC before procedures
  • Local management: oral lesions typically resolve with systemic treatment; ILST (intralesional steroid therapy) can be used for recalcitrant ulcers or nodules

Saddle Nose Defect — Differential Diagnosis

When the nasal bridge collapses
Most Likely Causes
  • Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA)
  • Relapsing polychondritis
  • Traumatic injury with septal hematoma/necrosis
  • Cocaine-induced septal destruction
  • Iatrogenic / post-surgical collapse
Important Not to Miss
  • Skeletal dysplasias (not true saddle nose; depressed ridge)
    • Achondroplasia
  • Nasal septal abscess
  • Invasive fungal infection (mucormycosis)
  • Congenital syphilis
  • Leprosy (Hansen disease)
Clinical Pearl

When you see a saddle nose deformity, your first thought should be GPA (especially if the patient also has sinusitis, respiratory symptoms, or oral findings). But always ask about cocaine use — cocaine causes vasoconstriction and ischemic necrosis of the nasal septum, producing a similar-looking defect. And don't forget mucormycosis in immunocompromised patients (especially uncontrolled diabetics) — the fungus invades blood vessels and destroys tissue rapidly.
