Section 01

HPV-Associated Benign Lesions

Four entities caused by different HPV subtypes — all benign, but know the distinctions

Squamous Papilloma High Yield

HPV 6 & 11 — Most common benign epithelial neoplasm of the oral cavity

Key Features

  • Pedunculated (on a stalk), well-circumscribed, exophytic
  • Finger-like projections — resembles a "sea anemone"
  • Usually white (keratinized) — can be pink if non-keratinized
  • Most common on: tongue, soft palate, uvula, lips
  • Typically solitary and <1 cm
  • Painless, slow-growing

Histopathology

  • Finger-like papillary projections of stratified squamous epithelium
  • Each projection has a fibrovascular connective tissue core
  • Surface may be keratinized or non-keratinized
  • No dysplasia

Treatment

  • Conservative surgical excision (including the base)
  • Recurrence is rare
  • No malignant transformation potential
📸
Clinical Appearance
Well-circumscribed, pedunculated, white papillary growth — "finger-like projections" visible. Classic tongue papilloma.

Condyloma Acuminatum Know This

HPV 6 & 11 — sexually transmitted, same HPV types as papilloma

Key Features

  • Sessile (broad-based) — unlike papilloma which is pedunculated
  • Larger and more clustered than papilloma
  • Pink, pebbly, cauliflower-like surface
  • Often multiple lesions
  • Same HPV types (6, 11) as papilloma — but sexually transmitted
  • More common on: lips, soft palate, lingual frenum

Histopathology

  • Broad, blunted rete ridges (vs. finger-like in papilloma)
  • Koilocytes — perinuclear clearing; hallmark of HPV infection
  • Acanthosis (thickened epithelium)

In children, oral condyloma should raise concern for sexual abuse. Must be evaluated in that context.

Papilloma = pedunculated, single, finger-like. Condyloma = sessile, multiple, cauliflower-like. Both are HPV 6/11 but condyloma is sexually transmitted and features koilocytes on histo.

Treatment

  • Surgical excision
  • Recurrence more common than papilloma
  • Evaluate for other STIs

Verruca Vulgaris (Common Wart)

HPV 2 & 4 — auto-inoculation from skin warts

Key Features

  • Usually on lips, anterior tongue, gingiva
  • White, rough, papillary surface
  • Often from auto-inoculation (patient bites skin warts, touches lips)
  • More common in children
  • HPV types 2 and 4 (different from papilloma/condyloma)

Histopathology

  • Prominent keratinization with a rough surface
  • Rete ridges tend to "cup" inward (point toward center of lesion)
  • Koilocytes present

Treatment

  • Excision, cryotherapy, or laser ablation
  • Concurrent skin warts should also be treated

Multifocal Epithelial Hyperplasia (Heck Disease) Know This

HPV 13 & 32 — distinct from the other HPV lesions

Key Features

  • Multiple soft, flat-topped, sessile papules/nodules
  • Same color as surrounding mucosa (mucosal-colored)
  • Widespread distribution — lips, buccal mucosa, tongue
  • More common in: Native American, Inuit, and Hispanic populations
  • Children and young adults predominantly
  • HPV types 13 and 32 — unique to this condition

Current HPV vaccines do NOT protect against HPV 13/32 — so Gardasil-9 does not prevent Heck Disease.

Treatment

  • Often self-limiting — may resolve spontaneously
  • Observation is acceptable
  • Imiquimod 5% topical 3×/wk (USC protocol) or CO2 laser
  • Excision only if symptomatic or cosmetically concerning

Section 02

Oral Potentially Malignant Disorders (OPMDs)

Leukoplakia, Erythroplakia, PVL, Oral Submucous Fibrosis, OLP, OLL

Leukoplakia High Yield

Clinical term — a diagnosis of exclusion

Definition (from lecture)

  • Demarcated white plaque that cannot be characterized clinically or pathologically as any other definable disease
  • Cannot be wiped off (distinguishes from pseudomembranous candidiasis)
  • Can be homogeneous or non-homogeneous
  • Frequently has fissures or folds
  • Dysplasia = precancer

Homogeneous vs. Non-Homogeneous

Homogeneous

Uniformly white, flat, thin

Smooth or slightly wrinkled

Lower risk of dysplasia

Transformation rate: ~5%

Non-Homogeneous

Mixed white and red (speckled/erythroleukoplakia)

Verrucous, nodular, or ulcerated surface

4.2–6.5× higher risk of malignant transformation

Transformation rate: ~25%

Non-homogeneous leukoplakia has 4.2–6.5× higher risk of malignant transformation than homogeneous leukoplakia. Always biopsy the RED component of a speckled leukoplakia — the red area is most likely to harbor dysplasia.

Sites of Concern

  • Floor of mouth and ventral/lateral tongue — highest risk locations
  • These are "high-risk" sites regardless of appearance

Malignant Transformation

  • Median time to cancer: 25 months
  • Overall transformation rate: 6.6–8.4%
  • Annual transformation rate: ~2% per year

Erythroplakia High Yield

The most dangerous OPMD — "dysplasia until proven otherwise"

Definition (from lecture)

  • Red macule or plaque that cannot be attributed to any other definable disease
  • Soft, velvety texture
  • Well-demarcated
  • Most common on: FOM, lateral tongue, soft palate
  • "Clinical term to describe dysplasia"
  • "Dysplasia until proven otherwise"
  • Median time to cancer: 3.7 months (vs. 25 months for leukoplakia)
  • Erythroplakia requires URGENT referral
  • 50% malignant transformation rate
  • 75–90% already cancer, CIS, or severe dysplasia at biopsy

Erythroplakia is far more likely to be malignant or pre-malignant than leukoplakia. 75–90% show severe dysplasia, carcinoma in situ, or invasive carcinoma at biopsy. DDx must rule out: atrophic glossitis, median rhomboid glossitis, plasma cell gingivitis, erosive lichen planus.

Proliferative Verrucous Leukoplakia (PVL) High Yield

The highest risk OPMD — 8× higher transformation than conventional leukoplakia

Key Features

  • Clinical-pathological diagnosis — requires clinical + pathologic correlation over time
  • Starts as simple leukoplakia → progressively becomes verrucous/warty
  • Multifocal — spreads to multiple sites over time
  • Resistant to treatment — high recurrence rate
  • More common in older women (unlike most oral cancers)
  • Often NOT associated with tobacco
  • Etiology unclear — no consistent HPV or tobacco association

PVL has the highest rate of malignant transformation among all OPMDs.

Transformation rate: 48–50% — 8× higher than conventional leukoplakia (9.3%/yr).

Diagnostic Criteria

Major Criteria
  • Leukoplakia with verrucous/warty features
  • Involves ≥2 oral subsites
  • Lesion has enlarged/spread over time
  • Recurrence in previously treated area
Minor Criteria
  • Spans ≥3 cm (all areas combined)
  • Female patient
  • Non-smoker
  • Duration >5 years

Progression Pattern

  • Simple leukoplakia → verrucous leukoplakia → verrucous carcinoma or SCC
  • Can transform to either verrucous carcinoma or conventional SCC
  • Requires long-term surveillance with repeat biopsies

Oral Submucous Fibrosis (OSF)

Strongly associated with areca nut/betel quid use

Key Features

  • Progressive fibrosis of the oral submucosa
  • Trismus — progressive inability to open the mouth (hallmark)
  • Blanching and stiffening of oral mucosa
  • Palpable fibrous bands — especially in buccal mucosa
  • Burning sensation, difficulty eating

Etiology

  • Betel quid / areca nut — primary causative agent
  • Arecoline (alkaloid in areca nut) stimulates fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis
  • Mechanism: areca alkaloids → fibroblasts produce a toxic microenvironment → collagen cross-linking & fibrosis (HIF-1α/2α, oxidative stress, EMT pathway)
  • Endemic in South/Southeast Asia, Pacific Islands

Malignant Transformation

  • Transformation rate: 5.1% (median 36 months)
  • Irreversible once fibrosis is established
  • Cessation of betel quid can slow but not reverse advanced disease

Oral Lichen Planus (OLP) Know This

Chronic inflammatory condition with low but real malignant potential

Clinical Features

  • Bilateral, symmetric distribution
  • Wickham's striae — white lace-like/reticular pattern (pathognomonic)
  • Erosive forms cause pain
  • Desquamative gingivitis — red, atrophic, sloughing gingiva
  • Most common sites: buccal mucosa, tongue, gingiva
  • Waxing and waning course

6 Clinical Subtypes

  • Reticular — white lace-like striae; most common; usually asymptomatic
  • Erosive — painful red ulcerated areas with overlying striae at periphery
  • Atrophic — thin, red mucosa
  • Plaque — can mimic leukoplakia
  • Papular — small white raised dots
  • Bullous — rare; fluid-filled vesicles/bullae

Malignant Transformation

  • Transformation rate: 0.2–1.4% (controversial)
  • Erosive forms carry higher risk
  • Controversial — some argue transformation cases were actually OLL (lichenoid lesions)

Oral Lichenoid Lesion (OLL)

Clinically mimics OLP but has identifiable cause — and higher malignant potential

Key Differences from OLP

  • Identifiable cause — drug reaction, dental material contact, GVHD
  • Often unilateral (vs. bilateral in OLP)
  • May resolve when causative agent is removed
  • Histologically can show deeper lymphocytic infiltrate and eosinophils

Common Causes

  • Medications: NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, antimalarials, oral hypoglycemics
  • Dental materials: amalgam restorations (contact lichenoid reaction)
  • GVHD (graft-vs-host disease)

"Ask the pathologist about architectural dysplasia" — OLL can harbor dysplasia that is morphologically subtle. When you get a biopsy back reading "lichenoid," communicate with pathology about whether dysplastic features are present.

3 Subtypes by Trigger

  • Contact lichenoid — dental restoration / amalgam contact
  • Drug-induced — NSAIDs, antihypertensives, antimalarials
  • GVHD-associated — post-allogeneic HSCT

Malignant Transformation

  • Transformation rate: 1.2–1.4%; 5.1% with dysplasia
  • Workup: remove the trigger; if persists 6–8 weeks → biopsy

39.6% of oral cancers arise from leukoplakia WITHOUT dysplasia. Absence of dysplasia does NOT mean safe — long-term follow-up is essential.

Resolution Data

  • Mild dysplasia: 18–39% had spontaneous resolution
  • All dysplasias: 32.5% had reduction in size or spontaneous resolution (10-year follow-up)
  • 18.6% completely disappeared; 12.7% reduced in size or clinical severity

Risk Factors for Malignant Transformation of OPMDs

Clinical Risk Factors

  • Non-homogeneous type (speckled, verrucous, nodular)
  • Location: floor of mouth, ventral/lateral tongue
  • Size >200 mm²
  • Female sex (higher transformation rate in some studies)
  • Non-smoker paradox — idiopathic leukoplakia in non-smokers may transform more often
  • Presence of dysplasia on biopsy
  • Presence of erythematous component

Histologic Risk Factors

  • Severity of dysplasia (mild → moderate → severe → CIS)
  • Presence of Candida superinfection
  • Loss of heterozygosity (LOH) at chromosomal loci 3p, 9p

The "non-smoker paradox" is counter-intuitive: leukoplakia in non-smokers is actually MORE likely to transform. The theory is that tobacco-associated leukoplakia may be a reactive response (hyperkeratosis) while idiopathic leukoplakia reflects intrinsic genetic instability.

Section 04

Biopsy Technique

When, where, and how to biopsy oral lesions

Biopsy Principles High Yield

Where to Biopsy

  • For mixed lesions: biopsy the RED component (most likely to show dysplasia)
  • For ulcerated lesions: biopsy the ulcer margin with adjacent intact epithelium — lets the pathologist see both the ulcer bed AND epithelial changes at the transition zone
  • Avoid necrotic center — non-diagnostic tissue
  • Include perilesional normal tissue for comparison

No association between incisional biopsy and 5-year survival or recurrence in Stage I/II OSCC. Tumor seeding is extremely rare — ~0.00012% for FNA, ~0.0011% for core biopsy. Do NOT delay biopsy out of fear of "spreading" cancer.

Biopsy Types

  • Incisional biopsy — standard for large lesions or suspected malignancy; removes a representative piece
  • Excisional biopsy — complete removal; for small (<1 cm), clinically benign-appearing lesions
  • Punch biopsy — quick, standardized depth; good for mucosal lesions
  • Brush biopsy (OralCDx) — screening adjunct only; NOT a definitive diagnosis tool

Important Principles

  • Always submit tissue for histopathologic examination — clinical diagnosis alone is not sufficient
  • Photograph the lesion BEFORE biopsy
  • Orient the specimen for the pathologist (suture or ink marking)
  • Include clinical information: size, site, duration, risk factors

Dysplasia Management

Grading, treatment modalities, and surveillance protocols

Epithelial Dysplasia Grading

WHO Grading System

Grade Extent 5yr MT Rate Management
Mild Lower third of epithelium 2.6–11.9% Close follow-up (3–6 months)
Moderate Lower two-thirds 4.1–8.7% Consider excision, close follow-up
Severe >Two-thirds of epithelium 32.2% Excision recommended
Carcinoma in situ Full thickness (no invasion) Very High Excision with clear margins

Architectural vs. Cytologic Features of Dysplasia

Architectural Changes
  • Irregular epithelial stratification
  • Drop-shaped rete ridges
  • Bulbous rete ridges
  • Loss of polarity of basal cells
  • Dyskeratosis (premature keratinization)
Cytologic Changes
  • Nuclear pleomorphism
  • Increased nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio
  • Prominent nucleoli
  • Abnormal/increased mitotic figures
  • Hyperchromatism

Think of dysplasia on a spectrum: the higher the grade, the more layers are abnormal. When changes reach full thickness without breaking through the basement membrane, it's carcinoma in situ. Once it invades through, it's invasive SCC.

Dysplasia Treatment Modalities High Yield

Modality Key Data Notes
Scalpel excision OR = 3.6 for recurrence Standard of care; higher recurrence than laser/ablative
CO2 laser HR = 0.14 for recurrence; 52.6% recurrence rate Lowest recurrence; 86% reduction vs. scalpel
Diode laser 93.5% CR; 6.5% recurrence Excellent tissue response
Cryotherapy 100% CR for lesions <2 cm² Limited to small, superficial lesions
Photodynamic therapy (PDT) 66–100% CR; 8.3% MT at 3 yr ALA-based; non-invasive option

CO2 laser has the best evidence for lowest recurrence. Scalpel excision remains standard of care but has the highest recurrence rate among surgical options.

Section 07

Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

Epidemiology, risk factors, clinical presentation, and staging

Epidemiology High Yield

  • ~90% of oral malignancies are SCC
  • Global incidence: ~36,600/year (oral cavity + oropharynx)
  • Mortality: ~58,500 oral + ~12,200 oropharyngeal deaths/year
  • Oral tongue is now the #1 site — 41.7% of oral cavity cancers
  • Male:Female ratio historically ~2:1 (gap narrowing)
  • Incidence increases with age — peak in 6th–7th decade
  • Rising incidence in younger patients (<40), especially tongue SCC

>50% present with regional or distant metastases at diagnosis. This is why early detection matters — by the time most patients present, the cancer has already spread.

Risk Factors for Oral SCC High Yield

Major Risk Factors

Tobacco + Alcohol: Account for 70–75% of oral cavity cancers — their effect is synergistic (multiplicative), not merely additive.

Risk Factor Details
Tobacco All forms — smoked (cigarettes, cigars, pipes), smokeless (chewing, snuff), reverse smoking. Contains >70 known carcinogens (nitrosamines, PAHs, etc.)
Alcohol Independent risk factor. Acts as solvent to increase mucosal permeability to carcinogens. Acetaldehyde (metabolite) is directly mutagenic.
Betel quid / areca nut OR = 9.9 (without tobacco). IARC Group 1 carcinogen. Arecoline damages DNA.
HPV (types 16, 18) Primarily oropharyngeal SCC (base of tongue, tonsils). Less significant for oral cavity SCC. E6/E7 oncoproteins inactivate p53 and Rb.
UV radiation Lip (vermilion) SCC. Actinic cheilitis → lip SCC pathway.
Immunosuppression Transplant recipients, HIV/AIDS — increased risk of all cancers including oral SCC. HSCT recipients: SIR = 7–16; chronic GVHD adds 2.8× additional risk.
Prior cancer 16% increased risk of subsequent cancers. Childhood cancer survivors: SIR = 5.0 (leukemia: SIR = 19.2).
Diet Mediterranean diet ↓ risk by 43%. Western/pro-inflammatory diet ↑ risk by 62%. Low fruit/vegetable intake may account for 54–65% of oral cancers.
Family history FDR with HNC: OR = 1.7–3.8; sibling with HNC: OR = 2.2–8.6. ≥3 FDRs with cancer at any site: OR = 3.79.

Genetic Predisposition Syndromes

  • Fanconi anemia — OR = 40.6 (extremely elevated risk)
  • Dyskeratosis congenita
  • Li-Fraumeni syndrome
  • Cowden syndrome
  • Xeroderma pigmentosum

Clinical Presentation of Oral SCC

Classic Features

  • Non-healing ulcer — present for >2–3 weeks (most common presentation)
  • Indurated (hard) margins — "woody" feel on palpation
  • Rolled/raised borders
  • Can present as: exophytic mass, endophytic ulcer, red patch, white patch, or mixed
  • Often painless in early stages (pain = late sign suggesting perineural invasion)
  • Fixation to underlying structures in advanced cases

Suspicious Signs

  • Any oral ulcer persisting >2–3 weeks without identifiable cause
  • Unexplained numbness or paresthesia (lip, chin, tongue)
  • Unexplained tooth mobility
  • Trismus
  • Dysphagia, odynophagia
  • Cervical lymphadenopathy (firm, fixed, non-tender)

Stage Size Depth of Invasion (DOI)
T1 ≤2 cm DOI ≤5 mm
T2 ≤2 cm (DOI >5–10 mm) or 2–4 cm (DOI ≤10 mm) DOI 5–10 mm

DOI ≠ tumor thickness. DOI is measured from the basement membrane of adjacent normal mucosa down to the deepest point of invasion. A small tumor (≤2 cm) with deep invasion (>5 mm) is upstaged to T2. DOI independently predicts nodal metastasis and survival.

  • M1 — Distant metastasis present (lungs most common)

SCC Treatment Overview

Primary Treatment by Stage

  • Early (Stage I–II): Surgery OR radiation (single modality)
  • Locally advanced (Stage III–IV): Surgery + adjuvant radiation ± chemotherapy
  • Unresectable: Definitive chemoradiation or palliative care

Surgical Considerations

  • Goal: complete excision with clear margins (ideally ≥5 mm pathologically)
  • Neck dissection — selective or modified radical for N+ disease or high risk of occult mets
  • Reconstruction: local flaps, free tissue transfer (radial forearm, fibula, ALT)

See AJCC 8th Edition stage groupings table above for survival by stage (I: 80–90%, II: 70–80%, III: 60–70%, IVA: 40–55%, IVB: 25–35%, IVC: <20%).

Section 08

UV-Associated Lip SCC

Actinic cheilitis and lip cancer

Lip SCC High Yield

Lip SCC comprises 25–30% of all oral cancers.

Why the Lip is Vulnerable

  • Lower lip receives 3× more UV exposure than upper lip
  • Vermilion border lacks melanin, has thin stratum corneum → minimal natural protection
  • Oral mucosa has lower DNA repair capacity than skin → higher carcinogenic risk from UV

Actinic Cheilitis (Precursor)

  • Chronic UV damage to the lower lip vermilion
  • Blurring of the vermilion border
  • Atrophy, dryness, scaling, crusting
  • Actinic cheilitis → lip SCC: MT rate 10–30%
  • >90% of AC patients have NEVER used lip photoprotection

Lip SCC Features

  • Lower lip >> upper lip
  • Better prognosis than intraoral SCC (earlier detection, less metastasis)
  • More common in: fair-skinned individuals, outdoor workers (farmers, fishermen)
  • Often presents as a non-healing crusted ulcer or exophytic mass on the vermilion
  • Women get less lip cancer — "lipstick hypothesis" (lipstick = de facto sunscreen, p=0.0008)

Actinic Cheilitis & Lip SCC (Lecture Slides)
Three clinical photos showing progressive lip pathology: (L) diffuse erythema, crusting, and atrophy of the lower lip vermilion with blurred vermilion border (actinic cheilitis); (R top) focal crusted ulcer on the lower lip with surrounding atrophic change; (R bottom) larger crusted/ulcerated lesion on the lower lip with irregular margins consistent with established lip SCC.

Prevention

SPF 30+ broad-spectrum lip balm — reapply every 2 hours during sun exposure.

  • Lip protection >1×/day halves lip cancer risk
  • Physical + chemical protection ↓ actinic cheilitis by 33–36%
  • Wide-brimmed hats — reduces direct UV to lower lip
  • Avoid peak UV hours (10 AM – 2 PM)
  • Treat actinic cheilitis early (vermilionectomy, topical 5-FU, imiquimod, cryotherapy)
Section 09

Referral & Workup

What to send with a referral for a suspicious lesion

Referral Checklist

Include ALL of the following when referring a suspicious oral lesion:

  • Lesion description — size, color, surface texture, margins, induration
  • Clinical photographs
  • Radiographs (if bone involvement suspected)
  • Duration — how long the lesion has been present
  • Signs/symptoms — pain, numbness, dysphagia, bleeding, etc.
  • Risk factors — tobacco, alcohol, HPV status, betel quid, sun exposure, family history, genetic conditions

Standard Workup

  • Biopsy (incisional for large/suspicious, excisional for small/benign-appearing)
  • CT with contrast — bone involvement, node evaluation
  • MRI — soft tissue extent, perineural spread
  • PET-CT — distant metastasis detection, recurrence surveillance
  • Chest imaging — lung metastasis screening
  • Panoramic radiograph / CBCT — jaw involvement

HPV-Mediated SCC

Oropharyngeal SCC driven by HPV 16 — testing, prognosis, and vaccination

HPV in Oral vs. Oropharyngeal SCC High Yield

  • Oropharynx (tonsils, base of tongue): 60–70% HPV+ → DO test for HPV (p16 IHC)
  • Oral cavity (tongue, FOM, gingiva, buccal): ~2–4% HPV+ → Do NOT routinely test for HPV

E6/E7 Molecular Mechanism

  • High-risk HPV 16/18 integrate into host genome → oncoprotein expression
  • E6 → degrades p53 via E6-AP ubiquitin ligase → loss of apoptosis / DNA damage response
  • E7 → inactivates Rb → releases E2F → uncontrolled cell cycle progression
  • Net effect: oncogenic transformation in oropharyngeal reticulated epithelium (tonsillar crypts, base of tongue)

Key Features

  • Driven by HPV-16 (and to a lesser extent HPV-18)
  • p16 positive on immunohistochemistry — surrogate marker for HPV involvement
  • Typically in younger, non-smoking patients
  • Male predominance (3:1)
  • Often presents as painless neck mass (metastatic node) before primary is apparent

HPV+ OPSCC Patient Profile

  • Younger: median age 54 (vs. 66 for HPV−)
  • Male, non-smoker
  • Small/occult primary but cystic neck nodes at presentation

HPV+ vs. HPV− Oropharyngeal SCC

Feature HPV+ OPSCC HPV− OPSCC
Typical patient Younger, non-smoker, male Older, heavy tobacco/alcohol
Site Oropharynx (tonsils, BOT) Oral cavity, larynx
p16 Positive Negative
3-Year OS 82% 57%
Response to treatment More radiosensitive Standard

HPV+ OSCC (oral cavity) has NO survival benefit — HPV status is irrelevant to outcome in oral cavity SCC. The survival advantage only applies to oropharyngeal SCC.

HPV Vaccination High Yield

Gardasil 9 (9-Valent Vaccine)

  • FDA-approved for prevention of oropharyngeal & head/neck cancers
  • Covers HPV types: 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58
  • 88% reduction in oral HPV 16/18/6/11 infection
  • 80% reduction in oral HPV-16 infection specifically
  • Does NOT cover HPV 13/32 (Heck Disease) or HPV 2/4 (verruca vulgaris)

Schedule

Age Doses
9–14 years 2 doses
15–26 years 3 doses
27–45 years Shared decision-making

Emerging: Vaccination After Diagnosis

  • Prophylactic vaccine as adjuvant (Gardasil 9 after treatment) — prevents re-infection at other mucosal sites
  • 9/12 studies showed decreased recurrence in HPV-related disease
  • RCTs needed to confirm benefit

Therapeutic Vaccines (Investigational)

  • Target E6/E7 oncoproteins to induce T-cell response
  • Phase I–III trials ongoing
  • PDS0101 + pembrolizumab showing promise
Section 11

Verrucous Carcinoma

Low-grade SCC variant with excellent prognosis

Verrucous Carcinoma High Yield

Low-grade variant of SCC — approximately 2–3% of oral carcinomas.

Clinical Features

  • Exophytic, broad-based, warty/verrucous growth with "cauliflower-like" surface
  • White or white-pink surface with deep clefts
  • Well-differentiated — shows minimal cytologic atypia
  • Locally aggressive — pushes into tissue with broad, well-demarcated "pushing" margins
  • Virtually never metastasizes

Demographics & Location

  • Older adults: mean age 67–70 years
  • Slight male predominance, but female predominance in snuff-dipping populations
  • Buccal mucosa (~59%), vestibule, alveolar ridge/gingiva
  • Often at site of smokeless tobacco placement

Risk Factors

  • Smokeless tobacco, betel quid / areca nut
  • May arise from PVL in non-tobacco users

Prognosis

Metric Value
Cervical LN metastasis 0% (pooled, 277 patients)
Distant metastasis 0% (pooled, 712 patients)
5-year survival (localized, surgery) 78–89%
Local recurrence 7–38% — close follow-up required

Treatment

  • Surgery alone is treatment of choice — wide local excision with negative margins
  • Neck dissection generally NOT indicated (no metastatic potential)
  • No survival benefit from postoperative radiation

Superficial biopsies may miss the diagnosis — need a deep or excisional biopsy to see the full lesion and demonstrate the characteristic pushing margins. A shallow punch biopsy may only show well-differentiated epithelium that looks benign.

Histopathology

  • Well-differentiated squamous epithelium
  • Broad, bulbous, "pushing" rete ridges extending into CT
  • Heavy keratinization with keratin-filled crypts ("church spire" keratosis)
  • Minimal dysplasia or cytologic atypia
  • No invasion through basement membrane in the classic sense (pushes rather than infiltrates)

Think of verrucous carcinoma as a "well-behaved cancer" — it grows locally and pushes tissue aside rather than invading destructively. It almost never metastasizes, so the prognosis with surgery is excellent. The catch: you need a deep biopsy to diagnose it, and radiation can potentially induce anaplastic transformation to conventional SCC.

Section 12

Oral Melanoma

Rare but highly aggressive — very different from cutaneous melanoma

Oral Mucosal Melanoma High Yield

Key Facts

  • <1% of all melanomas, ~0.5% of oral malignancies — rare but deadly
  • 5-year survival: ~20–34% (vs. ~90% for cutaneous melanoma)
  • UV radiation is NOT a risk factor (unlike cutaneous melanoma)
  • Etiology unknown

Clinical Features

  • Demographics: mean age 60–70, slight male predominance (1.5:1)
  • Location: hard palate (~50%), maxillary gingiva (~30%)
  • Appearance: pigmented (brown/black) macule, patch, or nodular mass
  • ~15% are amelanotic (worse prognosis — harder to diagnose)
  • Often asymptomatic early → bleeding, ulceration, swelling, loose teeth in advanced disease

Why Prognosis is So Poor

  • Diagnosed late (hidden location, asymptomatic early)
  • Rich vascular/lymphatic supply → early distant metastasis
  • Different molecular profile than cutaneous melanoma: c-KIT mutations common; BRAF rare → less responsive to targeted therapies

Distinguishing from Benign Pigmented Lesions

  • Physiologic/racial pigmentation — bilateral, symmetric, stable, present since youth
  • Oral melanotic macule — small, flat, well-demarcated, stable
  • Amalgam tattoo — history of nearby restoration, stable, radiographically visible metal particles
  • Smoker's melanosis — diffuse, anterior labial gingiva, associated with tobacco

Any new or changing pigmented lesion on the palate or gingiva in an older adult warrants biopsy — especially if:

  • Asymmetric, irregular borders
  • Color variation (brown, black, red, amelanotic areas)
  • Growing or ulcerated
  • ≥1 cm in size

Treatment

  • Wide local excision with negative margins — mainstay
  • Adjuvant RT improves local control but NOT overall survival
  • Immunotherapy (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) used but less effective than in cutaneous melanoma
  • Neck dissection: no consistent survival benefit; reserved for clinically positive nodes only
  • Prognosis remains poor regardless of treatment modality
Case Studies

Clinical Cases from Lecture

Case: Squamous Papilloma

Patient presents with a solitary, pedunculated, white papillary growth on the lateral tongue. It has been present for several months and is asymptomatic.

  • Diagnosis: Squamous papilloma (HPV 6/11)
  • Management: Conservative surgical excision at the base
  • Follow-up: Recurrence is rare

Case: Erythroplakia on Floor of Mouth

A 58-year-old male with 40 pack-year smoking history presents with a well-demarcated red, velvety patch on the floor of the mouth, noticed incidentally on exam. Asymptomatic.

  • Next step: Urgent referral + biopsy
  • Rationale: Erythroplakia is "dysplasia until proven otherwise." Over 90% show severe dysplasia, CIS, or invasive SCC. Median time to cancer is only 3.7 months.
  • Location risk: Floor of mouth is one of the highest-risk sites

Case: Verrucous Carcinoma

A 72-year-old male with a history of decades of smokeless tobacco use presents with a large, exophytic, warty white lesion on the buccal mucosa. Initial punch biopsy reads "well-differentiated squamous epithelium, no dysplasia."

  • Problem: Superficial biopsy may miss verrucous carcinoma
  • Next step: Deep incisional or excisional biopsy to evaluate pushing margins
  • Treatment: WLE alone — no neck dissection, no RT needed
  • Prognosis: Excellent — 0% nodal/distant mets, 78–89% 5yr survival

Case: Pigmented Lesion on Palate

A 65-year-old male presents with a new, asymmetric, dark brown macule on the hard palate that has been growing over the past 6 months. The borders are irregular with color variation.

  • Concern: Oral melanoma until proven otherwise
  • Next step: Urgent biopsy
  • Key facts: Hard palate is #1 site for oral melanoma; 5yr survival only 20–34%; UV is NOT a risk factor; c-KIT mutations common (BRAF rare)
  • Treatment if confirmed: WLE; adjuvant RT for local control (not OS); immunotherapy less effective than cutaneous
