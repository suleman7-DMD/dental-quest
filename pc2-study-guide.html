OBJ A1

Understand the Skeletal Anatomy of the Thorax

Prof walked through this as a physical palpation exercise, literally top-down on the body. Spent significant time — clearly important for both the pulmonary and cardiac exams.

The Landmark Palpation Sequence

This is the foundation for EVERYTHING in both cardiac and pulmonary exams. The professor presented it as a step-by-step sequence you'd perform on a real patient:

Suprasternal Notch→ Manubrium→ Sternal Angle (Angle of Louis)→ 2nd Rib (laterally)→ Count Interspaces downward

Suprasternal Notch: "If you went down your neck and went right where you have that notch there, that is your suprasternal notch." This divot at the base of the neck, above the sternum, is your absolute starting point.

Manubrium: From the suprasternal notch, slide inferiorly along the sternum. The manubrium is the top segment of the sternum, from the notch down to the first indentation you feel.

Sternal Angle (Angle of Louis): That indentation where the manubrium meets the body of the sternum. This is a CRITICAL landmark. The professor emphasized: "If you were on that suprasternal notch and keep going inferiorly until you feel that indentation... go a little bit to your right, you're gonna feel a hard area that is gonna be your second rib." Where the 2nd rib connects with the manubrium = the Angle of Louis = the sternal angle.

Interspaces — NAMING CONVENTION: The professor was very deliberate here: "Interspace 2 is UNDER the second rib." The interspace is ALWAYS named for the rib ABOVE it. So interspace 2 = below rib 2, interspace 3 = below rib 3, interspace 4 = below rib 4. This matters hugely for cardiac auscultation (valve locations).

As you count down interspaces, move your fingers LATERALLY (outward from the sternum). "Otherwise you're gonna get too close to the sternum, you may not be able to feel those areas, and then you're also gonna lose it at 7, 8, 9, because those ribs do not attach to the sternum."

Rib Attachments

RibsAttachmentType
1–7Attach directly to the sternumTrue ribs
8, 9, 10Each attaches to the rib above it (8→7, 9→8, 10→9)False ribs
11, 12Attach posteriorly to vertebrae, but NOT anteriorlyFree floating

Xiphoid Process: The inferior tip of the sternum.

"When you're doing your CPR, don't be that low because you don't wanna break that off because that's gonna cause a collapsed lung." Breaking the xiphoid during CPR → pneumothorax. Classic MCQ distractor.

Costochondral Angle: Where the costal cartilage meets the ribs at the inferior margin of the rib cage.

Why the 1st rib is hard to find: "It usually sits under the clavicle." That's precisely why you use the sternal angle → 2nd rib method instead of trying to count down from rib 1.

Bronchial Tree — Trachea to Alveoli

The professor walked through the airway anatomy from top to bottom:

  • Trachea descends from the larynx and bifurcates at the Carina into the right and left main stem bronchi.
  • Carina: "Heavily innervated — that will throw off a cough reflex to help prevent stuff from getting down into the lungs." This is a protective mechanism.
  • Right main stem bronchus = "almost a straight shot." Left = "basically goes off to a right angle."
"If somebody aspirates something, it gets below the cords, it gets into the carina area, there's a good chance it's gonna go to the right lung." — Aspirated foreign bodies preferentially go RIGHT because the right bronchus is wider, shorter, and more vertical. Classic board question.

Progressive Branching to the Alveoli

Main Stem Bronchi→ Lobar Bronchi→ Segmental Bronchi→ Bronchioles→ Terminal Bronchioles→ Respiratory Bronchioles→ Alveolar Ducts→ Alveolar Sacs→ Alveoli
  • Each alveolus surrounded by a capillary network that brings blood into close proximity with inhaled air.
  • Approximately 200 million alveoli per lung.
  • Extremely thin layer between capillary and alveolus where gas exchange occurs.

OBJ A4

Describe the Differences Between Internal and External Respiration

Prof spent moderate time here — repeated the gas exchange concept multiple times, fielded a student question, and made the critical N₂O connection.
TypeDefinitionLocation
External RespirationExchange of O₂ and CO₂ between an organism and the external environmentLungs — alveoli ↔ capillaries
Internal Respiration (Cellular)Utilization of oxygen by cells — cells use O₂ and produce CO₂Tissue level — cells ↔ capillaries

Gas Exchange — The Partial Pressure Principle

The slide states: "The exchange of gases in the alveoli depends entirely on the diffusion of gases across membranes and is controlled by the partial pressures of the respective gases on either side of the alveolar membrane."

The professor explained it simply: "Gases move from a high partial pressure to a low partial pressure." A student asked for clarification, and he added: "It's not gonna be zero, but as long as there's a delta, like there's a difference, it's gonna push from high to low."

  • Deoxygenated blood (high CO₂, low O₂) arrives at lungs via pulmonary arteries
  • O₂ is HIGH in alveolus, LOW in capillary blood → O₂ diffuses INTO capillary
  • CO₂ is HIGH in capillary blood, LOW in alveolus → CO₂ diffuses OUT into alveolus → exhaled
  • Oxygenated blood returns via pulmonary veins → left side of heart → body
  • At the cellular level: cells take in O₂ and give off CO₂ → that deoxygenated blood returns to right heart → lungs → cycle repeats
Critical N₂O foreshadow: "It's important to remember these things because when we talk about nitrous oxide, it's gonna be the same thing. You're gonna have a high partial pressure nitrous oxide that we're given to our patients, and there's nothing that's in the bloodstream, so it's gonna be a quick uptake." — Expect an MCQ linking partial pressure gradients to rapid N₂O uptake.

Room Air Composition (Prof quizzed the class on this)

21% O₂, ~78% nitrogen, very small amount of CO₂. "There's a partial pressure still, there's still partial pressure of that in the air." He asked this directly — fair game for MCQ.

Steps of Respiration (5 steps from slide — prof quickly ran through this diagram)

  • 1. Exchange of air between atmosphere and lungs (ventilation)
  • 2. O₂ and CO₂ alveolar exchange by diffusion (gas exchange)
  • 3. O₂ and CO₂ transport via pulmonary and systemic circulations (gas transport)
  • 4. O₂ and CO₂ exchange in tissue capillaries (gas exchange at tissues)
  • 5. Cellular utilization (cellular respiration)

2. Palpation

Prof spent significant time here — gave detailed hands-on descriptions of both expansion and fremitus technique.

Four things you palpate for: Pain, Abnormalities (masses/lesions), Expansion, Fremitus.

Expansion Technique (detailed):

  • Patient's back facing you
  • Place your hands together, thumbs meeting at midline
  • Pinch up some skin at midline (~8 cm apart)
  • Ask patient to breathe in → watch for symmetrical expansion both sides
  • DO NOT press hard and restrict their movement — "the idea is not to prevent them, but just let it sit there and see what happens"

Fremitus Technique (detailed):

  • Fremitus = palpable vibration
  • Place the ball of your hand on the patient's back
  • Have patient say "99"
  • Compare one side to the other — this is the key principle
  • Use the 1-1, 2-2, 3-3, 4-4 pattern (same level, left vs. right)
  • Must be over lung fields, NOT over bone — "if it's over the bone, it may make it different than if you're trying to feel over the lungs"
  • Can also be done anteriorly
"This isn't like the best exam out of all the exams to do" — fremitus alone isn't super reliable. But it directs you where to focus auscultation. "If I have an increase or decreased from this... I can check that out."

3. Percussion

Prof spent a LOT of time here — very detailed technique description. He clearly enjoys teaching this. "This is where you see a lot of it."

Technique (step-by-step as prof described):

  • Non-dominant hand: Place middle finger (the "pleximeter") flat on the chest wall
  • ONLY the finger touches — thumb, other fingers, palm of hand OFF the body
  • Strike at the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint area of that finger
  • Dominant hand: Use middle finger, "it's basically almost like throwing it, throwing your hand" — flick the wrist: tap tap, tap tap
  • "This takes some practice — that's why people would practice on textbooks"
  • "If you have long fingernails, you're gonna cut yourself up"

Two sounds you're listening for:

SoundMeaningProf's Analogy
ResonantAir-filled space underneath = normal lung"If you had your cheeks puckered out and you did it there, you're gonna hear some resonant sound"
DullSolid or fluid-filled = abnormal (or below diaphragm)"If you're percussing and put it up against your thigh, it's gonna sound flat"

Where things are resonant vs. dull:

  • Above the diaphragm (over the lungs) = resonant (normal patient)
  • Below the diaphragm = dull (no air-filled lung tissue)
Clinical application: Percuss downward → find where resonance transitions to dullness (that's the diaphragm level) → have patient take a deep breath → percuss below the previous dull area. If now resonant = lungs are expanding. Another way to check lung expansion.

Positioning tips: Upper back = stand behind patient. Lower back = move to the SIDE of the patient so you can reach the lower fields more easily.

4. Auscultation

Prof introduced auscultation concepts at moderate pace, then went into detailed stethoscope teaching — "this is probably like the most important exam if you're doing the pulmonary examination."

Normal breath sounds (from slide):

  • Vesicular: Soft, low-pitched, heard over most lung fields. Normal breathing sound.
  • Bronchial: Louder, higher-pitched, normally heard over trachea/large airways. If heard over peripheral lung fields = abnormal (suggests consolidation).

Adventitious sounds = sounds that shouldn't be there (detailed in next section).

The Ladder Pattern (Used for BOTH Percussion AND Auscultation)

Side-to-side comparison: 1-1, 2-2, 3-3 — always comparing one side to the other at the same level. "You're going basically back and forth in the same areas looking for differences from one side to the other."

Know the Difference Between Systole, Diastole & What Makes S1 & S2

Prof spent the MOST time on this section of the entire cardiovascular lecture. He walked through the entire pressure-valve logic step by step, using Bates pressure curve diagrams. "It's important because we're gonna build off of this."
VentriclesAtria
Systole (S1 → S2)CONTRACTINGRelaxing / filling
Diastole (S2 → S1)RELAXING / fillingContracting

Prof's critical clarification: "The blood that comes from the atrium is not what fills up the heart. You're always gonna have some blood that's left in the heart and the ventricles." The atrial kick contributes, but it's not the sole filling source.

S1 — "Lub" — Beginning of Systole (Prof's Complete Walkthrough)

  • We're in late diastole. Atria contract (that little bump before S1 on the pressure curve). Blood pushed into ventricles.
  • Ventricles begin to contract → ventricular pressure RISES
  • When ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure → MITRAL and TRICUSPID valves CLOSE
  • That closure = S1 (lub) = beginning of systole
  • "If you had to pick, which one are you hearing mostly?" → Mitral, because of higher pressure on the left side. "But it's both, just put them both there."

Mid-Systole

  • Pressure continues to increase in the ventricle
  • When ventricular pressure exceeds pressure in the aorta and pulmonary artery → AORTIC and PULMONIC valves OPEN (silently)
  • Blood ejected out to body (aorta) and lungs (pulmonary artery)

S2 — "Dub" — End of Systole

  • "All of a sudden there's no more fluid going out."
  • Ventricular pressure drops → now aortic/pulmonary artery pressure exceeds ventricular pressure
  • AORTIC and PULMONIC valves CLOSE
  • That closure = S2 (dub) = end of systole
  • "Most of the times you're gonna hear that second heart sound is gonna be from the aortic valve."

The Golden Rules

  • Heart sounds are made by valves CLOSING, not opening. Valves open silently.
  • S1 = Mitral + Tricuspid CLOSE (mostly Mitral heard) = START of systole
  • S2 = Aortic + Pulmonic CLOSE (mostly Aortic heard) = END of systole
  • lub-DUB: between lub and dub = systole. After dub = diastole.
"Instead of just memorizing, you start thinking how this works, it'll make it so much easier when it gets to heart murmurs." — He's telling you the exam requires UNDERSTANDING the pressure logic, not just memorizing.

S3 — Rapid Ventricular Filling

  • Occurs in DIASTOLE — specifically right AFTER S2 (early diastole)

  • Occurs in DIASTOLE — specifically right BEFORE S1 (late diastole)

Autonomic Regulation (Prof: "30,000 ft view — very simplified")

Chemoreceptors and pressure receptors in the aortic arch and carotid bodies monitor BP and HR. Low BP/HR → sympathetic kicks in (↑HR, ↑BP). Too high → parasympathetic kicks in (↓HR, ↓BP).

Key Definitions

TermDefinitionProf's Clinical Notes
Cardiac Output (CO)CO = Stroke Volume × Heart Rate"Important when we talk about nitrous oxide, especially with our pediatric population." Children are more HR-dependent.
PreloadStretch of cardiac muscle prior to contraction"There's so much you can increase the contraction by filling it, but there's a point where you're gonna fill it too much and it's not gonna work as well — that's where you have a cardiomyopathy."
AfterloadVascular resistance against which the cardiac muscle must contract"Somebody with a high blood pressure, the heart has to beat up against those vessels." High BP → increased afterload.

Blood Pressure — Full Technique (Prof's Step-by-Step)

  • Put cuff on, palpate the brachial artery
  • Put stethoscope in ears — earpieces going FORWARD (callback to Thorax stethoscope teaching)
  • Inflate cuff until you no longer feel the pulse, then pump up another 20 mmHg
  • Slowly release air and listen
  • First sound you hear = SYSTOLIC (Korotkoff sounds — turbulent flow through compressed artery)
  • Keep listening → sound disappears = DIASTOLIC (free flow, no turbulence)
  • "Don't stop listening after that. Keep listening for another 10 to 20 millimeters" just to check for an auscultatory gap (sound temporarily disappears then comes back — you don't want to call diastolic too early)

Why you hear the sounds (prof explained the physics): Fully occluded = silence (no flow). Partially compressed = turbulent flow = Korotkoff sounds (systolic). No compression = laminar flow = silence (diastolic).

Common mistake (from slide): Cuff or bladder too small → falsely elevated BP. Wrist cuffs: must follow positioning instructions exactly — often give falsely high readings.

Elective procedure cutoff: BP < 160/100. "I'd keep it below that for elective procedures." Emergency can push a little higher. "But that's your cutoff number."
OBJ B7

Heart Murmurs — Location, Cardiac Cycle & THE VALVE LOGIC METHOD

"I guarantee you you're getting a question like, is this a systolic murmur..." — He CONFIRMED a murmur MCQ will be on the exam.

Heart murmur = abnormal sound caused by turbulent blood flow.

6 Mechanisms of Murmurs (from slide — prof went through each)

  • 1. Flow across a partial obstruction (e.g., stenotic valve — not opening fully)
  • 2. Flow across a valvular irregularity without obstruction (e.g., endocarditis vegetations)
  • 3. Increased flow through normal structures (e.g., pregnancy — "anemia of pregnancy, high flow murmur" = physiologic/innocent)
  • 4. Flow into a dilated chamber (e.g., cardiomyopathy with dilated ventricles)
  • 5. Backward/regurgitant flow across an incompetent valve — "a leaky valve"
  • 6. Shunting (e.g., dialysis shunt — "almost like a bruit that you'll hear")

Two categories: Physiologic (innocent) vs. Non-physiologic (structural problem).

Why dentists need to know: 1) Antibiotic prophylaxis decisions. 2) Sedation concerns — "especially if they have an aortic stenosis, if you drop their blood pressure, is that going to be a problem."

3 Things to Identify (from slide — starred = most important)

  • Location* — which valve area are you hearing it?
  • Systolic vs. Diastolic* — when in the cardiac cycle?
  • Radiation — does it spread to neck, axilla, etc.?

THE RULE — How to Solve ANY Murmur Question

1. Location tells you the valve (e.g., R 2nd ICS = Aortic)

2. Timing tells you systole or diastole (between S1-S2 = systole; between S2-S1 = diastole)

3. Check the table: Is that valve supposed to be OPEN or CLOSED at that time?

4. Valve supposed to be OPEN + murmur = STENOSIS (not opening fully → obstructing flow)

5. Valve supposed to be CLOSED + murmur = REGURGITATION (not closing fully → leaking backward)

Prof's worked example: "Murmur in the right second inner space, during systole." → R 2nd ICS = Aortic. During systole, aortic is OPEN. Murmur while open = not opening fully = Aortic Stenosis.

Historical Context (Prof's Timeline)

  • Before early 2000s: High-risk patients received IV antibiotics for prophylaxis, not PO.
  • Early 2000s: Changed to PO antibiotics.
  • 2007 AHA Guidelines (THE BIG CHANGE): Went from prophylaxing moderate + high risk → ONLY high risk patients.

Objective 2

Differences Between Minimal, Moderate, Deep Sedation & General Anesthesia

PROF EMPHASIZED Among the most heavily tested content. Prof said "I want to outline where the importance comes in" — focus on the distinguishing features between each level.

Sedation Level Definitions (from slides — know these word-for-word qualifiers)

Analgesia: Diminution or elimination of pain.

Local Anesthesia: Elimination of sensation/pain in one part of the body by topical application or regional injection.

Minimal Sedation: A minimally depressed level of consciousness, produced by a pharmacological method, that retains the patient's ability to independently and continuously maintain an airway and respond normally to tactile stimulation and verbal command. Cognitive function and coordination may be modestly impaired. Ventilatory and cardiovascular functions are UNAFFECTED. Prof adds: "This patient is probably pretty wide awake."

Moderate Sedation: Drug-induced depression of consciousness during which patients respond purposefully to verbal commands, either alone or accompanied by light tactile stimulation. No interventions are required to maintain a patent airway, and spontaneous ventilation is adequate. Cardiovascular function is usually maintained.

Deep Sedation: Drug-induced depression of consciousness during which patients cannot be easily aroused but respond purposefully following repeated or painful stimulation. Ability to independently maintain ventilatory function may be impaired. May require assistance maintaining airway. Spontaneous ventilation may be inadequate. Cardiovascular function is usually maintained. Prof: "You can arouse them, and sometimes it takes a painful stimulus to do it. You may have to breathe for them."

General Anesthesia: Drug-induced loss of consciousness during which patients are NOT arousable, even by painful stimulation. Ventilatory function is often impaired. Often require assistance maintaining airway. Positive pressure ventilation may be required. Cardiovascular function may be impaired. Prof: "You cannot wake them up when they're under general anesthesia. Eventually you will when it wears away."

Geriatric Patients — "Go Low, Go Slow" (Prof spent significant time)

Pharmacokinetics = "What the body does WITH the drug" — uptake, distribution, metabolism, excretion.

Pharmacodynamics = "What the drug does TO the body."

↓ Cardiac output → drug takes longer to circulate from injection site to brain.

Prof's mnemonic: "Go LOW" (use lower starting dose, e.g., 1 mg instead of 2 mg) + "Go SLOW" (wait longer between titrations because decreased CO = longer circulation time).

Objective 4

Exchange of N₂O in Alveoli → Circulatory System

PROF EMPHASIZED Built carefully using gas exchange principles from the pulmonary lecture.

Fundamental principle: Gases diffuse from high pressure → low pressure across the alveolar membrane.

Normal gas exchange reference (partial pressures in mmHg):

GasAlveolusArterial BloodVenous Blood
O₂10310040
CO₂404046

O₂ moves from alveolus (103) → venous blood (40) because of the pressure gradient. CO₂ moves from venous blood (46) → alveolus (40). Same principle applies to N₂O.

N₂O Uptake (start of case): N₂O inhaled → alveolus has HIGH N₂O partial pressure → blood has ZERO N₂O → massive pressure gradient → N₂O rushes rapidly from alveolus → blood → brain. As blood saturates, diffusion rate slows → equilibrium → steady state.

N₂O Elimination (end of case): Switch to 100% O₂ → alveolus now has ZERO N₂O → blood is RICH in N₂O → gradient reverses → N₂O rushes from blood → alveolus → exhaled out of body. This is where diffusion hypoxia can occur (N₂O flooding into alveolus dilutes the O₂).

Pharmacology numbers: Blood-gas solubility coefficient = 0.47 at 37°C. Only ~0.06% metabolized in the body — rest goes in and comes out unchanged through the lungs. Not metabolized by the liver. Small quantities needed to reach required blood concentrations. This is why it's "quick on, quick off."

Solubility and speed (prof explained with 3 graphs):

  • Totally insoluble gas: Never enters blood, no matter the pressure — useless.
  • Relatively insoluble (= N₂O): Enters blood, reaches equilibrium quickly → fast onset + fast recovery. "It's not gonna take long to get to that point. Once it gets there, it can go off into the brain to work."
  • Highly soluble gas: Enters blood but takes a very long time to build up effective concentration — slow onset.

N₂O's relative insolubility = its greatest pharmacologic advantage.

Objective 7

Concentration Effect & Secondary Gas Effect

QUICK / FLEW THROUGH concentration effect. MODERATE TIME second gas effect with a great analogy.

Concentration Effect ("Vacuum-Type Effect"): Higher concentration of gas inhaled → more rapid increase in arterial tension. Only significant at >70%. "You really don't see this with nitrous oxide as much till you get over that 70% mark, and we can't give that in the office." → Not clinically relevant for dental N₂O. Know the definition but understand it doesn't apply.

Second Gas Effect: When other inhalation agents are combined with N₂O-O₂ during GA, N₂O's rapid uptake creates a "vacuum" that pulls the second gas (volatile anesthetic) along → increased concentration of second gas → you can use lower concentrations of the more potent/dangerous agent.

Think of a puddle of water outside — the water is the volatile agent. Think of the wind as your nitrous oxide. That puddle will evaporate at 5–10 mph but slowly. Bring the wind in at 30–40 mph — that puddle goes up faster. Higher N₂O concentration = more wind = pulls the second gas quicker.

Only relevant during general anesthesia, not dental N₂O alone. Advantage: minimizes required concentration of other agent → safer.

Objective 8

Diffusion Hypoxia & How to Prevent It

PROF EMPHASIZED Key objective — full mechanism and clinical management.

🚨🚨 Diffusion Hypoxia — Full Mechanism

When: Occurs at termination of sedation (when you turn off N₂O).

Mechanism: You turn off N₂O → switch to 100% O₂ → alveolus now has little/no N₂O → but blood is still RICH in N₂O → massive pressure gradient reversal → N₂O rushes from blood into alveolus → this flood of N₂O dilutes the O₂ in the alveolus → patient doesn't get enough O₂ → hypoxia.

Prevention: 100% O₂ for 3–5 minutes post-procedure. This flushes N₂O out while maintaining adequate oxygenation.

"Hangover" symptoms: Headache, Nausea, Dizziness, Lethargy

I always tell the patient, you should walk out of here the same way you walked in, except for the procedure we did. If they still have headache, nausea, or are dizzy — what am I gonna do? Got to get more oxygen. Eventually they'll turn around. We've had a couple of patients that's taken like 10–15 minutes, but that's about it. Most people within 5 minutes have recovered.

Important caveat: Nasal hood is an open system — patient also breathes room air around the mask edges. "Even though it's 100% oxygen coming there, the patient's really not getting 100% oxygen." But it's enough to prevent diffusion hypoxia.

If hangover symptoms persist: Keep giving O₂. Don't discharge until symptoms resolve. Most = 5 min, some = 10–15 min max.

Equipment, Central vs. Portable, Safety Features & Scavenging

PROF EMPHASIZED equipment safety — guarantees exam questions. Most time on flowmeter safety, reservoir bag, and scavenging.

Central Gas Supply System — Prof spent significant time

Large G/H tanks housed in a separate tank room. Tanks must be secured to walls with restrainers — "If that falls over and you knock off that stem, all that pressure — that's a projectile now, it can go through cinder block walls." At 2000 psi, a loose O₂ tank is a missile.

Pressure regulation chain: Tank (2000 psi) → Regulator on top of tank (brings down to ~50 psi or below) → Manifold (brings down further to safe breathing pressure). "So when you're breathing, it's not gonna blow the patient's face off."

Manifold: Usually connects 2 tanks — when one empties, the other automatically kicks in. Continuous supply.

Plumbing: Copper piping throughout the office. Soldered with silver solder. Different size copper tubing for each gas (safety feature — can't mix up). Must be installed by certified professional (not a regular plumber). Tested with nitrogen (not water) for leaks. Requires fire department sign-off + special permitting. Your dental supply rep has a list of qualified installers.

At the flowmeter level, the central system is the same as the portable system. BU has an Accutron system that's a portable-central hybrid — plugs into central system but can move room to room. Peds department has a standalone portable Accutron.

PROF EMPHASIZED Accutron Safety — Must turn O₂ on FIRST:

When I turn on my gas, I have to turn the oxygen on first. It automatically goes up to 3 L. If you look on the nitrous oxide side, the most you can give is 7 L. So it's automatically putting in a fail-safe to put that 3 L per minute. If you turned up the nitrous oxide as far as you could, you can't give anything less than 30% of oxygen.

Math: 3 L O₂ / (3 L O₂ + 7 L N₂O) = 3/10 = 30% O₂ minimum guaranteed.

O₂ Fail-Safe Shut-Off: "If for some reason you start turning off the oxygen, the oxygen goes off — the nitrous oxide does not flow to the patient because the valve shuts off." Patient NEVER gets N₂O without O₂.

Room Air Override: "If there's no flow going to the patient, no oxygen, no combination — the patient doesn't feel like they're suffocating. There's a room air override, so the patient is getting air." Safety feature preventing suffocation if system fails.

Pediatric limitation of Accutron: "If I wanna run 50% N₂O on a pediatric patient, I'm giving 3 L N₂O, 3 L O₂. That's 6 L per minute — that may be too much for a small pediatric patient." Fixed 3 L O₂ minimum means can't get below 6 L/min total at 50% N₂O.

Manual flowmeter: "If you're trying to keep the same liters per minute, you're gonna be changing the nitrous oxide and the oxygen continuously going back and forth." More work.

Digital system: "I want 6 L per minute and I want 50% — just dial in the percentage, it calculates: 3 L N₂O, 3 L O₂. You don't have to think about it." Much easier.

Scavenging System — Prof spent significant time

How it works: Patient breathes in → valve closes off suction → full gas delivery. Patient breathes out → valve opens → exhaled N₂O is suctioned away into central vacuum → vented outside.

Required suction rate: 45 L/min — most systems have a green zone indicator on the hose. Check throughout the case.

4-hose system (traditional/bag system): 2 large bottom hoses = delivery, 2 upper hoses = suction. Bulky, hard to work in anterior region, goes around head.

2-hose system (low-profile — what BU uses): 1 delivery + 1 continuous suction. Simpler, goes around ears (less bulky), thin tubing that's easy to autoclave. BU switched before COVID — turned out to be a benefit. Continuous suction means you need adequate flow to compensate for gas being pulled away.

Hopefully it's not in front of your air conditioning unit that's putting it back in, because you wanna make sure of that when you set these up.

Safety Features Article

1. "When Safety Features No Longer Are Safe"

Donaldson M, Donaldson D, Quarnstrom F. JADA 2012;143:134–143
⏱ Prof spent moderate time on this — opened the recording with it, used it as a bridge into the Technique lecture.

Background & Framing

N₂O–O₂ delivery systems are limited to a maximum of 70% N₂O / 30% O₂, ensuring the patient receives at least 9% more oxygen than ambient air (21%). The professor reinforces: "Our machines do not let us go below 30%, unless there's some problem with our machines."

The most important safety consideration is the prevention of hypoxia.

What the Article Found

The authors reviewed 12 safety features for N₂O–O₂ sedation. Despite these, dentists have still reported hypoxia incidents from incorrect equipment installation or equipment damage:

  • Incorrect installation → primarily a risk with centralized systems (gas lines plumbed through building). Could theoretically happen with portable if manufactured incorrectly, but rare.
  • Equipment damage → machine used for years develops problems over time.

The Crossed Gas Lines Scenario

🎯 High-Yield Exam Scenario
"If I put on 100% oxygen and this patient seems like they're getting sedated and overly sedated, then maybe my oxygen and nitrous oxide was put in backwards."

Crossed gas lines: If you dial up "oxygen" and the patient becomes MORE sedated, the lines are reversed. This applies to:

  • New installations — first time using a newly installed centralized system
  • Old machines with damage — been using it for years, suddenly patient on "100% O₂" is getting deeper

What To Do If Safety Feature Failure Suspected

Prof's Expanded Clinical Protocol

Slide says: "Remove the face mask from the patient immediately." Prof expands:

  1. Remove the nasal hood / face mask immediately
  2. Use your portable emergency oxygen tank — put patient on face mask or nasal cannula from that separate, known-good tank
  3. Do NOT just let them breathe room air — risk of diffusion hypoxia (see below)
🎯 Exam Alert — Diffusion Hypoxia

Prof asked the class: "What's your concern if you just take off the nasal hood and let them breathe room air?"

Answer: Diffusion hypoxia. N₂O dissolved in blood floods back into alveoli rapidly, diluting alveolar oxygen and causing transient hypoxia. It could resolve over time, but you have the emergency O₂ — use it.

This is why recovery protocol calls for 5 minutes of 100% O₂ after any N₂O administration.

2. Daily Equipment Check & Transition to Technique

Slides: "N₂O-O₂ Analgesia Technique — Preoperative Considerations"

Professor's Personal Daily Pre-Sedation Routine

Prof's Protocol — Every Single Day
  1. Turn on evacuation/suction → confirm scavenger working (>45 L/min)
  2. Turn on oxygen → cover nasal hood opening with hand → reservoir bag should expand (confirms no leaks). Check connections with soapy water bubbles.
  3. Turn on N₂O briefly (~2 L/min) → confirm it's flowing

5. Signs & Symptoms: Appropriate Sedation vs. Oversedation

Slides: "Signs & Symptoms of N₂O-O₂ Sedation" (multiple) / "Box 12-1" and "Box 12-2"

Signs of Appropriate Sedation

Behavioral: Relaxed, comfortable. Shoulders dropping, arms looser. Deeper respirations, deep sigh. Responds to directions (open mouth, turn head). Conscious and aware of surroundings. Cough and gag reflexes remain intact.

Eyes & Face: Less active, "glazed" or "glassy" eyes. "Flat" expression, loss of forehead wrinkling if they came in nervous. Smiles easily.

Patient May Also Experience: Tingling in extremities/around mouth. Heaviness OR lightness of arms/legs. Comfortable body warmth. Vasodilation of face/neck → redness or perspiration. Circumoral numbness.

🎯 Communication Technique — Use Suggestive Language
"Do NOT say 'you should be feeling these things.' If you tell them they're supposed to have these things, then they may think it's not working."

Correct (suggestive): "You may feel some tingling... your arms may feel heavy... these are all common with nitrous oxide."

Wrong (directive): "You should be feeling tingling now. Your arms should feel heavy."

Clinical Pearl — Exaggerated Responses

"You may tell them a bad joke and they'll smile, but if they start laughing outrageously, you may have them too deep — come back off your nitrous oxide."

Disproportionate/exaggerated responses = sign of oversedation. The patient should respond appropriately, not exaggeratedly.

Clinical Pearl — White Knuckle Syndrome
"It is NOT the reaction of the nitrous oxide on the muscles. It is the reaction of the anxiety on the patient."

N₂O doesn't directly relax skeletal muscles — patient lets go because anxiety decreased, not direct muscle relaxation.

Signs of Oversedation

🎯 Know These — The "Bad Things"
  • Detachment / dissociation from environment
  • Hallucinating, dreaming, fantasizing
  • Out-of-body experiences, floating, flying
  • Inability to move or communicate — this is a red flag (they should always be able to respond)
  • Humming that gets progressively louder — professor explains: they start humming because things feel uncomfortable, and it escalates as they get deeper
  • Drowsiness, dizziness
  • Diaphoresis (sweating)
  • Nausea, lightheadedness
  • Uncomfortable body warmth (contrast with comfortable warmth of appropriate sedation)
  • Fixed eyes (contrast with glazed eyes)
  • Sluggish/delayed responses, slurred words
  • Agitated or combative behavior
  • Vomiting → may progress to unconsciousness
Critical Teaching Point — Deeper ≠ Better
"You may put them deeper and think, well, maybe that's a good thing. No, it's not good — they're NOT gonna be comfortable and they're gonna be MORE anxious in the end."

9. Recovery, Discharge & Reversal Agents

Slides: "Minimal Sedation Recovery and Discharge" / "Moderate Sedation Recovery and Discharge"

Recovery Protocol

Prof's Clinical Pearl on Recovery Timing
"Most of the time, the patient is going to be recovered by the time you are finished your procedure."

His practical technique tip: Turn off the N₂O when you think you have 5 minutes left in your procedure. Switch to 100% O₂ at that point. By the time you finish the procedure, 5 minutes will have passed and the patient should be recovered.

If NOT recovered after 5 minutes → keep giving oxygen until they recover.

Recovery checklist:

  • 100% O₂ for minimum 5 minutes at the same flow rate the patient was on
  • Verify NO signs of: lethargy, dizziness, lightheadedness, or headache
  • If any of those present → suspect diffusion hypoxia → keep on O₂ longer
  • Obtain post-operative vital signs
  • Document that level of consciousness, oxygenation, ventilation, and circulation are satisfactory prior to discharge
  • Give post-operative verbal and written instructions to patient/parent/escort/guardian

Minimal vs. Moderate Discharge Differences

Minimal sedation (N₂O alone): Patient should return to baseline before discharge. Recovery room O₂/suction should be available if separate area used. Dentist or trained staff monitors during recovery.

Moderate sedation: O₂ and suction must be available. Staff continually monitors BP, heart rate, oxygenation, level of consciousness. Particularly important for IV sedation / deep sedation / GA when moving patients to recovery rooms. Person monitoring must be trained. Dentist makes discharge decision and does all documentation.

"You may shoot for minimum, you may put them in moderate, you may try to get into general anesthetic, but you never get out of moderate sedation, so it's always that issue."

11. Complete N₂O Technique Sequence & Patient Communication

Full Technique Sequence — Know Every Step

  1. Assemble and assess armamentarium; open tanks and turn on flowmeter — Check scavenger (45 L/min), check for leaks (soapy water), ensure ventilation vents to outside
  2. Confirm scavenging devices functioning
  3. Health history reviewed; vital signs recorded
  4. Informed consent obtained
  5. Activate O₂ flow; fill reservoir bag approximately ⅔ full by use of O₂ flush valve
  6. Secure nasal hood — snug around nose; select appropriate size
  7. Establish appropriate tidal volume in L/min by observing reservoir bag
  8. Begin N₂O titration
  9. Assess patient for signs and symptoms of appropriate sedation
  10. Titrate N₂O to desired level
  11. Perform operative procedures
  12. Terminate N₂O flow; administer 5 minutes postoperative 100% O₂

  14. Continue postoperative oxygenation if necessary

Patient Communication — Nose Breathing

Prof Spent Meaningful Time on This

"In the nose, in the nose, out the nose. Continue to work on breathing through their nose."

If the patient starts talking: "Tell them, 'Hey, if you talk, you're not gonna get the full effect of the nitrous oxide.' That'll get them to stop talking."

The flip side: "Don't ask them 50 questions then. Keep your questions to a minimum." You can't coach them to nose-breathe while simultaneously prompting them to talk.

Constant vs. Variable O₂ Technique

Constant O₂ Level Technique

O₂ flow stays fixed (e.g., 6 L/min). You add N₂O on top. Simpler. But total flow to patient increases as you add N₂O. If you go above 50% N₂O, flow rate goes up noticeably. Patients can usually tolerate it. Prof says: "Good flow is 8, but you can cheat a little and they can tolerate 10 L/min — makes calculations easier."

Variable O₂ Level Technique

As you turn N₂O up, you turn O₂ down — keeping total flow constant while changing the percentage. More complex — requires constantly adjusting both dials. With flow meters, you're moving knobs continuously.

Prof's Practical Tip

"Some flow meters will do it for you. If you get the digital ones where you just say 'I wanna start off 15%, go to 20%, 30%' — so much easier, it will calculate that for you."

19. Silent Regurgitation & Aspiration

Slide: "Nitrous Oxide — Silent regurgitation and subsequent aspiration need to be considered"
🎯 Safety-Critical for Pediatric Patients

The slide states the concern is with the pharyngeal-laryngeal reflexes remaining intact.

"This is where I'm pretty tight with the guidelines for fasting because it's easy to get them into a level of unconsciousness and these patients could have silent regurgitation — they could regurgitate and they can aspirate because you could lose the pharyngeal laryngeal reflexes and you want them to remain intact."

20. Objectives, Indications & Disadvantages of Pediatric N₂O

Slides: "Objectives of N₂O–O₂ Sedation" / "Indications" / "Disadvantages"

Decision to Use — Multifactorial

Must be discussed with the parent: why are we doing this? Is there an alternative? Generally accepted by most children — some report dreaming or "being on a space ride." But some feel they're losing control (adults too) → N₂O won't work. If claustrophobic due to nasal hood → also problematic.

Objectives of Pediatric N₂O Sedation

  • Reduce/eliminate anxiety
  • Reduce untoward movement and reaction to dental treatment
  • Enhance communication and patient cooperation — prof emphasizes: "You need to talk with your patient, especially the pediatric patients, because you can walk them through procedures." He gives an anecdote about a resident who worked too fast — prof had to step in and show how communication WITH N₂O made an anxious extraction successful.
  • Raise the pain reaction threshold
  • Increase tolerance for longer appointments — BUT with a critical caution:
🎯 Cross-Topic Connection — Local Anesthetic Max Dose
"A lengthy dental procedure — give the nitrous oxide, that's fine, that's probably not gonna be your limiting factor. But if you're gonna start doing a bunch of quadrant dentistry and you're gonna use a lot of local anesthetic, just be careful about what size patient you have, and that you do not exceed the maximum dose of your local anesthetic."

  • Aid treatment of mentally/physically disabled or medically compromised patients
  • Reduce gagging
  • Potentiate effect of sedatives — professor warns that pediatric patients may be using illicit or diverted medications without parents' knowledge (foreshadows opioid epidemic discussion)

Indications for Pediatric N₂O

  • Fearful, anxious patient — but NOT phobic ("somebody that has a phobia, it's not really gonna work")

  • Gag reflex interfering with dental care
  • Patient for whom profound local anesthesia cannot be obtained (e.g., abscessed tooth — "It may help, may not carry you through. Patient may need something more like a deep sedation.")
  • Cooperative patient undergoing lengthy procedures

Disadvantages

  • Lack of potency — can't put patients to sleep. But professor reframes: "I think it's also a safety factor. It prevents us from getting them very deep, so it keeps it safe for us." The low potency is both a disadvantage AND a built-in safety feature.
  • Dependent on psychological reassurance — "If you don't believe in it, why should your patients believe in it?" But also: "There are times when you say this is not gonna work and you know it's not gonna work because this patient needs deeper sedation."
  • Nasal hood interference with anterior maxillary region injections — low-profile helps but still some challenge
  • Patient must breathe through nose — won't work with obstruction, URI, deviated septum
  • Trace gas exposure — patient leaves, but you and your staff stay. If you can't control trace levels, that's an occupational health hazard. (Not a patient concern — a staff concern. Connects to biohazards lecture.)

ADHD / Intellectual Disability / CP / Autism

🎯 Highly Testable — ASD & N₂O Controversy

Why is N₂O controversial in ASD?

Some individuals with ASD have abnormalities of folic acid, vitamin B12, and MTHFR deficiency. Since N₂O inactivates vitamin B12 (from earlier pharmacology lectures), giving N₂O to a patient who already has B12 deficiency or MTHFR deficiency could worsen neurologic problems.

Bottom line: Rule out B12/MTHFR issues first. If cleared → N₂O can be used. Inform parents of risk regardless. The controversy is about the B12/folic acid/MTHFR connection, NOT about behavioral issues.

Asphyxiation — Two Key Scenarios

Scenario 1: Bag over head method

Scenario 2: Modified dental/medical equipment

"In medical practices or dental practices, where if you modify your nitrous oxide machine where you can give yourself 100% nitrous oxide, and you develop a mask that goes right around your face — your oxygen runs out, and you're getting 100% nitrous oxide and getting no oxygen."
🎯 Critical Concept — N₂O ≠ Usable Oxygen
"Even though it's nitrous oxide, that oxygen is not available for cellular use."

N₂O contains an oxygen atom (N₂O), but the body cannot use it for cellular respiration. 100% N₂O = 0% usable oxygen = asphyxiation. This is a fundamental concept.

The "Second Person in the Room" Rule

🎯 Major Medicolegal Point
"If you're going to use nitrous oxide and you're going to go with a high concentration, over 50%, always have a second person in the room with you."

Why? N₂O can cause:

  • Sexual phenomena / hallucinations
  • Auditory illusions
  • Dreaming and fantasizing
"They've been brought up in front of boards — health professionals — even though nothing was done, but because of the hallucination, auditory illusions that they have, they're dreaming and fantasizing, they think something had happened."

The second person serves as a third-party witness to protect both patient and provider. At concentrations >50%, this is essential medicolegal protection.

EXAM NIOSH Standards — The Numbers and WHY They Differ
TIME Prof spent significant time explaining WHY the numbers differ

1977 — NIOSH (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) set exposure standards:

Operating Room = 25 ppm

Easier to achieve because:

  • Patient has an endotracheal tube — it's a closed system. Patient breathes in and out through the tube with minimal trace gas leaking into the room.
  • ORs have air exchangers that turn over the room air regularly.

Dental Setting = 50 ppm

Harder to achieve because:

  • Patient breathes through a nasal hood — an open system (mouth is open, patient talks, mask can be displaced).
  • Dental offices typically don't have the same caliber of air exchange systems as ORs.
🔴 CRITICAL DISTINCTION

The 50 ppm dental standard is NOT enforced by OSHA. It is a recommendation, not a regulation. The goal in the dental office is simply to minimize trace gases as much as possible.

BEFORE you insert (preparation is everything):

Insertion technique step by step:

  2. Enter at approximately 30° angle to the skin (slide shows 30°)
  3. Advance until you see flashback (blood appearing in the catheter hub)
🔴 KEY STEP — "THIS IS WHERE PEOPLE MAKE MISTAKES"

Students see flashback and immediately try to advance the catheter. BUT the needle is beveled, so you may only be partway into the vein, not fully.

Prof's technique: Once you see flashback, go another 1–2 millimeters in the direction of the vein — NOT going deeper, just going straight ahead. THEN slide the catheter forward off the needle.

If you don't do this, you can get flashback but can't advance the catheter. Have to start over.

After catheter is in place:

  1. Put pressure at the proximal end of the catheter (further up on hand/arm) to prevent blood from flowing out
  2. Connect the fluids immediately
  3. Secure the catheter so you don't lose your IV

Dorsal hand V-shaped vein: Where two veins form a V — "go right between that, it's the best place because it's the widest area, plus it doesn't roll." Bend the hand over, make everything taut, take your thumb and pull skin taut for easier penetration.

KEY Historical Foundations & Definition
TIME William Harvey — moderate pace. Jorgensen — briefly.

William Harvey: Demonstrated continuous circulation of blood within a closed system. Showed that valves in heart and veins → unidirectional blood flow. This is the fundamental principle that makes IV medication delivery possible. Before Harvey, people thought blood left the body (open system).

Neils Bjorn Jorgensen (1945): Probably first person to use IV route for premedication. "Jorgensen's technique" / "Loma Linda technique":

  • Pentobarbital (barbiturate)
  • Meperidine/Demerol (opioid)
  • Scopolamine (anticholinergic)
TIME IV Sedation Definition — moderate, connected to N₂O

Slide: Administration of a drug → depression of the CNS. Goal: produce a state of sedation in which patient maintains protective reflexes, respiratory and cardiovascular systems minimally affected.

"You're gonna look at this definition and say we saw this before." — Same goal as N₂O sedation, just different route. Both aim for CNS depression while maintaining protective reflexes. Moderate sedation benchmark: "that patient should be able to talk to you and just feeling really, really comfortable."
EXAM IV Sedation Advantages — ALL 7 with Full Prof Commentary
TIME Prof spent SIGNIFICANT time — especially on titration and N/V prevention

1. Rapid onset: Arm-to-brain circulation = 20–25 seconds. You know what's happening pretty quickly.

2. Titration — Prof's most emphasized advantage: Not everyone responds the same to the same dose (bell-shaped curve). You have hyper-responders (small amount puts them way out) and hypo-responders (take a ton and barely move). Titration lets you give small amounts at a time and work up. Benefits: only give the minimum amount needed → faster recovery, fewer side effects. This is the fundamental advantage of IV over IM (where you can't titrate).

3. Continuous IV infusion — serves 3 purposes:

  • Keep a patent vein for more medication if needed
  • Give fluids to a patient who's been NPO for 8-12 hours
  • Usually D5W or sugar water to help with metabolism since they haven't eaten

4. N/V rare when administered correctly. Mostly from opioids. Prof's screening question: "Do you get car sick?" — or sick on amusement park rides. If yes → give prophylactic antiemetic IV before the procedure.

5. Salivation control: IV anticholinergics.

6. Gag reflex diminished. Prof makes an important clinical hierarchy: for simple gag issues (like impressions) → try N₂O first (5 min recovery, walk out). For actual surgical procedures where gag is a barrier → "IV sedation works a lot better than nitrous oxide." IV sedation has longer recovery.

7. Diminish motor disturbances / seizure activity. BUT some IV medications can also lower the seizure threshold — he'll address specific drugs later.

  • Ventilatory function often impaired → may need to maintain airway AND breathe for patient

7Pharmacology — Barbiturates
EXAM Barbiturates — Full Overview, Contraindications, Pain Threshold
TIME Prof covered at moderate pace with personal clinical experience

Historical: "When I was going through my training, this was a big thing over propofol." Less common now but still testable.

Mechanism: Generalized CNS depressants — cerebral cortex, limbic system, reticular activating system (sleep/wakefulness).

Spectrum: Light sedation → hypnosis → GA → coma → death. Classified: ultra-short, short, long acting.

Key pharmacologic facts:

  • No effect on pain threshold until loss of consciousness — then pain threshold DROPS. Patients become MORE reactive to pain under barbiturate sedation. Even pressure can make them move around.
  • Have anticonvulsant properties (raise seizure threshold — with ONE exception, see below)
  • Eliminated by biotransformation in liver, excreted through kidneys. If liver problems → can't metabolize properly → use less, be very careful.
  • NO REVERSAL AGENT EXISTS for barbiturates (unlike benzos and opioids)
  • Hangover effect — drug distributes into fat and slowly "leaches out" → slow recovery
  • Typical procedures: 15–45 minutes
"I would highly recommend propofol over the barbiturates." — Modern clinical preference. Propofol has replaced barbiturates.
Contraindications
  1. Allergy
  2. Uncontrolled pain — pain threshold DROPS under sedation; patients become hyperreactive
  3. Addiction to sedative-hypnotics
  4. Acute intermittent porphyria — classic board exam fact
  5. Respiratory disease with dyspnea or obstruction — barbiturates are respiratory depressants
KEY Pentobarbital — Drug of Choice for Elevated ICP

Classification: Short-acting barbiturate. Used for IV induction and seizure control.

Effects/Side effects:

  1. Reduces cerebral metabolism
  2. Modest decrease in blood pressure — if patient already has low BP, avoid
  3. Temporary respiratory depression
  4. Hangover (fat redistribution effect)

Advantages: Dose-dependent amnesia, dose-dependent drowsiness, hypnosis, DRUG OF CHOICE for elevated intracranial pressure (ICP).

Prof's clinical context: In the dental office, you probably won't treat elevated ICP patients. But if a neurosurgery patient needs intubation for respiratory reasons and has elevated ICP, barbiturates are the drug of choice for that induction. Prof repeated this across two class sessions = testable.

Disadvantages: Respiratory depression, decreased SVR → hypotension if volume depleted, no analgesia, highly addictive with prolonged use, cardiovascular depression (vasodilation), myoclonus, painful injection.

EXAM🔴 Methohexital (Brevital) — THE Big Seizure Threshold Distinction
🔴 CRITICAL DISTINCTION — CLASSIC BOARD QUESTION

All barbiturates RAISE the seizure threshold (anticonvulsant) EXCEPT:

METHOHEXITAL (BREVITAL) — LOWERS SEIZURE THRESHOLD

This means methohexital can INDUCE SEIZURES in patients with seizure history. It is the opposite of every other barbiturate.

Additional facts: Methohexital's duration parallels propofol. Propofol has essentially replaced methohexital in modern practice. Both reliably produce hypotension. Clinically useful IV doses cause at least some respiratory depression.

Barbiturates = drugs of choice for procedural sedation and airway management with high ICP.

EXAM🔴 Fentanyl — Chest Wall Rigidity (IV-Specific Side Effect)

🔴 FENTANYL CHEST WALL RIGIDITY

Problem: Rapid IV infusion → skeletal muscle and chest wall rigidity → patient CANNOT ventilate → hypoxia, respiratory distress, apnea, bronchoconstriction, laryngospasm.

Prevention: Don't give high doses. Inject slowly over 3–5 minutes.

Treatment:

⚠ Naloxone will NOT fix the chest wall rigidity!

💨 Inhalation Anesthetics — N₂O in the GA Context

Slide: Inhalation anesthetics are the most frequently used means of producing GA. Agents: N₂O, Halothane, Enflurane, Isoflurane, Desflurane, Sevoflurane.

Prof immediately pivoted to testing the class on N₂O.

Prof asked the class directly: “Is nitrous oxide a good general anesthetic?” Answer: NO.
• N₂O alone = only minimal sedation. Can get moderate sedation if >50%.
• A hyper-responder could drift to deep sedation — must rescue them back.
• N₂O MAC = 104–105% — physically impossible to deliver enough (can’t give >100%). It is a weak general anesthetic.
N₂O’s role in GA = Second Gas Effect / Potentiation. Running high-concentration N₂O alongside another volatile agent creates rapid uptake that concentrates the second gas in alveoli → you need less of the potent agent. Prof used the analogy of wind blowing over a puddle of water. The slide says: “Primary function in GA is to potentiate the actions of the other more potent drugs and thus permits a smaller dose or lesser concentration.”

If asked “What is the role of N₂O in GA?” → potentiation / second gas effect, NOT that it produces GA on its own.
📐 Understanding MAC (Minimum Alveolar Concentration)
Prof re-explained MAC with a vivid example: “If I had 50 people here and the MAC was 0.75, I gave everybody 0.75 MAC, and I did a standard surgical incision — half would move, half wouldn’t move — that’s without a local anesthetic.”

MAC = concentration at which 50% of patients won’t respond to a surgical stimulus.
CRITICAL RULE: Lower MAC = More Potent. MAC 0.75% = strong anesthetic. MAC 6% = weak (need much more drug).

LECTURE K: Management of Emergencies During Sedation

Dr. Manish K. Bhagania BDS, MDS — Clinical Associate Professor, OMS

These are emergencies specific to sedation, different from general dental office emergencies.

⚠ Introduction — ADRs

Dental office must identify, document, and manage ADRs + contact PCP. Most are NOT life-threatening — but some are.

3 reasons ADRs occur:
  1. Dentist error — but statistically LEAST likely cause. Prof: “Which is what is trying to be proven in the courts.” Due diligence = your defense.
  2. Random events — unpredictable even when everything done right.
  3. Reasonable risk of treatment — e.g., syncope during injection. If patient history was not seen/recorded/evaluated properly → that patient will have the adverse event. Thorough history + documentation = best protection.
💊 OVERDOSE (Prof Spent Significant Time)
Core principle: Overdose = exaggeration of normal pharmacologic effects. “If it’s a sedative and it’s an overdose, what does it cause? More sleep.”
Barbiturates = greatest POTENTIAL for adverse reaction (narrow therapeutic index — recall the S-shaped dose-response curves from oral sedation lecture). Opioids = greater NUMBER (more widely used). Don’t confuse these.
Only IV and inhalation routes permit titration. Oral/IN/IM = erratic, no control. “Once oral medication is ingested, there’s not much you can do.” IV is the only route allowing BOTH titration AND rapid reversal (for benzos and opioids).

Management Steps — Overdose

Terminate treatment

🚨 ALLERGY & ANAPHYLAXIS (Prof Spent Significant Time + Live EpiPen Demo)
Only 2 drugs NEVER shown to produce allergy: N₂O and O₂.
“Epinephrine allergy” is almost always an intravascular injection causing sympathomimetic effects (↑HR, agitation). NOT a true allergy. Document what actually happened.

Drug-Specific Allergy Notes

  • Barbiturate allergy → hives/urticaria or blood dyscrasias (agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia). More common in pts with asthma, urticaria, angioedema history.
  • Meperidine histamine release along vein path → looks like thrombophlebitis. NOT an allergy, no treatment needed. Classic MCQ distractor.

5 Questions for Allergy History

  1. What drug?
  2. What happened?
  3. What treatment? (epi? antihistamine?)
  4. Physician/EMS required? Hospitalized?
  5. Doctor’s name?

Clinical Manifestations

  • Most serious = Type I / Anaphylaxis — within 60 min. Targets: skin, respiratory, CV, GI.
  • Anaphylaxis + hypotension = “anaphylactic shock”
  • One system only = localized anaphylaxis
Onset Rule: Faster onset = MORE severe. Slower onset = less severe.

4 Syndromes of Generalized Anaphylaxis

Typical: Skin → Smooth muscle spasm → Respiratory distress → CV collapse (progressive).
Fatal: Respiratory + CV disturbances are predominant and EARLY.

⬇ HYPOTENSION (Prof Spent Moderate Time)

SBP <90. Context matters — 90/60 in ASA I young adult may be fine; same in elderly hypertensive = life-threatening.

11 Causes: Excessive premedication, steroids/anti-HTN/tranquilizers, sedative overdose, reflex (pain), vascular absorption of LA, hemorrhage, positional changes, hypoxia/hypercarbia, CV abnormalities, adrenocortical insufficiency, metabolic derangement.

Signs: Chest pain, SBP<90, HF, restlessness, anxiety, disorientation, pallor, cold/clammy skin, dilated pupils, prolonged cap refill, dyspnea.

Dilated pupils = hypotension/shock. Constricted pupils (miosis) = opioid OD. Opposite!

Management: P-A-B-C → Decrease inhalation agent / give antagonist (benzo→flumazenil, opioid→naloxone) / BLS for barbs → Fluids (rapid IV D5W/NS/RL) → Dopamine vasopressor for severe cases only.

⬆ HYPERTENSIVE EPISODES (Prof Spent Moderate Time)
Most common cause = PAIN (light anesthesia). Fix: give more local. Transient elevations usually well tolerated.

8 Causes: Light anesthesia, pain, hypercarbia, hypoxia (these 4 → catecholamine release), emergence delirium (connects to ketamine), fluid overload, hyperthermia, pre-existing cardiac.

Hypertensive crisis: SBP ≥250 and/or DBP ≥130 → end-organ damage. Prof confirmed this is real — he’s seen it. Most likely in chronic stable hypertension. Goal: do NOT drop BP rapidly → risk compromising cerebral perfusion → stroke. Bring down gradually. Distinguish cardiac vs. non-cardiac.

Definitive care: Monitor BP/HR q5 min + O₂ + fluids + EMS. Meds: Nitroprusside, IV nitroglycerin (prof: “not your priority drug”), IV labetalol (beta blocker — prof added, not on slide).

💓 CARDIAC DYSRHYTHMIAS (Prof Spent Moderate Time)
Most common intraoperative complication. Any deviation from NSR. Incidence under GA: 4-60%.

DeRango’s 5 Observations (HIGH YIELD)

  1. Majority of continuously monitored anesthetized pts WILL show some dysrhythmia (normal)
  2. Higher incidence with heart disease history
  3. Higher incidence in intubated patients
  4. More frequent in surgeries >3 hours
  5. Higher incidence on digitalis preoperatively

Precipitating factors (7): Halothane (sensitizes myocardium), elevated CO₂, pain under light anesthesia, vagal responses, intubation, anoxia, duration.

Management: Adequate ventilation, adjust anesthesia level, pain control. Continuous ECG required for deep sedation/GA.
