OBJ A1

Understand the Skeletal Anatomy of the Thorax

Prof walked through this as a physical palpation exercise, literally top-down on the body. Spent significant time — clearly important for both the pulmonary and cardiac exams.

The Landmark Palpation Sequence

Foundation for EVERYTHING in both cardiac and pulmonary exams — a step-by-step sequence performed on a real patient:

Suprasternal Notch→ Manubrium→ Sternal Angle (Angle of Louis)→ 2nd Rib (laterally)→ Count Interspaces downward
  • Suprasternal Notch: Divot at base of neck, above sternum — absolute starting point
  • Manubrium: Top segment of sternum, from notch down to first indentation
  • Sternal Angle (Angle of Louis): Where manubrium meets body of sternum — CRITICAL landmark. "If you were on that suprasternal notch and keep going inferiorly until you feel that indentation... go a little bit to your right, you're gonna feel a hard area that is gonna be your second rib." Where 2nd rib connects with manubrium = Angle of Louis = sternal angle.
  • Interspaces — NAMING CONVENTION: Interspace is ALWAYS named for the rib ABOVE it. Interspace 2 = below rib 2, interspace 3 = below rib 3, etc. Matters hugely for cardiac auscultation (valve locations).
As you count down interspaces, move your fingers LATERALLY (outward from sternum). Otherwise you'll lose count at ribs 7-9 because those ribs do not attach to the sternum.

Rib Attachments

RibsAttachmentType
1–7Attach directly to the sternumTrue ribs
8, 9, 10Each attaches to the rib above it (8→7, 9→8, 10→9)False ribs
11, 12Attach posteriorly to vertebrae, but NOT anteriorlyFree floating
  • Xiphoid Process: Inferior tip of sternum
CPR warning: Don't compress too low — breaking the xiphoid → pneumothorax. Classic MCQ distractor.
  • Costochondral Angle: Where costal cartilage meets ribs at inferior margin of rib cage
  • Why 1st rib is hard to find: It sits under the clavicle — that's why you use the sternal angle → 2nd rib method instead

Describe the Lobes of the Lungs and Where to Auscultate Each Area

Prof spent moderate time on lobes, then heavily emphasized the right middle lobe point — came back to it TWICE across the lecture.

Lung Lobes

Bronchial Tree — Trachea to Alveoli

  • Trachea descends from larynx, bifurcates at the Carina into R and L main stem bronchi
  • Carina: Heavily innervated → triggers cough reflex to prevent aspiration into lungs
  • R main stem bronchus = almost a straight shot. L = goes off at ~right angle.
Classic board question: Aspirated foreign bodies preferentially go to the RIGHT lung — right bronchus is wider, shorter, and more vertical.

Progressive Branching to the Alveoli

Main Stem Bronchi→ Lobar Bronchi→ Segmental Bronchi→ Bronchioles→ Terminal Bronchioles→ Respiratory Bronchioles→ Alveolar Ducts→ Alveolar Sacs→ Alveoli
  • Each alveolus surrounded by a capillary network that brings blood into close proximity with inhaled air.
  • Approximately 200 million alveoli per lung.
  • Extremely thin layer between capillary and alveolus where gas exchange occurs.

OBJ A4

Describe the Differences Between Internal and External Respiration

Prof spent moderate time here — repeated the gas exchange concept multiple times, fielded a student question, and made the critical N₂O connection.
TypeDefinitionLocation
External RespirationExchange of O₂ and CO₂ between an organism and the external environmentLungs — alveoli ↔ capillaries
Internal Respiration (Cellular)Utilization of oxygen by cells — cells use O₂ and produce CO₂Tissue level — cells ↔ capillaries

Gas Exchange — The Partial Pressure Principle

Core rule: Gases move from high partial pressure to low partial pressure. Not to zero — just needs a delta (difference) to drive diffusion.

  • Deoxygenated blood (high CO₂, low O₂) arrives at lungs via pulmonary arteries
  • O₂ is HIGH in alveolus, LOW in capillary blood → O₂ diffuses INTO capillary
  • CO₂ is HIGH in capillary blood, LOW in alveolus → CO₂ diffuses OUT into alveolus → exhaled
  • Oxygenated blood returns via pulmonary veins → left side of heart → body
  • At the cellular level: cells take in O₂ and give off CO₂ → deoxygenated blood returns to right heart → lungs → cycle repeats
Critical N₂O foreshadow: Same partial pressure physics drives rapid N₂O uptake — high PP N₂O inhaled + nothing in bloodstream = quick uptake. Expect an MCQ linking partial pressure gradients to rapid N₂O uptake.

Room Air Composition (Prof quizzed the class — fair game for MCQ)

21% O₂, ~78% nitrogen, very small amount of CO₂.

Steps of Respiration (5 steps from slide)

  • 1. Exchange of air between atmosphere and lungs (ventilation)
  • 2. O₂ and CO₂ alveolar exchange by diffusion (gas exchange)
  • 3. O₂ and CO₂ transport via pulmonary and systemic circulations (gas transport)
  • 4. O₂ and CO₂ exchange in tissue capillaries (gas exchange at tissues)
  • 5. Cellular utilization (cellular respiration)

2. Palpation

Prof spent significant time — detailed hands-on descriptions of both expansion and fremitus technique.

Four things you palpate for: Pain, Abnormalities (masses/lesions), Expansion, Fremitus.

Expansion Technique:

  • Patient's back facing you
  • Place your hands together, thumbs meeting at midline
  • Pinch up some skin at midline (~8 cm apart)
  • Ask patient to breathe in → watch for symmetrical expansion both sides
  • DO NOT press hard — don't restrict their movement, just let hands sit there and observe

Fremitus Technique:

  • Fremitus = palpable vibration
  • Place the ball of your hand on patient's back
  • Have patient say "99"
  • Compare one side to the other — the key principle
  • Use the 1-1, 2-2, 3-3, 4-4 pattern (same level, left vs. right)
  • Must be over lung fields, NOT over bone — bone alters the vibration
  • Can also be done anteriorly
Fremitus alone isn't the most reliable exam — but it directs you where to focus auscultation. Increased or decreased → investigate further.

3. Percussion

Prof spent a LOT of time here — very detailed technique description.

Technique (step-by-step):

  • Non-dominant hand: Middle finger ("pleximeter") flat on chest wall
  • ONLY the finger touches — thumb, other fingers, palm OFF the body
  • Strike at the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint area of that finger
  • Dominant hand: Middle finger, flick the wrist — tap tap, tap tap
  • Takes practice — people practice on textbooks

Two sounds you're listening for:

SoundMeaningAnalogy
ResonantAir-filled space underneath = normal lungLike percussing puffed-out cheeks
DullSolid or fluid-filled = abnormal (or below diaphragm)Like percussing your thigh — flat sound
  • Above diaphragm (over lungs) = resonant (normal)
  • Below diaphragm = dull (no air-filled lung tissue)
Clinical application: Percuss downward → find resonance-to-dullness transition (= diaphragm level) → have patient take deep breath → percuss below previous dull area. If now resonant = lungs are expanding. Another way to check lung expansion.

Positioning: Upper back = stand behind patient. Lower back = move to the SIDE for easier reach.

4. Auscultation

Prof: "This is probably like the most important exam if you're doing the pulmonary examination."

Normal breath sounds:

  • Vesicular: Soft, low-pitched, heard over most lung fields. Normal breathing sound.
  • Bronchial: Louder, higher-pitched, normally heard over trachea/large airways. If heard over peripheral lung fields = abnormal (suggests consolidation).

Adventitious sounds = sounds that shouldn't be there (detailed in next section).

The Ladder Pattern (Used for BOTH Percussion AND Auscultation)

Side-to-side comparison: 1-1, 2-2, 3-3 — always comparing one side to the other at the same level, looking for differences.

Heart Anatomy, Valves & Complete Blood Flow Pathway

Prof skipped reading objectives and went straight into anatomy. Moderate time on anatomy, significant time walking through blood flow interactively.

Orientation of the Heart

  • Anteriorly: Majority = RV, then RA, pulmonary artery off RV. Small portion of LV on left. LA not visible anteriorly.
  • Base of heart = superior
  • Apex = inferior
  • PMI (Point of Maximal Impulse) = at the apex = 5th interspace, midclavicular line

Complete Blood Flow — Valve by Valve

Right side (deoxygenated blood):

Body→ SVC (upper body) + IVC (lower body) + Coronary Sinus (heart's own drainage)→ Right Atrium→ TRICUSPID VALVE→ Right Ventricle→ PULMONIC VALVE→ Pulmonary Artery (splits R & L)→ LUNGS (gas exchange)

Left side (oxygenated blood):

Lungs→ 4 Pulmonary Veins (2 from each lung)→ Left Atrium→ MITRAL VALVE→ Left Ventricle→ AORTIC VALVE→ Aorta→ Body
  • Note: Deoxygenated blood still has some oxygen — just less than arterial blood
  • Coronary sinus: Heart's own blood vessels drain into RA
  • Prof wants you to know the path and the valves — not obscure details

The Four Valves in Order of Blood Flow

1. Tricuspid (RA → RV) · 2. Pulmonic (RV → PA) · 3. Mitral (LA → LV) · 4. Aortic (LV → Aorta)

OBJ B2

Know the Difference Between Systole, Diastole & What Makes S1 & S2

Prof spent the MOST time on this section of the entire cardiovascular lecture. Walked through entire pressure-valve logic step by step using Bates pressure curve diagrams. "It's important because we're gonna build off of this."
VentriclesAtria
Systole (S1 → S2)CONTRACTINGRelaxing / filling
Diastole (S2 → S1)RELAXING / fillingContracting

Key clarification: The atrial kick contributes blood to the ventricles, but it's not the sole filling source — there's always some residual blood in the ventricles.

S1 — "Lub" — Beginning of Systole

  1. Late diastole: atria contract → blood pushed into ventricles
  2. Ventricles begin to contract → ventricular pressure RISES
  3. Ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure → MITRAL and TRICUSPID valves CLOSE
  4. That closure = S1 (lub) = beginning of systole
  5. Predominantly hearing the Mitral component (higher L-sided pressure), but it's both valves

Mid-Systole

  1. Ventricular pressure continues to rise
  2. Ventricular pressure exceeds aortic/PA pressure → AORTIC and PULMONIC valves OPEN (silently)
  3. Blood ejected out to body (aorta) and lungs (PA)

S2 — "Dub" — End of Systole

  1. Ejection ends — no more fluid going out
  2. Ventricular pressure drops → aortic/PA pressure exceeds ventricular pressure
  3. AORTIC and PULMONIC valves CLOSE
  4. That closure = S2 (dub) = end of systole
  5. Predominantly hearing the Aortic component

The Golden Rules

  • Heart sounds are made by valves CLOSING, not opening. Valves open silently.
  • S1 = Mitral + Tricuspid CLOSE (mostly Mitral heard) = START of systole
  • S2 = Aortic + Pulmonic CLOSE (mostly Aortic heard) = END of systole
  • lub-DUB: between lub and dub = systole. After dub = diastole.
Prof's exam advice: UNDERSTAND the pressure logic — don't just memorize. This is what makes murmur questions solvable.

Describe Adventitious Sounds: S3, S4 & Splitting of S2

S3 — Rapid Ventricular Filling

  • Occurs in DIASTOLE — right AFTER S2 (early diastole)
  • Associated with CHF (Congestive Heart Failure)
  • Logic: Rapid ventricular filling → ventricles fill in diastole → S3 = diastole

S4 — Atrial Contraction

  • Occurs in DIASTOLE — right BEFORE S1 (late diastole)
  • S4 can be benign — may be heard on a healthy person

OBJ B4

Describe the Conduction System & Where Heart Sounds Align with ECG

Prof spent moderate time — key pathway, AV node delay reason, and critical "depolarization =/= contraction" point.

Conduction Pathway

SA Node (pacemaker)→ Internodal + Interatrial pathways→ AV Node (DELAY)→ Bundle of His→ R & L Bundle Branches→ Purkinje Fibers
  • Both atria depolarize simultaneously (interatrial pathway)
  • AV node delay purpose: Give time for atrium to contract and push blood into ventricles before the ventricles contract
  • SA node modulated by ANS — sympathetic speeds up, parasympathetic (vagal) slows down
Defibrillation =/= "jumpstarting" the heart. It depolarizes the entire heart so the pacemaker (SA node) hopefully picks up on its own. Common misconception — perfect MCQ.

Autonomic Regulation ("30,000 ft view")

Chemoreceptors and pressure receptors in the aortic arch and carotid bodies monitor BP and HR. Low BP/HR → sympathetic kicks in (↑HR, ↑BP). Too high → parasympathetic kicks in (↓HR, ↓BP).

Context matters for bradycardia: Cardiovascular athlete with HR in the 40s = normal. Sedentary person with HR in the 40s = probable heart block.

Blood Pressure — Full Technique

  1. Cuff on, palpate the brachial artery
  2. Stethoscope earpieces going FORWARD
  3. Inflate until you no longer feel the pulse, then pump up another 20 mmHg
  4. Slowly release air and listen
  5. First sound = SYSTOLIC (Korotkoff sounds — turbulent flow through compressed artery)
  6. Sound disappears = DIASTOLIC (free laminar flow, no turbulence)
  7. Keep listening another 10–20 mmHg to check for auscultatory gap (sound temporarily disappears then returns — don't call diastolic too early)

Physics: Fully occluded = silence (no flow). Partially compressed = turbulent flow = Korotkoff sounds (systolic). No compression = laminar flow = silence (diastolic).

  • Cuff too small → falsely elevated BP. Wrist cuffs: must follow positioning instructions exactly — often give falsely high readings.
Elective procedure cutoff: BP < 160/100. Emergency can push higher. This is the cutoff number to know.

THE RULE — How to Solve ANY Murmur Question

1. Location tells you the valve (e.g., R 2nd ICS = Aortic)

2. Timing tells you systole or diastole (S1-S2 = systole; S2-S1 = diastole)

Objective 2

Differences Between Minimal, Moderate, Deep Sedation & General Anesthesia

PROF EMPHASIZED Among the most heavily tested content. Focus on the distinguishing features between each level.

Sedation Level Definitions (from slides — know these word-for-word qualifiers)

Analgesia: Diminution or elimination of pain.

Local Anesthesia: Elimination of sensation/pain in one part of the body by topical application or regional injection.

Minimal Sedation: A minimally depressed level of consciousness, produced by a pharmacological method, that retains the patient's ability to independently and continuously maintain an airway and respond normally to tactile stimulation and verbal command. Cognitive function and coordination may be modestly impaired. Ventilatory and cardiovascular functions are UNAFFECTED. Patient is essentially wide awake.

Moderate Sedation: Drug-induced depression of consciousness during which patients respond purposefully to verbal commands, either alone or accompanied by light tactile stimulation. No interventions are required to maintain a patent airway, and spontaneous ventilation is adequate. Cardiovascular function is usually maintained.

Deep Sedation: Drug-induced depression of consciousness during which patients cannot be easily aroused but respond purposefully following repeated or painful stimulation. Ability to independently maintain ventilatory function may be impaired. May require assistance maintaining airway. Spontaneous ventilation may be inadequate. Cardiovascular function is usually maintained. May need to breathe for the patient.

General Anesthesia: Drug-induced loss of consciousness during which patients are NOT arousable, even by painful stimulation. Ventilatory function is often impaired. Often require assistance maintaining airway. Positive pressure ventilation may be required. Cardiovascular function may be impaired. Patient cannot be woken until drug wears off.

Objective 4

Exchange of N₂O in Alveoli → Circulatory System

PROF EMPHASIZED Built carefully using gas exchange principles from the pulmonary lecture.

Fundamental principle: Gases diffuse from high pressure → low pressure across the alveolar membrane.

Normal gas exchange reference (partial pressures in mmHg):

GasAlveolusArterial BloodVenous Blood
O₂10310040
CO₂404046

O₂ moves from alveolus (103) → venous blood (40) because of the pressure gradient. CO₂ moves from venous blood (46) → alveolus (40). Same principle applies to N₂O.

N₂O Uptake & Elimination — Step by Step

Uptake (start of case):

  1. N₂O inhaled → alveolus has HIGH N₂O partial pressure
  2. Blood has ZERO N₂O → massive pressure gradient
  3. N₂O rushes rapidly: alveolus → blood → brain
  4. As blood saturates → diffusion rate slows → equilibrium → steady state

Elimination (end of case):

  1. Switch to 100% O₂ → alveolus now has ZERO N₂O
  2. Blood is RICH in N₂O → gradient reverses
  3. N₂O rushes from blood → alveolus → exhaled out
  4. This is where diffusion hypoxia can occur — N₂O flooding into alveolus dilutes the O₂

Pharmacology numbers:

  • Blood-gas solubility coefficient = 0.47 at 37°C
  • Only ~0.06% metabolized in the body — rest goes in and comes out unchanged through the lungs
  • Not metabolized by the liver
  • Small quantities needed to reach required blood concentrations → "quick on, quick off"

Solubility and speed (prof explained with 3 graphs):

  • Totally insoluble gas: Never enters blood — useless
  • Relatively insoluble (= N₂O): Enters blood, reaches equilibrium quickly → fast onset + fast recovery
  • Highly soluble gas: Enters blood but takes very long to build up effective concentration — slow onset

N₂O's relative insolubility = its greatest pharmacologic advantage.

Objective 7

Concentration Effect & Secondary Gas Effect

QUICK / FLEW THROUGH concentration effect. MODERATE TIME second gas effect with a great analogy.

Concentration Effect ("Vacuum-Type Effect"): Higher concentration of gas inhaled → more rapid increase in arterial tension. Only significant at >70%. Since dental machines max at 70% → not clinically relevant for dental N₂O. Know the definition but understand it doesn't apply.

Second Gas Effect

  • When other inhalation agents are combined with N₂O-O₂ during GA
  • N₂O's rapid uptake creates a "vacuum" that pulls the second gas (volatile anesthetic) along
  • → increased concentration of second gas → can use lower concentrations of the more potent/dangerous agent
Think of a puddle of water outside — the water is the volatile agent. Think of the wind as your nitrous oxide. That puddle will evaporate at 5–10 mph but slowly. Bring the wind in at 30–40 mph — that puddle goes up faster. Higher N₂O concentration = more wind = pulls the second gas quicker.

Only relevant during general anesthesia, not dental N₂O alone.

Objective 8

Diffusion Hypoxia & How to Prevent It

PROF EMPHASIZED Key objective — full mechanism and clinical management.

🚨🚨 Diffusion Hypoxia — Full Mechanism

When: Occurs at termination of sedation (when you turn off N₂O).

Mechanism — 4 steps:

  1. Turn off N₂O → switch to 100% O₂
  2. Alveolus now has little/no N₂O, but blood is still rich in N₂O
  3. Massive pressure gradient reversal → N₂O rushes from blood into alveolus
  4. N₂O flood dilutes the O₂ in the alveolus → patient doesn't get enough O₂ → hypoxia

Prevention: 100% O₂ for 3–5 minutes post-procedure. Flushes N₂O out while maintaining adequate oxygenation.

"Hangover" symptoms: Headache, Nausea, Dizziness, Lethargy

  • Patient should leave the same way they arrived (minus the dental procedure)
  • If symptoms persist → keep giving O₂
  • Most patients recover in ~5 minutes; some take 10–15 minutes max
  • Do NOT discharge until symptoms resolve

Important caveat: Nasal hood is an open system — patient also breathes room air around the mask edges. Not truly 100% O₂ delivery, but sufficient to prevent diffusion hypoxia.

Equipment, Central vs. Portable, Safety Features & Scavenging

PROF EMPHASIZED equipment safety — guarantees exam questions. Most time on flowmeter safety, reservoir bag, and scavenging.

Central Gas Supply System — Prof spent significant time

  • Large G/H tanks in a separate tank room. Must be secured to walls with restrainers — a loose O₂ tank at 2000 psi is a projectile that can go through cinder block walls
  • Pressure regulation chain:

Tank (2000 psi) → Regulator (~50 psi) → Manifold (safe breathing pressure)

  • Manifold: Usually connects 2 tanks — when one empties, other kicks in automatically (continuous supply)
  • Plumbing: Copper piping, silver solder, different size tubing per gas (safety feature), installed by certified professional (not regular plumber), tested with nitrogen (not water), requires fire department sign-off
  • At the flowmeter level, central system = same as portable system

Flowmeter — Prof spent SIGNIFICANT time (Accutron specifics)

Components (from slide): O₂ Connection, O₂ Flow Tube, N₂O Flow Tube, O₂ Flush, Total Flow Knob, DISS connectors, Downspout (reservoir bag connection), Room Air Override, Outspout (gas to patient), % N₂O Knob.

PROF EMPHASIZED Accutron Safety — Must turn O₂ on FIRST:

  • O₂ automatically starts at 3 L/min
  • N₂O max = 7 L/min
  • Math: 3 L O₂ / (3 + 7) = 30% O₂ minimum guaranteed

O₂ Fail-Safe Shut-Off: If O₂ flow stops, N₂O valve automatically shuts off. Patient NEVER gets N₂O without O₂.

Room Air Override: If all gas flow fails, patient breathes room air — prevents suffocation.

Pediatric limitation: Fixed 3 L O₂ minimum means at 50% N₂O, total flow = 6 L/min — may be too much for a small pediatric patient.

Portable Systems — Digital vs. Manual

  • Manual flowmeter: Must constantly adjust N₂O and O₂ knobs to maintain desired ratio — more work
  • Digital system: Set total flow + desired % → machine calculates automatically. Much easier.
  • Some machines display % O₂ (not % N₂O): If showing 65% O₂ → N₂O = 35%. % N₂O + % O₂ = 100%. Know how to convert.
  • Some portable systems have face mask option — useful for emergencies

Pin Index Safety System — Prof spent moderate time

Pin Index = prevents wrong TANK on wrong YOKE (portable machines). Each gas has unique pin hole placement. If it doesn't fit, don't force it — it doesn't belong there.

DISS (Diameter Index Safety System) = prevents wrong HOSE on wrong PORTAL (both central and portable). Different diameter connectors for O₂ vs. N₂O at the back of the flowmeter.

Reservoir Bag — Prof spent MOST time of any equipment slide

BU uses a low-profile system (no bag) for convenience, but has backup for PPV with emergency O₂ tank + face mask.

Three Functions:

  1. Supplemental Gas Supply: Buffer for deep inspirations when patient overbreathes the set flow rate. Bag flattens slightly → refills.
  2. Respiratory Monitoring: Small movements = patient is breathing. (Will NOT move dramatically like on TV.)
  3. Flow Assessment:
    • Bag flattens = patient overbreathing → increase flow
    • Bag over-expands = too much flow → decrease flow
    • Protocol if flat: O₂ flush → fill to 2/3 → increase both N₂O + O₂ (maintain same %) → monitor
PPV with Reservoir Bag — Critical Point
Cannot provide PPV through the nasal hood — air just blows out through the nose. Must: remove nasal hood → connect face mask → switch to O₂ → squeeze bag for positive pressure ventilation.

Pre-oxygenation (3–5 min at start): Use this time to calibrate the proper flow rate for the patient.

Conduction Tubing — Prof spent moderate time with cautionary tale

Do NOT extend tubing beyond standard length. Two problems:

  1. Increases dead space (like anatomical dead space) → reduces effective N₂O delivery
  2. N₂O sits in tubing after the case and seeps into environment → more trace exposure for staff

Breathing Apparatus — Nasal Hood & Face Mask

  • Face mask in dentistry: Can't do restorative work with it on. Useful for short pediatric procedures — cycle on/off: gas → topical → gas → injection → gas → extraction → gas → O₂
  • Nasal Hood: Most are disposable (recommended). Most are scented (cherry, bubble gum, vanilla — may mask N₂O's sweet smell). Different sizes available. MUST possess scavenging capability (slide emphasizes this in bold).

Scavenging System — Prof spent significant time

How it works:

  1. Patient breathes in → valve closes off suction → full gas delivery
  2. Patient breathes out → valve opens → exhaled N₂O suctioned away → central vacuum → vented outside

Required suction rate: 45 L/min — most systems have a green zone indicator on the hose. Check throughout the case.

  • 4-hose system (traditional): 2 large bottom = delivery, 2 upper = suction. Bulky, hard in anterior region, wraps around head.
  • 2-hose system (low-profile — BU uses): 1 delivery + 1 continuous suction. Simpler, goes around ears, thin tubing, easy to autoclave. Continuous suction means adequate flow needed to compensate.

Exhaust vent must NOT be near HVAC intake — otherwise you recirculate N₂O into the building.

Adhesive nasal hood system: Adhesive around nasal piece → better seal. White knob for end-tidal CO₂ (capnography) hookup — great for deep sedation. One-time use. Plastic ring adapter stays with machine — NOT disposable.

Safety Features Article

1. "When Safety Features No Longer Are Safe"

Donaldson M, Donaldson D, Quarnstrom F. JADA 2012;143:134–143
⏱ Prof spent moderate time on this — opened the recording with it, used it as a bridge into the Technique lecture.

Background & Framing

  • N₂O-O₂ delivery systems are limited to a maximum of 70% N₂O / 30% O₂ — patient always receives at least 9% more oxygen than ambient air (21%)
  • Machines cannot go below 30% O₂ unless equipment is malfunctioning
  • Most important safety consideration: prevention of hypoxia

What the Article Found

The authors reviewed 12 safety features for N₂O-O₂ sedation. Despite these, dentists have still reported hypoxia incidents from:

  • Incorrect installation — primarily a risk with centralized systems (gas lines plumbed through building). Rare with portable units.
  • Equipment damage — machine used for years develops problems over time.

The Crossed Gas Lines Scenario

🎯 High-Yield Exam Scenario

Crossed gas lines: If you dial up "oxygen" and the patient becomes MORE sedated, the lines are reversed.

  • New installations — first time using a newly installed centralized system
  • Old machines with damage — been using it for years, suddenly patient on "100% O₂" is getting deeper

       ↓
  Patient gets MORE sedated       ← RED FLAG
       ↓
  Lines are REVERSED
       ↓
  You're actually delivering N₂O!
       ↓
  REMOVE MASK IMMEDIATELY

What To Do If Safety Feature Failure Suspected

Prof's Expanded Clinical Protocol

Slide says: "Remove the face mask from the patient immediately." Prof expands:

  1. Remove the nasal hood / face mask immediately
  2. Use your portable emergency oxygen tank — put patient on face mask or nasal cannula from that separate, known-good tank
  3. Do NOT just let them breathe room air — risk of diffusion hypoxia
🎯 Exam Alert — Diffusion Hypoxia

Why not just room air? N₂O dissolved in blood floods back into alveoli rapidly, diluting alveolar oxygen → transient hypoxia.

  • Mechanism: N₂O leaves blood → enters alveoli → displaces O₂ → hypoxemia
  • Could resolve over time, but you have emergency O₂ — use it
  • This is why recovery protocol calls for 5 minutes of 100% O₂ after any N₂O administration

5. Signs & Symptoms: Appropriate Sedation vs. Oversedation

Slides: "Signs & Symptoms of N₂O-O₂ Sedation" (multiple) / "Box 12-1" and "Box 12-2"

Signs of Appropriate Sedation

Behavioral:

  • Relaxed, comfortable — shoulders dropping, arms looser
  • Deeper respirations, deep sigh
  • Responds to directions (open mouth, turn head)
  • Conscious and aware of surroundings
  • Cough and gag reflexes remain intact

Eyes & Face:

  • Less active, "glazed" or "glassy" eyes
  • "Flat" expression — loss of forehead wrinkling if they came in nervous
  • Smiles easily

Patient-Reported Sensations:

  • Tingling in extremities / around mouth
  • Heaviness OR lightness of arms/legs
  • Comfortable body warmth
  • Vasodilation of face/neck → redness or perspiration
  • Circumoral numbness
🎯 Communication Technique — Use Suggestive Language
Correct (suggestive)Wrong (directive)
"You may feel some tingling...""You should be feeling tingling now."
"Your arms may feel heavy...""Your arms should feel heavy."
"These are all common with N₂O.""It's not working if you don't feel this."

Rationale: If you tell them they're supposed to have these symptoms and they don't feel them, they may think it's not working.

Clinical Pearl — Exaggerated Responses
  • Disproportionate/exaggerated responses = sign of oversedation
  • Example: you tell a bad joke → patient smiles = appropriate; patient laughs outrageously = too deep
  • Action: reduce N₂O percentage
Clinical Pearl — White Knuckle Syndrome
  • N₂O does NOT directly relax skeletal muscles
  • Patient lets go of armrests because anxiety decreased, not from direct muscle relaxation
  • The grip release = indirect behavioral sign of anxiolysis

Signs of Oversedation

🎯 Know These — The "Bad Things"
  • Detachment / dissociation from environment
  • Hallucinating, dreaming, fantasizing
  • Out-of-body experiences, floating, flying
  • Inability to move or communicate — RED FLAG (they should always be able to respond)
  • Humming that gets progressively louder — starts from discomfort, escalates as they get deeper
  • Drowsiness, dizziness
  • Diaphoresis (sweating)
  • Nausea, lightheadedness
  • Uncomfortable body warmth (contrast with comfortable warmth)
  • Fixed eyes (contrast with glazed eyes)
  • Sluggish/delayed responses, slurred words
  • Agitated or combative behavior
  • Vomiting → may progress to unconsciousness
Critical Teaching Point — Deeper ≠ Better
"You may put them deeper and think, well, maybe that's a good thing. No, it's not good — they're NOT gonna be comfortable and they're gonna be MORE anxious in the end."

Pushing deeper makes them MORE anxious, not less. The patient will NOT be comfortable — oversedation produces distressing experiences (hallucinations, loss of control, dissociation).

9. Recovery, Discharge & Reversal Agents

Slides: "Minimal Sedation Recovery and Discharge" / "Moderate Sedation Recovery and Discharge"

Recovery Protocol

Prof's Clinical Pearl on Recovery Timing

Practical technique tip: Turn off N₂O when you think you have 5 minutes left in your procedure. Switch to 100% O₂. By procedure end, 5 minutes have passed and patient should be recovered.

If NOT recovered after 5 minutes → keep giving oxygen until they recover.

Recovery checklist:

  • 100% O₂ for minimum 5 minutes at the same flow rate the patient was on
  • Verify NO signs of: lethargy, dizziness, lightheadedness, or headache
  • If any of those present → suspect diffusion hypoxia → keep on O₂ longer
  • Obtain post-operative vital signs
  • Document that level of consciousness, oxygenation, ventilation, and circulation are satisfactory prior to discharge
  • Give post-operative verbal and written instructions to patient/parent/escort/guardian

Full Technique Sequence — Know Every Step

  1. Assemble and assess armamentarium; open tanks and turn on flowmeter — Check scavenger (45 L/min), check for leaks (soapy water), ensure ventilation vents to outside
  2. Confirm scavenging devices functioning
  3. Health history reviewed; vital signs recorded
  4. Informed consent obtained
  5. Activate O₂ flow; fill reservoir bag approximately ⅔ full by use of O₂ flush valve
  6. Secure nasal hood — snug around nose; select appropriate size
  7. Establish appropriate tidal volume in L/min by observing reservoir bag
  8. Begin N₂O titration
  9. Assess patient for signs and symptoms of appropriate sedation
  10. Titrate N₂O to desired level
  11. Perform operative procedures
  12. Terminate N₂O flow; administer 5 minutes postoperative 100% O₂
  13. Assess recovery
  14. Continue postoperative oxygenation if necessary
  15. Ensure recovery and dismiss patient
  16. Complete sedation record
  17. Disinfect and/or sterilize sedation equipment

Patient Communication — Nose Breathing

Prof Spent Meaningful Time on This
  • Coach patient: in the nose, out the nose — continue breathing through their nose
  • If patient starts talking: "If you talk, you're not gonna get the full effect" — that gets them to stop
  • The flip side: don't ask them 50 questions — keep questions to a minimum. Can't coach nose-breathing while simultaneously prompting them to talk.

Constant vs. Variable O₂ Technique

Constant O₂ Level Technique
  • O₂ flow stays fixed (e.g., 6 L/min)
  • Add N₂O on top → total flow increases
  • Simpler
  • Patients can usually tolerate higher total flow (up to ~10 L/min)
Variable O₂ Level Technique
  • As N₂O goes up, O₂ goes down
  • Keeps total flow constant
  • More complex — constantly adjusting both dials
Prof's Practical Tip

Digital flowmeters do the variable technique automatically — you set the percentage, it calculates flow rates for you.

N₂O Percentage Formula
N₂O% = L of N₂O ÷ (L of N₂O + L of O₂) × 100

It's always Part ÷ Whole

20. Objectives, Indications & Disadvantages of Pediatric N₂O

Slides: "Objectives of N₂O-O₂ Sedation" / "Indications" / "Disadvantages"

Decision to Use — Multifactorial

  • Must be discussed with the parent: why are we doing this? Is there an alternative?
  • Generally accepted by most children — some report dreaming or "being on a space ride"
  • Won't work if: patient feels they're losing control (adults too), or is claustrophobic from nasal hood

Objectives of Pediatric N₂O Sedation

  • Reduce/eliminate anxiety
  • Reduce untoward movement and reaction to dental treatment
  • Enhance communication and patient cooperation — communication WITH N₂O is essential. Prof's anecdote: a resident worked too fast, Prof stepped in and demonstrated how communication + N₂O made an anxious extraction successful.
  • Raise the pain reaction threshold
  • Increase tolerance for longer appointments — with a critical caution below:
🎯 Cross-Topic Connection — Local Anesthetic Max Dose

The limiting factor in lengthy procedures isn't the N₂O — it's the local anesthetic maximum dose.

  • N₂O can run for a long time (though duration increases adverse effects)
  • But multi-quadrant dentistry requires lots of local anesthetic
  • Be careful about patient size and do not exceed maximum dose
  • Aid treatment of mentally/physically disabled or medically compromised patients
  • Reduce gagging
  • Potentiate effect of sedatives — warning: pediatric patients may use illicit/diverted medications without parents' knowledge

Indications for Pediatric N₂O

  • Fearful, anxious patient — but NOT phobic
  • Special healthcare needs patients
  • Gag reflex interfering with dental care
  • Patient for whom profound local anesthesia cannot be obtained (e.g., abscessed tooth — may help, may not carry through; patient may need deep sedation)
  • Cooperative patient undergoing lengthy procedures

Disadvantages

  • Lack of potency — can't put patients to sleep. But Prof reframes: this is also a safety feature — prevents getting them very deep.
  • Dependent on psychological reassurance — provider must believe in it. But also: recognize when patient needs deeper sedation.
  • Nasal hood interference with anterior maxillary region injections — low-profile helps but still challenging
  • Patient must breathe through nose — won't work with obstruction, URI, deviated septum
  • Trace gas exposure — not a patient concern but an occupational health hazard for staff. If you can't control trace levels, this is a chronic exposure risk. (Connects to biohazards lecture.)

🎯 Critical Concept — N₂O ≠ Usable Oxygen

N₂O contains an oxygen atom (N₂O), but the body CANNOT use it for cellular respiration.

The "Second Person in the Room" Rule

🎯 Major Medicolegal Point

Always have a second person in the room when using >50% N₂O.

Why? N₂O at high concentrations can cause:

  • Sexual phenomena / hallucinations
  • Auditory illusions
  • Dreaming and fantasizing

Healthcare professionals have been brought before boards — even when nothing happened, patients believed something occurred due to hallucinations.

The second person serves as a third-party witness to protect both patient and provider.

  • N₂O acts on the endogenous opioid system → potential gateway for opioid-prone individuals

EXAM NIOSH Standards — The Numbers and WHY They Differ
TIME Prof spent significant time explaining WHY the numbers differ

1977 — NIOSH (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) set exposure standards:

Operating Room = 25 ppm

Easier to achieve because:

  • Endotracheal tube = closed system → minimal trace gas leaking into room
  • ORs have air exchangers that turn over room air regularly

Dental Setting = 50 ppm

Harder to achieve because:

  • Nasal hood = open system (mouth open, patient talks, mask displaced)
  • Dental offices typically lack OR-caliber air exchange systems
🔴 CRITICAL DISTINCTION

The 50 ppm dental standard is NOT enforced by OSHA. It is a recommendation, not a regulation. The goal in the dental office is simply to minimize trace gases as much as possible.

🔴 ALLEN'S TEST — Prof gave detailed clinical explanation

Connects to invasive BP monitoring (arterial lines). The arterial line goes into the radial artery — accessible, clean, gives second-to-second BP, allows arterial blood gas draws.

  2. You compress BOTH ulnar and radial arteries
  3. Patient opens hand — should be pale/blanched
  4. You release the ulnar artery only

  6. If hand stays pale → no collateral from ulnar artery → do NOT put arterial line in radial artery ✗

  ──────────── Vein Wall ────────────────

4IV Sedation
KEY Historical Foundations & Definition
TIME William Harvey — moderate pace. Jorgensen — briefly.

William Harvey: Demonstrated continuous circulation of blood within a closed system. Showed that valves in heart and veins → unidirectional blood flow. This is the fundamental principle that makes IV medication delivery possible. Before Harvey, people thought blood left the body (open system).

Neils Bjorn Jorgensen (1945): Probably first to use IV route for premedication. "Jorgensen's technique" / "Loma Linda technique":

  • Pentobarbital (barbiturate)
  • Meperidine/Demerol (opioid)
  • Scopolamine (anticholinergic)
TIME IV Sedation Definition — moderate, connected to N₂O

Slide: Administration of a drug → depression of the CNS. Goal: produce a state of sedation in which patient maintains protective reflexes, respiratory and cardiovascular systems minimally affected.

Same goal as N₂O sedation — just different route. Both aim for CNS depression while maintaining protective reflexes. Moderate sedation benchmark: patient should be able to talk to you and just feel really comfortable.

EXAM IV Sedation Advantages — ALL 7 with Full Prof Commentary
TIME Prof spent SIGNIFICANT time — especially on titration and N/V prevention

1. Rapid onset: Arm-to-brain circulation = 20–25 seconds

2. Titration — Prof's most emphasized advantage:
  • Not everyone responds the same (bell-shaped curve): hyper-responders (small dose → way out) and hypo-responders (large dose → barely affected)
  • Titration = give small amounts, work up → only give minimum amount needed
  • Result: faster recovery, fewer side effects
  • This is the fundamental advantage of IV over IM (IM cannot titrate)

3. Continuous IV infusion — 3 purposes:

  • Keep a patent vein for additional medication if needed
  • Give fluids to NPO patient (8-12 hours without food/drink)
  • Usually D5W or sugar water to help with metabolism

4. N/V rare when administered correctly. Mostly from opioids. Screening question: "Do you get car sick?" or sick on amusement park rides. If yes → prophylactic antiemetic IV before the procedure.

5. Salivation control: IV anticholinergics.

6. Gag reflex diminished.

  Simple gag issues (impressions)  → try N₂O first (5 min recovery, walk out)
  Surgical procedures with gag     → IV sedation works much better than N₂O
                                     (but longer recovery)

7. Diminish motor disturbances / seizure activity. BUT some IV medications can also lower the seizure threshold — addressed with specific drugs later.

EXAM Benzo Mechanism, Properties & Contraindications
Clinically Useful Properties

Antianxiety, sedative-hypnotic, anticonvulsant, skeletal muscle relaxant. Acts on the hippocampus.

  • Jaw dislocation: muscles in spasm → benzos as muscle relaxant to reduce the dislocation
  • Dose-dependent effect spectrum: anxiolysis → sedation → anticonvulsant. They break seizures in emergencies
Mechanism of Action
Benzo binds stereospecific receptor on postsynaptic GABA neuron → ↑ Cl⁻ permeability into cell (intracellular) → hyperpolarization → less excitable state → stabilization

Ties back to Pain Control I: intracellular/extracellular ion shifts. Driving chloride INTO the cell.

7Pharmacology — Barbiturates
EXAM Barbiturates — Full Overview, Contraindications, Pain Threshold
TIME Prof covered at moderate pace with personal clinical experience

Historical: Major drug before propofol. Less common now but still testable.

Mechanism: Generalized CNS depressants — cerebral cortex, limbic system, reticular activating system (sleep/wakefulness).

Spectrum: Light sedation → hypnosis → GA → coma → death. Classified: ultra-short, short, long acting.

Key pharmacologic facts:

  • No effect on pain threshold until LOC — then pain threshold DROPS. Patients become MORE reactive to pain under barbiturate sedation. Even pressure can cause movement
  • Have anticonvulsant properties (raise seizure threshold — with ONE exception, see methohexital below)
  • Eliminated by biotransformation in liver, excreted through kidneys. Liver problems → use less, be very careful
  • NO REVERSAL AGENT EXISTS for barbiturates (unlike benzos and opioids)
  • Hangover effect — drug distributes into fat and slowly "leaches out" → slow recovery
  • Typical procedures: 15–45 minutes
Modern preference: propofol over barbiturates. Propofol has replaced barbiturates in most settings.
Contraindications
  1. Allergy
  2. Uncontrolled pain — pain threshold DROPS under sedation; patients become hyperreactive
  3. Addiction to sedative-hypnotics
  4. Acute intermittent porphyria — classic board exam fact
  5. Respiratory disease with dyspnea or obstruction — barbiturates are respiratory depressants
KEY Pentobarbital — Drug of Choice for Elevated ICP

Classification: Short-acting barbiturate. Used for IV induction and seizure control.

Effects/Side effects:

  1. Reduces cerebral metabolism
  2. Modest decrease in blood pressure — avoid in hypotensive patients
  3. Temporary respiratory depression
  4. Hangover (fat redistribution effect)

Advantages: Dose-dependent amnesia, dose-dependent drowsiness, hypnosis, DRUG OF CHOICE for elevated intracranial pressure (ICP).

Clinical context: In dental office, unlikely to encounter elevated ICP. But for neurosurgery patients needing intubation with elevated ICP, barbiturates are the drug of choice. Prof repeated this across two class sessions = testable.

Disadvantages: Respiratory depression, decreased SVR → hypotension if volume depleted, no analgesia, highly addictive with prolonged use, cardiovascular depression (vasodilation), myoclonus, painful injection.

EXAM🔴 Methohexital (Brevital) — THE Big Seizure Threshold Distinction
🔴 CRITICAL DISTINCTION — CLASSIC BOARD QUESTION

All barbiturates RAISE the seizure threshold (anticonvulsant) EXCEPT:

METHOHEXITAL (BREVITAL) — LOWERS SEIZURE THRESHOLD

This means methohexital can INDUCE SEIZURES in patients with seizure history. It is the opposite of every other barbiturate.

Additional facts:

  • Duration parallels propofol
  • Propofol has essentially replaced methohexital in modern practice
  • Both reliably produce hypotension
  • Clinically useful IV doses cause at least some respiratory depression

Barbiturates = drugs of choice for procedural sedation and airway management with high ICP.

💨 Inhalation Anesthetics — N₂O in the GA Context

Inhalation anesthetics = most frequently used means of producing GA. Agents: N₂O, Halothane, Enflurane, Isoflurane, Desflurane, Sevoflurane.

Prof immediately pivoted to testing the class on N₂O.

Prof asked the class directly: “Is N₂O a good general anesthetic?” → NO.
  • N₂O alone = only minimal sedation. Can reach moderate sedation if >50%
  • A hyper-responder could drift to deep sedation — must rescue them back
  • N₂O MAC = 104–105% — physically impossible to deliver enough (can’t give >100%) → weak general anesthetic
N₂O’s true role in GA = Second Gas Effect / Potentiation
  • High-concentration N₂O alongside another volatile agent → rapid uptake concentrates the second gas in alveoli
  • Result: need less of the potent agent
  • Analogy: wind blowing over a puddle of water
  • Slide: “Primary function in GA is to potentiate the actions of the other more potent drugs and thus permits a smaller dose or lesser concentration.”
⚠ MCQ trap: “What is the role of N₂O in GA?” → Potentiation / second gas effect, NOT that it produces GA on its own.
📐 Understanding MAC (Minimum Alveolar Concentration)
Prof re-explained MAC with a vivid example: “If I had 50 people here and the MAC was 0.75, I gave everybody 0.75 MAC, and I did a standard surgical incision — half would move, half wouldn’t move — that’s without a local anesthetic.”

MAC = concentration at which 50% of patients won’t respond to a surgical stimulus (without local anesthetic).

CRITICAL RULE: Lower MAC = More Potent.
  • MAC 0.75% = strong anesthetic (little drug needed)
  • MAC 6% = weak anesthetic (need much more drug)

Dr. Manish K. Bhagania BDS, MDS — Clinical Associate Professor, OMS

These are emergencies specific to sedation, different from general dental office emergencies.

⚠ Introduction — ADRs

Dental office must identify, document, and manage ADRs + contact PCP. Most are NOT life-threatening — but some are.

3 reasons ADRs occur:
  1. Dentist error — but statistically LEAST likely cause. Due diligence = your defense. (Prof: “Which is what is trying to be proven in the courts.”)
  2. Random events — unpredictable even when everything done right
  3. Reasonable risk of treatment — e.g., syncope during injection. If patient history was not seen/recorded/evaluated properly → that patient will have the adverse event. Thorough history + documentation = best protection.

🚨 ALLERGY & ANAPHYLAXIS (Prof Spent Significant Time + Live EpiPen Demo)
Only 2 drugs NEVER shown to produce allergy: N₂O and O₂.
“Epinephrine allergy” is almost always an intravascular injection causing sympathomimetic effects (↑HR, agitation). NOT a true allergy. Document what actually happened.

Drug-Specific Allergy Notes

  • Barbiturate allergy → hives/urticaria or blood dyscrasias (agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia). More common in pts with asthma, urticaria, angioedema history.
  • Meperidine histamine release along vein path → looks like thrombophlebitis. NOT an allergy, no treatment needed. Classic MCQ distractor.

5 Questions for Allergy History

  1. What drug?
  2. What happened?
  3. What treatment? (epi? antihistamine?)
  4. Physician/EMS required? Hospitalized?
  5. Doctor’s name?

Clinical Manifestations

  • Most serious = Type I / Anaphylaxis — within 60 min. Targets: skin, respiratory, CV, GI.
  • Anaphylaxis + hypotension = “anaphylactic shock”
  • One system only = localized anaphylaxis

⬇ HYPOTENSION (Prof Spent Moderate Time)

SBP <90. Context matters — 90/60 in ASA I young adult may be fine; same in elderly hypertensive = life-threatening.

11 Causes: Excessive premedication, steroids/anti-HTN/tranquilizers, sedative overdose, reflex (pain), vascular absorption of LA, hemorrhage, positional changes, hypoxia/hypercarbia, CV abnormalities, adrenocortical insufficiency, metabolic derangement.

Signs: Chest pain, SBP<90, HF, restlessness, anxiety, disorientation, pallor, cold/clammy skin, dilated pupils, prolonged cap refill, dyspnea.

Pupil cheat sheet: 
  Dilated pupils    ──▶ Hypotension / Shock
  Constricted pupils ──▶ Opioid OD
  (OPPOSITE!)

Management: P-A-B-C → Decrease inhalation agent / give antagonist (benzo→flumazenil, opioid→naloxone) / BLS for barbs → Fluids (rapid IV D5W/NS/RL) → Dopamine vasopressor for severe cases only.

⬆ HYPERTENSIVE EPISODES (Prof Spent Moderate Time)
Most common cause = PAIN (light anesthesia). Fix: give more local. Transient elevations usually well tolerated.

8 Causes:

  • Catecholamine-release group (4): Light anesthesia, pain, hypercarbia, hypoxia
  • Other (4): Emergence delirium (connects to ketamine), fluid overload, hyperthermia, pre-existing cardiac
Hypertensive crisis: SBP ≥250 and/or DBP ≥130 → end-organ damage. Most likely in chronic stable hypertension.

Goal: do NOT drop BP rapidly → risk compromising cerebral perfusion → stroke. Bring down gradually. Distinguish cardiac vs. non-cardiac.

Definitive care: Monitor BP/HR q5 min + O₂ + fluids + EMS. Meds: Nitroprusside, IV nitroglycerin (not priority drug per prof), IV labetalol (beta blocker — prof added, not on slide).

💓 CARDIAC DYSRHYTHMIAS (Prof Spent Moderate Time)
Most common intraoperative complication. Any deviation from NSR. Incidence under GA: 4–60%.

DeRango’s 5 Observations (HIGH YIELD)

  1. Majority of continuously monitored anesthetized pts WILL show some dysrhythmia (normal)
  2. Higher incidence with heart disease history
  3. Higher incidence in intubated patients
  4. More frequent in surgeries >3 hours
  5. Higher incidence on digitalis preoperatively

Precipitating factors (7): Halothane (sensitizes myocardium), elevated CO₂, pain under light anesthesia, vagal responses, intubation, anoxia, duration.

Management: Adequate ventilation, adjust anesthesia level, pain control. Continuous ECG required for deep sedation/GA.
