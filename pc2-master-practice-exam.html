THE HEART IN 5 MINUTES

Blood flow — memorize this sentence:

Vena cava → Right Atrium → Right Ventricle → Pulmonary Arteries → LUNGS → Pulmonary Veins → Left Atrium → Left Ventricle → Aorta → Body
Trick
RIGHT side = deoxygenated, going TO lungs. LEFT side = oxygenated, going TO body. Pulmonary arteries carry DEOXY blood (only artery that does). Pulmonary veins carry OXY blood (only vein that does).

HEART SOUNDS — the only thing you need

S1 = Mitral + Tricuspid close = START of systole (AV valves slam shut)

S2 = Aortic + Pulmonic close = START of diastole (semilunar valves slam shut)

Memory trick
S1 = "Mitral/Tricuspid" = think "M-T" = the first two letters of the alphabet that look like mountains (AV valves)

S3 = volume overload = CHF (ventricle is floppy, blood sloshes in)

S4 = stiff ventricle = hypertrophy/decreased compliance (atria pushing against a stiff wall)

Splitting of S2 = normal during inspiration (pulmonic valve closes late because more blood returns to right heart)

MURMURS — the only algorithm you need

WHEN does it happen?

  • Systolic murmur (between S1→S2) = Stenosis of Aortic/Pulmonic OR Regurgitation of Mitral/Tricuspid
  • Diastolic murmur (between S2→S1) = Stenosis of Mitral/Tricuspid OR Regurgitation of Aortic/Pulmonic
Memory
"PASS" = Pulmonic/Aortic Stenosis = Systolic

Holosystolic (pansystolic) = blood rushing from high→low pressure the WHOLE time = VSD, mitral regurg, tricuspid regurg

Echocardiogram = THE diagnostic test of choice for any murmur. Period.

CARDIAC MATH — 3 formulas, that's it

Cardiac Output = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume

MAP = (SBP + 2×DBP) / 3
→ Example: BP 110/80 → MAP = (110 + 160)/3 = 90

Pulse Pressure = SBP − DBP
→ Example: 110/80 → Pulse pressure = 30

Preload = stretch BEFORE contraction (how full the ventricle is)
Afterload = resistance AGAINST contraction (what the heart pushes against)

IE PROPHYLAXIS — the decision tree

WHO needs it? (4 conditions only):

  1. Prosthetic valve
  2. Previous IE
  3. Certain CHD
  4. Cardiac transplant WITH valvulopathy

Who does NOT? → Pacemakers, stents, septal closure devices, vascular grafts

WHAT procedures? → Manipulation of gingival tissue, periapical region, or perforation of oral mucosa

WHAT drug?

Amoxicillin 2g PO, 30-60 min before

  • PCN allergy → Cephalexin 2g OR Azithromycin 500mg OR Doxycycline 100mg
  • Can't swallow → Ampicillin 2g IM/IV or Cefazolin/Ceftriaxone 1g IM/IV

LUNGS IN 5 MINUTES

Primary breathing muscle = DIAPHRAGM (that's it, everything else is accessory)

Gas exchange = 100% passive diffusion driven by partial pressure gradients. No active transport. None.

Internal respiration = gas exchange at the tissues (O₂ to cells, CO₂ back)
External respiration = gas exchange at the alveoli (O₂ in, CO₂ out)

PERIPHERAL VASCULAR — 5 things to know

Allen's test = tests radial + ulnar artery patency (do BEFORE radial artery access)

Raynaud's = cold → white fingers (vasospasm) → blue → red. Sharply demarcated.

Chronic venous insufficiency = pitting edema + brown skin pigmentation + possible ulcers (incompetent valves)

Venipuncture preference order: Dorsum of hand > Ventral forearm > Lateral antecubital > Dorsal wrist > Medial antecubital (WORST — near brachial artery + median nerve)

IM injection volumes: Vastus lateralis = MOST volume (4-5 mL adult). Deltoid = LEAST (1-2 mL) but MOST COMMON site.

PULSE/BP QUICK HITS

  • Evaluate before treatment: HR below 60 or above 110 → investigate first
  • Hyperventilation = rapid deep breathing → anxious patient → CO₂ blown off → respiratory alkalosis → tingling, lightheadedness
  • BP: MAP formula above. Always monitor before, during, after sedation.
  • Hypertensive crisis = SBP ≥250 and/or DBP ≥130

THE 30-SECOND ALGORITHM FOR ANY AUSCULTATION MCQ

Algorithm
  1. Read the findings listed
  2. Check percussion: Dull → pneumonia or effusion. Hyperresonant → pneumothorax or emphysema
  3. Check fremitus: Increased → pneumonia (ONLY one that increases it)
  4. Check breath sounds: Wheezes → asthma. Crackles → CHF. Absent → pneumothorax
  5. Egophony/bronchophony/pectoriloquy = always pneumonia

LECTURE D: N₂O PHARMACOLOGY

N₂O PHYSICAL PROPERTIES — the 5-point list:

  1. Nonirritating
  2. Sweet-smelling (this is N₂O, NOT oxygen — exam trap)
  3. Colorless
  4. Boiling point: −88.5°C (gas at room temp)
  5. Converts to liquid at 28°C and 50 atm pressure

CYLINDERS:

  • O₂ = GREEN, 2000 psi at 70°F
  • N₂O = BLUE, 750 psi at 70°F (575 at 50°F)
  • N₂O stored as 95% liquid, 5% vapor
  • N₂O pressure gauge stays at 750 until almost empty → NOT accurate indicator of remaining supply

N₂O KEY NUMBERS

N₂O GAS EFFECTS — the three you MUST know

Concentration Effect = "Vacuum effect" — higher concentration inhaled → faster arterial tension rises. Significant only at >70%.

Second Gas Effect = When N₂O is combined with another inhalation agent, rapid N₂O uptake creates vacuum that concentrates the second gas → minimizes required dose of the other agent.

Diffusion Hypoxia = Occurs at TERMINATION — N₂O floods back into alveoli and dilutes O₂ → headache, nausea, dizziness, lethargy ("hangover"). Prevention: 100% O₂ for 3–5 minutes post-sedation.

N₂O CONTRAINDICATIONS — absolute vs relative

ABSOLUTE (memorize the list):

  • COPD
  • Cystic fibrosis
  • Pneumothorax
  • Significant bowel obstruction
  • B12 deficiency / pernicious anemia
  • Recent pneumoencephalography
  • First trimester pregnancy
  • Bleomycin sulfate therapy (cancer)
  • Psychological impairment / psychotropic drug use
  • Current/recovering substance abuse
  • Unable to understand/consent

  • Upper respiratory infection
  • Middle ear disturbance/surgery
  • Recent eye surgery (perfluoropropane/sulfur hexafluoride)

NOT a contraindication: MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA (N₂O is safe in MH patients!)

Memory trick
Anything with trapped gas spaces is absolute (COPD, pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, middle ear). Anything involving DNA/reproduction is absolute (B12, first trimester, bleomycin).

N₂O ADVANTAGES vs DISADVANTAGES

Advantages: "PRACTICE BUILDER" — Rapid onset, rapid recovery, depth controllable, no injection, excellent safety record, patient acceptance

Disadvantages: Cost of equipment, low potency, requires patient cooperation, chronic exposure risks, staff training

CASE REVIEW — FULL BREAKDOWN

Lecture N — all three cases plus every question, explained simply and completely. Let's crush this.

CASE #1

The Patient: Healthy 21-year-old male. Needs extraction of 4 full bony impacted third molars (#1, 16, 17, 32). PMH: spontaneous pneumothorax at age 18. No meds, NKDA, ear tubes at age 3. Wants "something more than local anesthesia."

Question 1: What would you offer this patient (if trained in all forms of anesthesia)?

The key options beyond local anesthesia are N₂O/O₂ sedation, IV sedation (moderate sedation), or general anesthesia.

BUT HERE'S THE TRAP
This patient has a history of spontaneous pneumothorax. Pneumothorax is an ABSOLUTE contraindication to N₂O. Why? Because N₂O is 34x more soluble than nitrogen — it diffuses into closed air-filled spaces faster than nitrogen can diffuse out, causing those spaces to expand. A pneumothorax is a trapped pocket of air in the pleural space. Give this patient N₂O and you could re-expand or worsen a pneumothorax. This could be life-threatening.

So your answer: You would offer IV moderate sedation or general anesthesia. For 4 full bony impactions, IV sedation or GA is appropriate anyway given the complexity of the surgery. N₂O is OFF the table entirely.

Question 2: What medications would you use?

For IV moderate sedation, the standard combination is:

  • Midazolam (Versed) — a benzodiazepine. Provides anxiolysis, sedation, and anterograde amnesia. It's 2-3x more potent than diazepam, water-soluble (less irritating to veins), rapid onset (1-3 min IV), short duration.
  • Fentanyl — a synthetic opioid analgesic. Provides pain control during the procedure. Very potent, rapid onset, short acting.
  • Local anesthesia — still required! IV sedation doesn't eliminate pain, it manages anxiety and consciousness. You always supplement with local anesthetic for actual pain control at the surgical sites.

If doing GA, you'd use agents like propofol for induction, potentially a paralytic agent, and maintain the airway with intubation.

Question 3: What medications would you AVOID and why?

  • N₂O — ABSOLUTELY AVOID. Pneumothorax is an absolute contraindication (explained above — gas expansion in closed spaces).
  • Ketamine — should be used cautiously or avoided. It can increase secretions and has sympathomimetic effects. Not first-line for a routine dental extraction under sedation.
  • NSAIDs (like ibuprofen) post-op — actually these are fine for this patient since he has no GI issues or allergies. So no special avoidance here on the pain management side.

The big testable point: N₂O is the drug to avoid because of the pneumothorax history.

Question 4: What is your preoperative workup?

This is a systematic checklist — think of it as the standard pre-sedation evaluation:

  • PMH (Past Medical History): Review the pneumothorax — when did it happen, was it treated (chest tube?), has he had imaging since? Has it recurred? Any current respiratory symptoms? The ear tubes at age 3 are remote history and not clinically significant now.
  • Medications: Currently none — good, no drug interactions to worry about.
  • Allergies: NKDA — confirmed.
  • Physical Exam (PE):
    • Vital signs — baseline BP, HR, RR, O₂ sat, temperature
    • Lung auscultation — CRITICAL given pneumothorax history. Listen to all lung fields bilaterally. Are breath sounds equal? Any decreased sounds on one side? Any wheezing, crackles?
    • Cardiac exam — auscultate heart sounds
  • Airway Assessment:
    • Mallampati Classification (I-IV) — have patient open mouth, stick out tongue, assess visibility of the oropharyngeal structures. Class I (full visibility of soft palate, fauces, uvula, pillars) is easiest to intubate; Class IV (only hard palate visible) is most difficult.
    • Mouth opening, neck mobility, jaw protrusion, thyromental distance
  • ASA Classification: This patient is likely ASA II — he has a history of a systemic disease (previous pneumothorax) but it's not currently limiting. If fully resolved with no recurrence, could argue ASA I, but the history bumps him to II to be safe.
  • Consider chest X-ray if there's any concern about recurrence of pneumothorax.
  • Informed consent — explain the procedure, risks, benefits, alternatives, and get written consent.

Question 6: What IV site would you avoid due to proximity of vital structures?

The medial antecubital fossa (the inner part of the elbow crease).

Why? Because the brachial artery and median nerve run right through there. If you accidentally hit the brachial artery, you get arterial puncture and potentially a large hematoma. If you damage the median nerve, the patient could have sensory or motor deficits.

The slide shows the order of preference for venipuncture sites (from Malamed):

  1. Dorsum of the hand (first choice)
  2. Ventral forearm
  3. Lateral antecubital fossa (outer elbow — safer)
  4. Dorsal wrist
  5. Medial antecubital fossa (LAST choice — avoid if possible)

Question 7: IV started, good blood return, but fluids aren't running. Causes?

The slide gives you four causes of a non-running IV:

  1. IV infusion bag too close to (or below) heart level — IV fluids run by gravity. If the bag isn't elevated enough above the patient, fluid won't flow.
  2. Bevel of the needle against the wall of the vein — the opening of the catheter is pushed up against the vein wall, blocking flow. Repositioning the catheter slightly fixes this.
  3. Tourniquet left on the arm — this is a classic mistake. You applied a tourniquet to find the vein, started the IV, but forgot to release the tourniquet. The pressure from the tourniquet blocks venous return and prevents flow.
  4. Infiltration — the catheter has slipped out of the vein and fluid is going into the surrounding tissue instead. You'd see painless, colorless swelling at the IV site.

Question 8: Blood backing up into the IV line — most likely cause?

The IV bag is too low (too close to heart level).

This is pure physics/gravity. The IV fluid column must have higher pressure than the venous pressure to flow into the patient. If the bag is at or below the level of the patient's heart, venous pressure exceeds the fluid column pressure, and blood backs up into the tubing. The fix is simple: raise the IV bag higher.

Question 10: Gave too much midazolam — what do you do?

Flumazenil (Romazicon)

The specific benzodiazepine reversal agent (antagonist).

Key points about flumazenil:

  • It competitively binds to the GABA-A/benzodiazepine receptor, displacing midazolam
  • Onset: 1-2 minutes IV
  • Duration: ~45-60 minutes (IMPORTANT — midazolam may outlast flumazenil, so you need to monitor for re-sedation)
  • Give in small incremental doses (typically 0.2 mg at a time)

Also remember general supportive care: Maintain the airway, give supplemental O₂, monitor vitals closely, support ventilation if needed (bag-valve-mask), and keep the patient monitored until fully recovered. If a reversal agent is administered, the patient needs to meet discharge criteria and the observation period may need to be extended because of the risk of re-sedation.

Question 11: Write a post-op pain prescription

The patient will have moderate-severe pain for 2 days, then moderate discomfort for 3-5 more days. He's healthy, no allergies, no contraindications.

A reasonable approach:

For the first 2 days (moderate-severe pain):

  • Ibuprofen 600mg every 6 hours PLUS Acetaminophen 500-1000mg every 6 hours (alternating or together — multimodal analgesia)
  • If truly severe: a short course of opioid (e.g., hydrocodone/acetaminophen or oxycodone/acetaminophen, small quantity like 10-12 tablets) as a rescue medication

For days 3-7 (moderate discomfort):

  • Ibuprofen 400-600mg every 6 hours as needed
  • Acetaminophen 500mg as needed for breakthrough

The combination of ibuprofen + acetaminophen is now the gold standard for post-surgical dental pain — studies show it's as effective or better than many opioid combinations for dental pain. This patient has no GI issues, so NSAIDs are safe.

CASE #2

The Patient: 52-year-old. PMH: gastric ulcers, aortic stenosis, hypertension. Needs extraction of 8 mandibular teeth (#21-28, routine extractions). Meds: Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ — a thiazide diuretic for hypertension). NKDA. Wants more than local, but does NOT want to be "knocked out" (no GA).

Question 1: What type of sedation would you recommend?

The patient explicitly says no GA. So your options are N₂O/O₂ sedation or IV moderate sedation.

Key considerations with this patient:

  • Aortic stenosis — this is significant cardiovascular disease. You need to know how severe it is. Severe aortic stenosis carries real risk under sedation because these patients are preload-dependent and can decompensate with drops in blood pressure.
  • Hypertension — controlled on HCTZ, which is good.
  • Gastric ulcers — this affects your post-op pain management choices (NSAID avoidance).

For routine extractions in a patient with cardiac history who wants to stay conscious, N₂O/O₂ with local anesthesia is a reasonable first-line option. It provides anxiolysis, has minimal cardiovascular effects, rapid onset/offset, and the patient stays awake and cooperative. You could also offer IV moderate sedation, but this carries more hemodynamic risk in a patient with aortic stenosis (midazolam and other agents can drop blood pressure).

If you do IV sedation, extreme caution with drug selection and dosing — avoid anything that significantly drops blood pressure (preload reduction is dangerous in aortic stenosis).

Bottom Line
N₂O is likely the safest, most conservative recommendation for this patient, and it matches the patient's request (more than local, not knocked out). There are no contraindications to N₂O in this patient's history.

Question 2: What is your pre-operative workup?

  • PMH review: Detailed cardiac history — when was aortic stenosis diagnosed? How severe? Any symptoms (syncope, chest pain, dyspnea on exertion)? Is the hypertension well controlled? Gastric ulcer status — active or healed?
  • Medications: HCTZ — check if patient is taking it consistently. HCTZ can cause hypokalemia (low potassium), which can predispose to cardiac arrhythmias. May want to check a recent metabolic panel/electrolytes.
  • Allergies: NKDA confirmed.
  • Vital signs: BP (is the hypertension actually controlled today?), HR, RR, SpO₂
  • Physical exam:
    • Cardiac auscultation — listen for the aortic stenosis murmur (see Q3 below)
    • Lung auscultation
    • Airway assessment (Mallampati)
  • ASA Classification: This patient is at least ASA III — has multiple systemic diseases (aortic stenosis, HTN, gastric ulcers). Aortic stenosis especially, if moderate-severe, puts him at ASA III.
  • Medical consult: Consider getting a cardiology consult to determine the severity of the aortic stenosis. An echocardiogram can evaluate valve area, gradient, and ejection fraction. This is important because the management approach changes significantly if the stenosis is severe vs. mild.
  • Infective Endocarditis Prophylaxis: Does this patient need it? NO — aortic stenosis alone (without a prosthetic valve) is NOT on the AHA list of conditions requiring IE prophylaxis. The conditions that require it are: prosthetic cardiac valve/material, previous IE, certain congenital heart diseases, and cardiac transplant with valvulopathy. Aortic stenosis by itself does NOT qualify.
  • Informed consent

Question 3: Where do you hear the murmur of aortic stenosis, and when in the cardiac cycle?

Location: The right 2nd intercostal space (right sternal border) — this is the aortic auscultation area. Remember: valves are best heard AWAY from their anatomical position, further downstream in the direction of blood flow.

Timing: Aortic stenosis is a SYSTOLIC murmur. It occurs during ventricular ejection (systole) because the blood is being forced through a narrowed (stenotic) aortic valve. You hear it between S1 and S2. It's classically described as a crescendo-decrescendo ("diamond-shaped") murmur that radiates to the carotid arteries.

Quick memory aid
Stenosis of a semilunar valve (aortic, pulmonic) = Systolic. Stenosis of an AV valve (mitral, tricuspid) = Diastolic.

Question 4: What monitors would you use?

If doing N₂O/O₂ (minimal sedation):

  • Pulse oximeter (O₂ sat + HR)
  • Blood pressure monitoring (pre-op, intra-op, post-op)
  • Visual observation of mucosa color, chest excursions, respirations
  • Given his cardiac history, ECG monitoring should be strongly considered even though it's technically only required for moderate sedation with significant cardiovascular disease

If doing IV moderate sedation:

  • All of the above PLUS
  • Precordial stethoscope or capnography
  • ECG — definitely indicated given aortic stenosis and hypertension
  • Continuous monitoring of level of consciousness
  • Time-oriented anesthetic record

Question 5: Post-operative pain prescription for Case 2

Critical Difference
This patient has GASTRIC ULCERS. NSAIDs (ibuprofen, aspirin, naproxen) should be AVOIDED — they inhibit COX-1, which reduces protective prostaglandin production in the gastric mucosa, worsening or reactivating ulcers and increasing bleeding risk.

For 4-5 days of mild to moderate discomfort:

  • Acetaminophen (Tylenol) 500-1000mg every 6 hours as needed — this is safe for the stomach because it works centrally, not peripherally on COX enzymes in the GI tract
  • If acetaminophen alone isn't enough, a short course of a mild opioid (e.g., Tylenol #3 = acetaminophen + codeine, or hydrocodone/acetaminophen) could be added, but the pain is described as only mild-moderate so this may not be needed
  • Could also consider a COX-2 selective inhibitor (like celecoxib) which has a somewhat better GI safety profile than traditional NSAIDs, but even this should be used cautiously in a patient with active or recent ulcers

Bottom line: Acetaminophen is your go-to. Avoid NSAIDs because of the gastric ulcers.

CASE #3

The Scenario: A colleague wants to start offering N₂O in their practice and is asking you questions before taking a course. This case tests your N₂O knowledge directly — everything from the N₂O lectures (C, D, E, F, G, H).

Question 1: What medical conditions is N₂O contraindicated in?

Absolute Contraindications:

  • Pneumothorax (gas expansion in closed pleural space)
  • COPD (already compromised respiratory function; N₂O displaces O₂)
  • Cystic fibrosis (air trapping + compromised lungs)
  • Bowel obstruction (N₂O expands trapped gas in obstructed bowel)
  • Recent pneumoencephalography (air in cranial vault)
  • Pernicious anemia / B12 deficiency (N₂O oxidizes the cobalt in B12, inactivates methionine synthase → worsens the deficiency, can cause neurologic damage)
  • First trimester of pregnancy (risk to fetal development during organogenesis; N₂O interferes with folate/B12 metabolism needed for DNA synthesis)
  • Bleomycin sulfate therapy (cancer drug; N₂O + bleomycin can cause pulmonary toxicity)
  • Psychological impairment (can't cooperate or understand)
  • Current psychotropic drug use
  • Current or recovering drug addiction (risk of abuse/relapse)
  • Patient in shock, semi-conscious, or with serious head/facial injuries
  • Unable to understand or unwilling to consent

Relative Contraindications:

  • Upper respiratory tract infection (nasal congestion = can't breathe through nose = can't deliver the gas properly)
  • Middle ear disturbance/surgery (N₂O can expand gas in the middle ear)
  • Recent eye surgery using perfluoropropane or sulfur hexafluoride (these gases are used in retinal surgery; N₂O can expand them and increase intraocular pressure)

Question 2: How potent is N₂O? Can it produce general anesthesia?

N₂O is NOT very potent. Its MAC (Minimum Alveolar Concentration) = 105%. MAC is the concentration of an inhaled anesthetic needed to prevent movement in 50% of patients in response to a surgical stimulus. Since you can't deliver 105% of anything in a breathable mixture (you'd need more than 100% N₂O with no oxygen), N₂O alone CANNOT produce general anesthesia.

At the concentrations used clinically in dentistry (typically 30-50% N₂O with the rest being O₂), you achieve minimal to moderate sedation — the patient stays conscious, responsive, and breathing on their own. You CANNOT "put someone to sleep" with N₂O alone. It's an excellent anxiolytic and mild analgesic, but it's a weak anesthetic.

This is actually one of its major safety advantages — because it's so weak as an anesthetic, the margin of safety is very wide. You'd run out of oxygen before you could deliver enough N₂O to achieve GA.

Question 3: Are there health hazards for staff?

Yes. Chronic occupational exposure to trace N₂O is a real concern:

  • 1967 Vaisman report: Found reproductive problems in male and female anesthesiologists in Russia exposed chronically
  • 1980 survey of dentists/dental assistants: Long-term exposure associated with increased general health and reproductive problems
  • Mechanism: N₂O oxidizes the cobalt in Vitamin B12, preventing it from acting as a coenzyme for methionine synthase. Methionine synthase is critical for DNA production and cellular reproduction. This is why chronic exposure can cause reproductive issues and neurologic problems (myeloneuropathy).
  • Neurologic effects: Chronic exposure can cause myeloneuropathy — sensory and proprioception impairment. Usually temporary with slow recovery, but can be permanent.

NIOSH (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) limits:

  • Operating room: 25 ppm
  • Dental setting: 50 ppm

Question 4: How can you mitigate these health risks?

Scavenging system — this is the single most important protective measure:

  • A scavenging nasal hood is standard on all N₂O delivery systems in the US. Practicing without one is below the standard of care.
  • It works by collecting exhaled gas from the patient through the nasal mask and evacuating it via vacuum suction to the outside atmosphere
  • Evacuation flow rate: 45 L/min
  • Must be confirmed functioning before every use

Additional measures:

  • Ensure good mask fit (minimize leaks)
  • Maintain good room ventilation
  • Use infrared (IR) spectrophotometry to detect and monitor ambient N₂O levels (can detect levels below 1 ppm)
  • Get baseline readings, then do periodic monitoring
  • Minimize the total flow of gases
  • Turn off N₂O flow before removing the mask
  • Staff who are pregnant (especially first trimester) should be aware of exposure levels and may need to avoid the operatory during N₂O use

Question 5: Are there any allergies associated with N₂O?

No. There are NO true allergies to N₂O. It is an inorganic gas (N₂O) — it doesn't trigger an immune-mediated allergic response. Some patients may experience nausea or other side effects, but these are pharmacologic effects, not allergic reactions. The latex in the nasal hood could cause a latex allergy reaction, but that's an allergy to the equipment, not to the gas itself.

Question 6: Is it addictive?

N₂O does have abuse potential. Key points from the abuse lectures:

  • N₂O abusers tend to be older than typical inhalant abusers (solvent sniffers tend to be younger)
  • Healthcare personnel who have ready access are noted recreational users
  • It's easily obtainable: stolen cylinders, auto racing supplies (mixed with sulfur dioxide — noxious), whipped cream propellant ("whippets"), aerosol cans of whipping cream
  • This is why current or recovering drug addiction is an absolute contraindication — you don't want to introduce a potentially pleasurable inhalant to someone with addiction vulnerability
  • The abuse risk is real enough that it needs to be managed: secure cylinders, track inventory, monitor staff

Question 7: What monitors will you need?

For N₂O/O₂ minimal sedation, the monitoring requirements are:

  • Someone must remain in the operatory at all times (dentist or trained individual)
  • Oxygenation: Continuously evaluate color of mucosa/skin/blood + pulse oximetry (O₂ saturation)
  • Ventilation: Continuously observe chest excursions, verify respirations
  • Circulation: Blood pressure and heart rate — pre-operatively, intra-operatively, and post-operatively
  • Documentation: A record must be maintained

You also need to confirm your scavenging system is functioning, have adequate oxygen supply, and have equipment to deliver O₂ under positive pressure (in case of emergency).

THE SEDATION CONTINUUM (Tested in Cases 1 & 2)

This is foundational to understanding WHY you'd pick one sedation method over another. The professor spent significant time on this:

Minimal Sedation → Patient has a minimally depressed level of consciousness. They can independently and continuously maintain an airway, respond normally to verbal commands and tactile stimulation. Ventilatory and cardiovascular functions are unaffected. This is where N₂O lives.

Moderate Sedation (Conscious Sedation) → Drug-induced depression of consciousness where patients respond purposefully to verbal commands (alone or with light tactile stimulation). No interventions required to maintain airway. Spontaneous ventilation is adequate. Cardiovascular function usually maintained. This is where IV midazolam + fentanyl lives.

Deep Sedation → Patients cannot be easily aroused but respond purposefully to repeated or painful stimulation. Ability to maintain ventilatory function may be impaired. May need help maintaining airway. Spontaneous ventilation may be inadequate. Cardiovascular function usually maintained.

General Anesthesia → Drug-induced loss of consciousness. Patients are not arousable, even by painful stimulation. Often require assistance maintaining airway. Positive pressure ventilation may be required. Cardiovascular function may be impaired.

The critical principle
Anesthesia is a CONTINUUM — you can unintentionally slide deeper than intended. You must be trained to rescue a patient to one level deeper than you intend to sedate them. If you're targeting moderate sedation, you must be able to manage deep sedation.

DIFFUSION HYPOXIA (Relevant to Cases 1 & 3)

This comes up because it connects to WHY you give 100% O₂ at the end of N₂O and WHY it's a safety consideration:

  • What it is: When you stop N₂O, the large volume of N₂O that has accumulated in the blood rapidly diffuses back into the alveoli. This flood of N₂O dilutes the oxygen in the alveoli, temporarily dropping the patient's oxygen levels.
  • When it occurs: At termination of N₂O sedation
  • Symptoms: "Hangover" effect — headache, nausea, dizziness, lethargy
  • Prevention: Administer 100% O₂ for 3–5 minutes after discontinuing N₂O
  • The rule: N₂O procedures begin AND end with 100% oxygen

LARYNGOSPASM (Could be tested as a complication scenario)

Since these cases involve sedation, laryngospasm is a potential complication the exam could ask about:

  • What it is: A protective reflex — the vocal cords slam shut to prevent foreign material from entering the airway
  • When it happens: Extraordinarily uncommon during minimal and moderate sedation, more associated with deeper levels
  • Partial laryngospasm: High-pitched crowing sound (stridor), increased difficulty ventilating
  • Complete laryngospasm: Ominous absence of sound despite exaggerated respiratory efforts, soft tissue retraction in supraclavicular and intercostal regions — this is an emergency
Management sequence
  1. Position patient supine
  2. Give 100% O₂, stop N₂O
  3. Displace tongue with forceps
  4. Evaluate and suction the airway
  5. Positive-pressure oxygen to physically break the spasm by forcing O₂ through the vocal cords
  6. If refractory: Succinylcholine 10mg (depolarizing muscle relaxant) — but caution: causes apnea, so you must be prepared to ventilate
  7. Assess and control ventilation

AIR EMBOLISM (Referenced in IV Complications — could be MCQ)

  • Extremely unlikely during IV sedation but testable
  • Prevention: Remove air from syringes and IV tubing before the procedure; make sure IV bag doesn't empty
  • Patient can tolerate up to 1 mL/kg body weight of air in peripheral venous circulation without adverse effect
  • Small children at greater risk
  • Management: Position patient on LEFT SIDE in HEAD-DOWN position (Durant's maneuver — traps air in the right atrium away from the pulmonary outflow)

HCTZ AND HYPOKALEMIA (Case 2 Hidden Gem)

Hydrochlorothiazide is a thiazide diuretic used for hypertension. The case mentions it specifically because it has a clinically important side effect: hypokalemia (low potassium). Why does this matter?

  • Low potassium predisposes to cardiac arrhythmias
  • In a patient who already has aortic stenosis, arrhythmias can be particularly dangerous because the heart is already working harder to push blood through a narrowed valve
  • This is why the pre-operative workup for Case 2 should include checking electrolytes/metabolic panel
  • If potassium is low, it should be corrected before elective sedation

INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS PROPHYLAXIS — WHAT QUALIFIES (Case 2 Trap)

The exam loves to test what does and does NOT require IE prophylaxis. The current AHA guidelines say prophylaxis is only for:

Conditions requiring prophylaxis:

  • Prosthetic cardiac valve or material (including transcatheter valves, valve repair with devices/rings/clips)
  • Previous, relapse, or recurrent IE
  • Congenital heart disease (specific types — unrepaired cyanotic, repaired with residual defects, repaired with prosthetic material within 6 months)
  • Cardiac transplant recipients who develop valvulopathy
  • Left ventricular assist devices or implantable hearts

Conditions that do NOT require prophylaxis (common exam traps):

  • Aortic stenosis (without prosthetic valve) ← Case 2 patient
  • Mitral valve prolapse
  • Hypertension
  • Previous CABG
  • Rheumatic heart disease (unless prosthetic valve)

The regimen (when needed):

  • Amoxicillin 2g orally 30-60 min before procedure (adult dose)
  • Pediatric dose: 50 mg/kg
  • If allergic to penicillin: clindamycin, azithromycin, or clarithromycin

VENIPUNCTURE SITE ORDER OF PREFERENCE (Case 1, Q6 — Extremely Testable)

  1. Dorsum of hand — first choice, easy access, veins visible
  2. Ventral forearm — good backup
  3. Lateral antecubital fossa — outer elbow, relatively safe
  4. Dorsal wrist — acceptable but more painful
  5. Medial antecubital fossa — LAST resort, proximity to brachial artery and median nerve

PUTTING IT ALL TOGETHER — THE DECISION TREE

If you see a case on the exam, run this mental algorithm:

The Case Algorithm
  1. Step 1: Read the PMH. What conditions does this patient have? Do any of them contraindicate N₂O? Do any require special cardiac/respiratory precautions?
  2. Step 2: ASA classify the patient. This tells you risk level and what type of sedation is appropriate.
  3. Step 3: What does the patient want? More than local? Not "knocked out"? This narrows your sedation options.
  4. Step 4: Pick your sedation. Match the patient's medical status, desires, and the procedure's complexity to the appropriate sedation level.
  5. Step 5: Pre-op workup. PMH, meds (watch for drug interactions and side effects like HCTZ → hypokalemia), allergies, PE (vitals + relevant systems), airway (Mallampati), ASA class, informed consent, fasting guidelines, medical consults if needed.
  6. Step 6: Monitors. Match monitoring to sedation level — minimal vs. moderate have different requirements.
  7. Step 7: Know your drugs and their reversals. Midazolam → flumazenil. Fentanyl → naloxone. Know the doses.
  8. Step 8: Pain management. Consider the patient's PMH — gastric ulcers = no NSAIDs. Normal patient = ibuprofen + acetaminophen multimodal approach. Opioids only as rescue for severe pain, limited quantity.
  9. Step 9: Know your complications. Non-running IV causes, blood backing up, infiltration, venospasm, hematoma, air embolism, laryngospasm, diffusion hypoxia.
